# Anxiety, OCD & Trauma on the Inpatient Unit

> **Review status:** AI-drafted, evidence-anchored - **pending Dr. Moss's review/attestation before learner use.**

**In one line** - On the unit, anxiety, OCD, and PTSD are usually layered onto a primary admission diagnosis; your job is to separate the medical and substance mimics from the psychiatric disorder, treat the disorder with antidepressants plus structured behavioral work, and resist the reflex to standing benzodiazepines.

**How it presents on the unit** - Pure anxiety, OCD, or PTSD rarely earns an inpatient bed on its own; you will far more often meet them riding alongside a mood episode, psychosis, or substance use disorder. Distinguish anxiety *the symptom* - a near-universal reaction to admission, withdrawal, akathisia, or an undiagnosed medical problem - from anxiety *the disorder*, which is pervasive, predates the crisis, and carries its own functional impairment. PTSD may surface as hyperarousal, nightmares, or a "behavioral" reaction to restraint, seclusion, or a same-gender vs. opposite-gender examiner. OCD can masquerade as psychosis when rituals are elaborate, or as treatment refusal when contamination fears collide with the milieu.

**Differential & can't-miss mimics** - Anxiety is a final common pathway, so screen the body first: cardiopulmonary causes (arrhythmia, PE), thyroid disease (hyperthyroidism), hypoglycemia, caffeine and stimulant intoxication, and alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal. On a psychiatric unit, **akathisia** is the classic miss - restless, driven motor anxiety from antipsychotics that worsens if you reflexively treat it as "agitation" with more dopamine blockade. Sudden-onset panic, chest pain, dyspnea, or autonomic instability deserves a medical workup, not reassurance.

**Initial workup** - Keep it focused and hypothesis-driven: vitals with orthostatics, a careful medication and substance history (including caffeine and recent benzodiazepine changes), and a withdrawal assessment when indicated. Order TSH, glucose/fingerstick, and a basic metabolic panel; add ECG and troponin for cardiac complaints and the PERC/Wells pathway for suspected PE. A toxicology screen helps when stimulants are plausible. Examine for tremor, the inner-restlessness of akathisia, and goiter.

**Acute inpatient management** - Combine medication, skills, and milieu. **SSRIs and SNRIs are first-line** across anxiety disorders, OCD, and PTSD; counsel patients on the delayed onset and the transient early activation. **Avoid standing benzodiazepines on the unit** - they drive dependence, falls, and delirium, and are especially hazardous with comorbid SUD or in older adults; reserve them for specific, time-limited indications (e.g., a structured alcohol or sedative withdrawal protocol). Pair pharmacology with disorder-specific behavioral work: **exposure and response prevention (ERP)** for OCD, **prolonged exposure (PE)** and **cognitive processing therapy (CPT)** for PTSD, and **interoceptive exposure** for panic, introduced in graded form even during a short stay. Wrap all of it in **trauma-informed care** - predictability, explained procedures, offered choices, and minimized restraint/seclusion - which both reduces retraumatization and lowers behavioral escalation.

**What the student does**
- Screen new admissions for an anxiety, OCD, or PTSD history and document whether symptoms predate the acute crisis.
- At the bedside, evaluate restlessness deliberately: ask about an inner urge to move and inspect for akathisia before labeling a patient "anxious."
- Review the medication list and home regimen for benzodiazepine use, abrupt discontinuation, or high caffeine intake, and flag taper or withdrawal risk to the team.
- Coach one concrete skill - paced diaphragmatic breathing or grounding - and note the patient's response.
- Track target symptoms (panic frequency, ritual time, nightmares) on serial exams to gauge medication response.

**Disposition & discharge essentials** - Confirm the SSRI/SNRI is at a tolerated dose with a clear titration plan, and verify outpatient follow-up that can deliver ERP, PE, or CPT - the medication rarely finishes the job alone. Reconcile medications so no patient leaves on a new standing benzodiazepine without an explicit, time-limited rationale and taper. Give the patient and family a relapse-warning plan and crisis contacts, and communicate trauma-informed considerations to the next setting.

**High-yield pearls**
- Anxiety is a symptom before it is a diagnosis - clear the medical and withdrawal mimics first.
- Akathisia is the great inpatient impostor; treat the cause, not with more dopamine blockade.
- SSRIs/SNRIs are first-line; benzodiazepines are a liability on the unit, not a maintenance plan.
- Definitive treatment is behavioral - ERP for OCD, PE/CPT for PTSD, interoceptive exposure for panic.
- The scale to know for OCD is the **Y-BOCS** (Yale-Brown Obsessive-Compulsive Scale) - the gold-standard clinician-rated measure of OCD severity and treatment response (a symptom checklist plus 10 severity items, obsessions and compulsions scored separately).
- Trauma-informed care is clinical, not optional: predictability and choice prevent escalation.

**Pair with** - the Mental Status Exam tool (Language & Interview tab) for eliciting anxiety and trauma history without retraumatizing; the **Reflection & PIF** set (the Reflection & Identity tool) to process your own response to distressing presentations; and the **Differential Diagnosis** scaffolds for working the anxiety-mimic list systematically.

# Geriatric Psychiatry on the Inpatient Unit

> **Review status:** AI-drafted, evidence-anchored - **pending Dr. Moss's review/attestation before learner use.**

**In one line** - On the geriatric inpatient unit, your central job is to separate reversible from irreversible causes of cognitive and behavioral change, treat the treatable, and avoid iatrogenic harm from the very drugs meant to help.

**How it presents on the unit** - Older adults rarely arrive with a tidy chief complaint. Watch for late-life depression (often somatic, apathetic, or framed as "I just want to go home"), new or worsening psychosis (which in this age group is a red flag for an organic cause until proven otherwise), agitation in the setting of dementia, and the overlapping delirium-dementia-depression triad. Functional decline, falls, poor intake, and "failure to thrive" are common admitting framings that hide a psychiatric or medical driver.

**Differential & can't-miss mimics** - The skill that defines competent geriatric psychiatry is distinguishing delirium vs dementia vs depression. Delirium is acute, fluctuating, and marked by inattention; it is a medical emergency and the can't-miss diagnosis on every confused elder. Dementia is chronic and progressive with preserved alertness early. Depression can masquerade as cognitive impairment - the pseudodementia presentation (Caine 1981), where patients say "I don't know" rather than confabulate, show effortful disengagement, and improve cognitively when mood is treated. Do not forget vascular depression (Alexopoulos 1997): late-onset depression with executive dysfunction and white-matter burden, often less responsive to monotherapy. These three states coexist; finding one does not exclude the others.

**Initial workup** - Keep it focused and bedside-anchored. Formally test attention (digit span, months-of-the-year backward, serial subtraction) - inattention reorients you toward delirium. Perform a structured cognitive screen and document a baseline. Review every medication for deliriogenic and anticholinergic load. Screen for infection (UTI, pneumonia), metabolic derangement, hypoxia, urinary retention, constipation, and pain. Collateral history is not optional; establish the true baseline and the tempo of change.

**Acute inpatient management** - Apply the Beers Criteria and assess cumulative anticholinergic burden, then actively deprescribe deliriogenic and high-risk agents. Dose with start-low-go-slow titration. Treat fall risk and orthostasis as primary safety issues, not afterthoughts - many psychotropics worsen both. Respect the antipsychotic boxed warning for dementia-related behavioral disturbance: non-pharmacologic strategies come first, and antipsychotics are used only when necessary, at the lowest effective dose, time-limited, with documented risk-benefit discussion. Capacity assessment is frequently in play (treatment refusal, disposition disputes); assess it for the specific decision at hand. For severe, treatment-resistant, psychotic, or catatonic late-life depression, ECT is effective and often well-tolerated in older adults - keep it on the table early rather than as a last resort.

**What the student does** -
- Test attention at the bedside daily and document fluctuation.
- Build a complete medication reconciliation and flag every Beers/anticholinergic agent for the team.
- Call family or the facility for collateral on baseline cognition and function.
- Screen for and report constipation, retention, pain, and infection signs.
- Sit with the patient unhurried - orientation, sensory aids (glasses, hearing aids), and presence are real interventions.

**Disposition & discharge essentials** - Confirm cognitive and functional baseline before discharge; clarify whether the patient can return to prior setting or needs a higher level of care. Reconcile medications again, document what was deprescribed and why, and ensure a clear taper plan for any time-limited antipsychotic. Confirm follow-up, caregiver education, and safety supports (fall precautions, supervision of meds).

**High-yield pearls**
- New psychosis in an older adult is delirium or a medical cause until proven otherwise.
- Inattention is the fingerprint of delirium - test it, don't infer it.
- "I don't know" answers and effortful disengagement suggest pseudodementia; treat the depression.
- The drug list is often the diagnosis - deprescribing is active treatment.
- Non-pharmacologic management is first-line for dementia-related agitation; antipsychotics are time-limited exceptions.

**Pair with** - the [Delirium guidance](?page=delirium.md), the <a href="tools/capacity.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Decisional Capacity tool</a>, and the [Differential Diagnosis scaffolds](?page=ddx.md).

# Mood Disorders on the Inpatient Unit

> **Review status:** AI-drafted, evidence-anchored - **pending Dr. Moss's review/attestation before learner use.**

**In one line** - Inpatient mood work is about safety, getting the diagnosis right (especially separating unipolar from bipolar), and starting an effective, measured treatment while the milieu and sleep do half the work.

**How it presents on the unit** - Patients rarely arrive with a tidy outpatient picture. Severe unipolar depression shows up as psychomotor retardation, poor oral intake, hopelessness, and sometimes mood-congruent psychosis (nihilistic or guilt-laden delusions) - a presentation that can shade into catatonia. Mania presents as decreased need for sleep, pressured speech, grandiosity, impulsivity, and irritability that destabilizes the milieu; floridly manic or psychotic patients often lack insight and arrive on a hold. Mixed features (depressed mood with concurrent activation/agitation) are easy to miss and carry elevated suicide risk. Across all of these, acute suicidality is the modal reason for admission - assess it actively and serially, not once at intake.

**Differential & can't-miss mimics** - Rule out medical mimics before anchoring on a primary mood disorder: thyroid dysfunction (hypo- and hyperthyroid states), corticosteroids and other medications, intoxication or withdrawal (alcohol, stimulants, sedatives), and CNS processes (delirium, stroke, frontal/limbic lesions, neurocognitive disorders). Delirium masquerading as depression or mania is the classic inpatient trap. Critically, always screen for a past manic or hypomanic episode before calling a depression "unipolar" - a bipolar depression misread as unipolar can be pushed into mania or rapid cycling by an antidepressant.

**Initial workup** - Keep it focused and decision-relevant: a full MSE with explicit suicide assessment, collateral history (the single highest-yield diagnostic move for distinguishing bipolar from unipolar), medication reconciliation, TSH, CBC, CMP, and urine drug screen. Add a pregnancy test in patients who can become pregnant before starting teratogenic agents, and a head CT/MRI when the picture is atypical, late-onset, or has focal findings.

**Acute inpatient management** - Treatment is medications plus milieu plus safety, layered deliberately. Before starting an antidepressant for depression, screen for bipolarity (e.g., Mood Disorder Questionnaire plus collateral) so you do not destabilize an unrecognized bipolar patient. For unipolar depression, practice measurement-based, sequential treatment in the spirit of STAR*D: pick an agent, dose it adequately, track response with a scale, and switch or augment by protocol rather than by impression. For suicidality and for bipolar illness, lithium deserves specific emphasis - it carries a distinct anti-suicidal effect (Cipriani 2013) and is a first-line maintenance mood stabilizer with the strongest long-term evidence (BALANCE trial). Reserve ECT for severe, psychotic, catatonic, or treatment-resistant depression, for life-threatening states (refusal of food/fluids, acute high suicide risk), and as a preferred option in pregnancy. The milieu is therapeutic, not incidental: protect sleep and circadian rhythm actively, since sleep loss both worsens depression and precipitates mania (Harvey; IPSRT/Frank). For acute mania, reduce stimulation, restore sleep, and start or optimize a mood stabilizer or antipsychotic. Throughout, maintain a safe environment - contraband removal, appropriate observation level, and a collaboratively developed safety plan.

**What the student does** -
- Perform and document a focused MSE every day, with an explicit, serial suicide assessment.
- Obtain collateral (family, prior records, outpatient clinician) and specifically ask about past mania/hypomania.
- Track symptoms with a standardized measure so the team can see trajectory, not just snapshots.
- Reconcile medications and verify labs (TSH, UDS, pregnancy test) are back and reviewed.
- Round on the milieu: check sleep, oral intake, and whether the safety plan is current.

**Disposition & discharge essentials** - Discharge readiness is functional and safety-based, not calendar-based: improving mood and resolving acute risk, adequate sleep and intake, an established and tolerated medication, and a concrete follow-up. Send the patient out with a timely outpatient appointment (ideally within a week), a clear medication plan with monitoring (lithium levels, renal/thyroid follow-up where relevant), a written safety plan with crisis contacts, means-restriction counseling, and engaged family or supports when consent allows.

**High-yield pearls** -
- Screen every depressed patient for past mania before writing for an antidepressant.
- New "depression" or "mania" in an older or medically complex patient is delirium until proven otherwise.
- Lithium is the mood stabilizer with anti-suicidal and best maintenance evidence - use it, and monitor it.
- Sleep is treatment: protect it to pull patients out of depression and to prevent mania.
- ECT is not a last resort - it is first-line for psychotic, catatonic, or life-threatening mood states and in pregnancy.

**Pair with** - the <a href="tools/mse.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Mental Status Exam tool</a> for documenting mood, affect, and psychotic features; the [Differential Diagnosis scaffolds](?page=ddx.md) for the medical-mimic differential; and the [suicide-risk & safety pocket card](?page=pg_suicide.md) for structured risk assessment and safety planning.

# Neurodevelopmental Disorders on the Inpatient Unit

> **Review status:** AI-drafted, evidence-anchored - **pending Dr. Moss's review/attestation before learner use.**

**In one line** - On the unit, neurodevelopmental disorders - ADHD, autism spectrum disorder (ASD), and intellectual disability (ID) - are usually the *context* a patient arrives with, not the reason for admission; your job is to adapt how you communicate, treat the comorbidity that actually brought them in, and never write new symptoms off as "just their baseline."

**How it presents on the unit** - You will rarely admit someone *for* ADHD or autism. Instead these conditions sit underneath the admitting problem: an autistic adult admitted for self-injury or "behavioral dyscontrol," an adult with ID admitted after aggression at a group home, an adolescent with ADHD and a first mood episode, or a previously undiagnosed patient whose lifelong social-communication differences become visible under stress. The core inpatient skills are recognizing the developmental baseline, adapting the interview, and distinguishing a true change from chronic traits.

**Differential & can't-miss mimics** - The cardinal error is **diagnostic overshadowing**: attributing new or escalating symptoms to the developmental disability and under-investigating a treatable cause. A few specific traps:

- **ASD vs psychosis** - restricted interests, literal/concrete language, and social-communication difficulty are *not* delusions or thought disorder. New psychosis can co-occur, but don't relabel lifelong autistic features as psychotic.
- **ADHD vs bipolar mania** - ADHD is chronic, pervasive, and cross-situational from childhood; mania is *episodic* with mood elevation, decreased need for sleep, and grandiosity. Episodic "ADHD-like" symptoms are a mood or substance flag.
- **ID vs acquired cognitive change** - intellectual disability is a static developmental baseline; an *acute* change in cognition or behavior is a delirium/medical workup, not the ID.
- **Catatonia in autism** - under-recognized and important; new mutism, immobility, posturing, or stupor in an autistic patient should prompt a catatonia evaluation.

**Initial workup** - Establish the developmental and adaptive-functioning baseline from caregivers, records, and (for adolescents) school/IEP documentation before you interpret any behavior. Clarify communication preferences and sensory triggers. For any *change* in behavior, screen the body first: pain, dental problems, constipation/urinary retention, infection, GERD, sleep loss, and medication effects (including akathisia). Then screen for treatable psychiatric comorbidity - mood, anxiety, OCD, psychosis, catatonia, and substance use - which is usually the real reason for admission.

**Acute inpatient management** - Lead with the environment and communication, not medication. Provide predictability (routine, visual schedules, consistent staff), reduce sensory load, allow extra processing time, use concrete language, and support augmentative/alternative communication where needed. Treat **behavior as communication** - run the functional and medical assessment before reaching for a PRN. Treat the diagnosed comorbidity on its own merits. Use psychotropics judiciously: risperidone and aripiprazole are FDA-approved for *irritability/aggression associated with autism* (they do **not** treat the core social-communication features), so target symptoms explicitly, monitor metabolically, and avoid reflexive standing antipsychotics. For ADHD, stimulants are first-line and effective, but do not start them during acute mania or psychosis, screen cardiovascular history and substance-misuse/diversion risk, and consider non-stimulants (atomoxetine, viloxazine, guanfacine/clonidine ER) when those are concerns. Capacity is decision-specific; use supported decision-making, involve guardians where they exist, and stay least-restrictive.

**What the student does** -
- Get the developmental and baseline-functioning history from caregivers/records *before* interpreting behavior.
- Ask about communication and sensory preferences, and adapt the interview (concrete language, extra time, AAC, a quieter space).
- For any behavior change, screen for pain, constipation, infection, dental issues, and medication effects first.
- Keep catatonia on the differential in autistic patients (mutism, posturing, immobility).
- Distinguish chronic, cross-situational ADHD from episodic mood symptoms - and flag the stimulant cautions to the team.

**Disposition & discharge essentials** - Coordinate early with caregivers, group homes, case managers, and developmental-disability services; transitions are where these admissions succeed or fail. Confirm the patient is back to developmental baseline, reconcile medications with clear indications (especially any antipsychotic started for irritability - document target, dose, and review plan), and ensure follow-up that can support the patient's communication and functioning. For adolescents, loop in school supports and the outpatient prescriber.

**High-yield pearls**
- Diagnostic overshadowing is the cardinal error - new symptoms in ASD/ID earn a workup, not a shrug.
- Behavior is communication; look for pain and medical drivers before sedation.
- Antipsychotics (risperidone, aripiprazole) treat autism-*associated* irritability, not the core social-communication features.
- Catatonia is under-recognized in autistic patients - look for it.
- ADHD is chronic and cross-situational; episodic presentations flag a mood or substance cause, and stimulants aren't started during acute mania/psychosis.
- Know the ADHD rating scales: the **ASRS-v1.1** is the adult self-report screen; the **NICHQ Vanderbilt** (parent and teacher forms) is its pediatric counterpart - both support, but never replace, the clinical diagnosis.
- Capacity is decision-specific - use supported decision-making and the least restrictive option.

**Pair with** - the [Differential Diagnosis scaffolds](?page=ddx.md), the [Psychopharmacology primer](?page=psychopharm_primer.md), the <a href="tools/capacity.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Decisional Capacity tool</a>, and the [Agitation guidance](?page=agitation.md).

# Nutrition & Metabolic Health in Psychiatry

> **Review status:** AI-drafted, evidence-anchored - **pending Dr. Moss's review/attestation before learner use.**

**For:** MS3 on the unit. Two clerkship objectives live here - *nutrition in individual psychiatric care* and *nutrition in population mental health (metabolic syndrome prevention)*. Educational; not individualized medical advice.

## Why this is a psychiatry problem, not a side issue
People with serious mental illness die 10-20 years earlier than the general population, mostly from cardiometabolic disease. Several psychotropics drive weight gain, dyslipidemia, and glucose dysregulation; diet and activity are both a *cause* of that risk and part of the *treatment*. Owning metabolic health is part of owning the psychiatric care.

## Metabolic monitoring you should know cold
For anyone on an antipsychotic (and many other psychotropics), track at **baseline, ~12 weeks, then annually** (more often if high-risk):

| Measure | Why |
|---|---|
| Weight / BMI (and waist circumference) | Earliest, most actionable signal |
| Blood pressure | Metabolic syndrome criterion |
| Fasting glucose or HbA1c | Antipsychotic-associated diabetes |
| Fasting lipid panel | Dyslipidemia, esp. triglycerides |
| Personal/family cardiometabolic history | Risk stratification |

This is the **ADA/APA consensus monitoring** standard - see the metabolic-monitoring landmark on the Landmark Trials page.

## Drug-specific burden (highest -> lowest, roughly)
- **Highest:** olanzapine, clozapine.
- **Intermediate:** quetiapine, risperidone, paliperidone.
- **Lower:** aripiprazole, ziprasidone, lurasidone, cariprazine.
- Mitigation options: choose a lower-burden agent when feasible; olanzapine/samidorphan; metformin as an evidence-based add-on for antipsychotic weight gain; early lifestyle intervention. *Confirm doses/choices against current guidance and your team.*

## Individual counseling - what actually has evidence
- **Dietary pattern beats single nutrients.** A Mediterranean-style pattern (vegetables, fruit, legumes, whole grains, fish, olive oil; less ultra-processed food) improved depressive symptoms in a randomized trial (the **SMILES** trial, Jacka 2017) and is the best-supported "food as treatment" signal.
- **Limit ultra-processed foods and sugar-sweetened beverages** - tied to worse mood and metabolic outcomes.
- **Protein + fiber, steady glycemic load** help satiety and energy; relevant to fatigue and binge patterns.
- **Omega-3 (EPA-predominant)** has modest adjunctive evidence in depression - reasonable to mention, not a substitute for first-line care.
- **Substance-style counseling fits here** - pair with Motivational Interviewing for sustainable change.

## Medication-specific food points to teach
- **Lithium:** stable sodium and **hydration** matter - dehydration or big salt swings change levels.
- **MAOIs:** tyramine restriction (aged cheeses, cured meats, tap beer) to avoid hypertensive crisis.
- **Grapefruit** inhibits CYP3A4 - relevant for several psychotropics.
- **Caffeine/alcohol:** sleep disruption, anxiety, interactions; worth a specific ask.

## Population / systems lens
- **Metabolic syndrome prevention** in people on psychotropics is a population-health responsibility, not just a clinic task - screening programs, shared-care with primary care, and access to dietitians.
- **Food insecurity** is common in serious mental illness and worsens outcomes - screen and connect to resources (a systems-based-practice objective).

## On the unit
Protect regular meals; treat the dietitian as a team member; flag the patient who isn't eating or who is over-eating in response to medication. A one-line plan ("started olanzapine - baseline metabolic labs sent, dietitian consult, weight tracking") shows you're thinking metabolically.

**Pair with** - the [Psychopharmacology primer](?page=psychopharm_primer.md), the metabolic-monitoring and antipsychotic-metabolic landmarks on the [Landmark Trials](?page=landmark_trials.md) page, and the [Motivational Interviewing guide](?page=motivational_interviewing.md).

# Osteopathic (OMM/OPP) Resources for the Psychiatry Clerkship

> **Note from Dr. Moss:** I'm an MD, so this page does **not** teach OMM technique. It points you to trusted osteopathic resources and - most importantly - to **your OMM/OPP faculty and OMM lab**, who own the hands-on instruction and check-offs for the osteopathic objectives in your syllabus.

**Why this page exists.** Your UNE COM syllabus lists Osteopathic Principles & Practice as a graded competency and names OMM objectives in psychiatry: a basic **structural exam** for somatic dysfunction tied to anxiety/insomnia/pain/autonomic dysregulation, and **gentle techniques** (e.g., suboccipital release, rib raising, diaphragmatic/thoracic release) to support anxiety, somatic tension, and sympathetic overactivity. Use the resources below - and your osteopathic faculty - to meet them.

## Start here (your primary sources)
- **Your course OMM/OPP faculty and OMM lab materials.** They hold the authoritative technique instruction, indications/contraindications, and your check-offs. This site can't substitute for that.

## Trusted reference resources
- **AACOM - [OMM Explained](https://www.aacom.org/become-a-doctor/about-osteopathic-medicine/omm-explained):** a clear overview of osteopathic manipulative medicine and its principles.
- **AACOM - [Competency & Curriculum Resources](https://www.aacom.org/medical-education/teaching-and-learning/competency-curriculum-resources):** materials from AACOM's Educational Council on Osteopathic Principles (ECOP) that frame OMM competencies.
- **American Osteopathic Association ([osteopathic.org](https://osteopathic.org/)):** the profession's body; standards and patient/clinician resources.
- **Foundations of Osteopathic Medicine, 4th ed.** (Seffinger MA, ed.; Wolters Kluwer; AOA-endorsed) - the standard comprehensive text, including osteopathic considerations in the behavioral sciences and specialties. Often available through your library ([LWW Health Library](https://osteopathicmedicine.lwwhealthlibrary.com/book.aspx?bookid=2582)).

## The evidence base (read it critically)
The mechanistic rationale is that OMM may shift **autonomic balance** (toward parasympathetic tone), which is why it's proposed for anxiety, sleep, and stress-related somatic symptoms. **Be honest about the evidence:** it is **emerging and limited** - mostly small, heterogeneous, or pilot studies, with few rigorous double-blind trials. Present it as adjunctive, not established first-line.

- [OMT as a complementary approach to mitigate stress, anxiety, and depression - a systematic review](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1550830725001818) (2025).
- [OMT and autonomic function through the lens of heart-rate variability](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7575744/) (Frontiers, 2020).
- [Manual osteopathic techniques and the autonomic nervous system - a systematic review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/medicine/articles/10.3389/fmed.2024.1358529/full) (Frontiers, 2024).

## How to use this on the rotation
Frame OMM as part of the **biopsychosocial / osteopathic structure-function** picture: when you note anxiety, insomnia, chronic pain, or autonomic symptoms, consider a structural contribution and discuss with your osteopathic faculty whether a gentle technique is appropriate. Document your osteopathic reasoning in the assessment - that's what the OPP competency is looking for.

# Medication During Pregnancy and Breastfeeding - What You Need to Know
A Guide for Families

*From the ReConnect Psychiatry Program*

---

## The Question You're Really Asking

"Is it safe to take medication while I'm pregnant or breastfeeding?"

This is one of the most common and most important questions families ask. The honest answer: it is not a yes-or-no question. It is a weighing question - and your treatment team weighs it *with* you, not *for* you.

Here is what we mean. Untreated depression in pregnancy and after birth carries real risks. Medication carries real risks too. The job is not to find a risk-free option - there isn't one. The job is to find the option where the benefits most clearly outweigh the risks for *you and your baby*.

This guide explains how that decision gets made, what the research says, and what to watch for.

---

## What Untreated Depression Does

This part often gets left out. When people talk about "risks of medication in pregnancy," they can forget the risks of *not* treating.

Untreated perinatal depression is linked to:

**During pregnancy:**
- Preterm birth (baby arriving early)
- Low birth weight
- Trouble bonding with the baby after birth
- Higher risk of postpartum depression becoming severe
- Higher risk of using alcohol or drugs to cope
- In severe cases, missed appointments and poor nutrition - depression makes everything harder

**After birth:**
- Disrupted parent-infant bonding during a key window of development
- Infant stress - babies sense and react to a parent's emotional state
- Strain on the whole family
- Risk of postpartum psychosis (rare, but more likely when depression goes untreated)
- A longer, harder recovery the longer treatment waits

**This does not mean you must take medication.** It means "doing nothing" is not the same as "doing nothing risky." Your treatment team weighs both sides.

---

## How Your Treatment Team Chooses a Medication

Medication choices in pregnancy and breastfeeding follow a clear process. Here is what your prescriber is thinking about:

### Step 1: Do You Need Medication at All?

For mild depression, therapy alone may be enough - especially CBT or interpersonal therapy. For moderate to severe depression, medication is usually advised alongside therapy. That is especially true if sleep is badly disrupted, you cannot care for yourself or the baby, or you have needed medication for depression before. One does not replace the other.

### Step 2: Have You Taken Something Before That Worked?

If a medicine worked well for you before, your prescriber will usually choose it again - even in pregnancy. The reason: switching to a "safer" medicine that does not work for you means you get the new medicine's risks *plus* untreated depression. A medicine that works usually beats a safer-on-paper one that does not.

### Step 3: What Does the Safety Data Say?

Not all medicines have the same amount of research. Your prescriber checks databases like LactMed (for breastfeeding) and pregnancy registries. Here is what the research currently shows for the most-used options:

---

## Common Medications and What We Know

### SSRIs (Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors)

These are the most commonly prescribed antidepressants during pregnancy and breastfeeding.

**Sertraline (Zoloft)**
- The most-studied antidepressant in pregnancy and breastfeeding
- Very low levels pass into breast milk - among the lowest of any antidepressant
- Considered first-line for perinatal depression by most treatment guidelines
- Common side effects: nausea (usually temporary), sexual side effects, mild sleep changes
- Takes 2-4 weeks to reach full effect

**Escitalopram (Lexapro)**
- Good safety data in pregnancy
- Reasonable breastfeeding data - moderate but generally well-tolerated levels in milk
- Often chosen when sertraline does not work or causes side effects
- Common side effects: similar to sertraline

**Fluoxetine (Prozac)**
- Long track record - one of the most-studied antidepressants overall
- Higher levels in breast milk than sertraline (it stays in the body longer)
- Some prescribers avoid it as a first choice during breastfeeding for that reason, but it remains an option
- Can be a good choice in pregnancy if it worked for you before

**Paroxetine (Paxil)**
- Good breastfeeding safety data (low transfer to milk)
- **Note for pregnancy:** Some older studies raised concern about a small increase in heart defects with first-trimester use. Newer studies are less clear. Most experts consider the absolute risk very small, but your prescriber may prefer a different SSRI *during pregnancy* and consider paroxetine *for breastfeeding*
- Must not be stopped abruptly - requires a gradual taper

### SNRIs (Serotonin-Norepinephrine Reuptake Inhibitors)

**Venlafaxine (Effexor) / Desvenlafaxine (Pristiq)**
- Less data than SSRIs, but no clear pattern of major birth defects
- May cause brief withdrawal-like symptoms in the newborn (jitteriness, irritability) that resolve within days
- A reasonable option when SSRIs have not worked
- Must not be stopped abruptly

### Other Medications

**Bupropion (Wellbutrin)**
- A good option when tiredness and low drive are the main symptoms
- Compatible with breastfeeding (low transfer to milk)
- Does not cause sexual side effects (unlike SSRIs)
- Less pregnancy data than SSRIs, but no clear safety signals
- Not a good choice if anxiety is a major symptom (it can make anxiety worse)

**Mirtazapine (Remeron)**
- Sometimes used when sleep is badly disrupted (it is sedating)
- Limited but reassuring pregnancy data
- Compatible with breastfeeding
- Side effects: weight gain and sedation (helpful or unhelpful, depending on the situation)

**Zuranolone (Zurzuvae)**
- Developed specifically for postpartum depression - the first pill approved just for PPD
- A short course: one capsule each evening for 14 days, then done
- Works in days, not weeks - different from standard antidepressants
- Key safety rule: no driving for at least 12 hours after each dose
- Breastfeeding data is limited; discuss with your prescriber
- Usually filled through a specialty pharmacy - ask the team to start insurance approval early
- Full family guide: `Zuranolone_Zurzuvae_Medication_FAM.md`
- *(An older IV version, brexanolone/Zulresso, was withdrawn from the U.S. market in 2025.)*

---

## What About the Baby?

### During Pregnancy

Some babies whose mothers took antidepressants (especially SSRIs and SNRIs) in the third trimester show **neonatal adaptation syndrome** - temporary jitteriness, irritability, mild breathing changes, or feeding trouble. It usually appears in the first 1-3 days after birth and clears within days to two weeks. It is not withdrawal the way adults experience it. It is the baby adjusting.

Your OB and pediatrician will know about your medication and will watch the baby after delivery. This is routine and manageable.

**Important:** Stopping medication in the third trimester to avoid this syndrome is generally *not* advised. The risk of depression returning right at the most vulnerable postpartum weeks outweighs these mild, temporary symptoms.

### During Breastfeeding

All medicines pass into breast milk to some degree. The question is how much, and whether it matters. For the medicines above, the amount reaching the baby is generally very small - often under 2% of the mother's dose, adjusted for weight.

Your treatment team checks **LactMed**, a database run by the National Institutes of Health, for the latest breastfeeding safety data on any medicine.

**You do not have to choose between breastfeeding and medication.** For most antidepressants, you can do both. If there is a concern with your specific medicine, your prescriber will go over options - timing doses around feeds, choosing a medicine with lower milk transfer, or using formula for some feeds.

---

## Decisions That Are Yours to Make

Your treatment team brings the medical facts. These decisions stay yours:

**Whether to breastfeed at all.** Breastfeeding has benefits. So does a healthy, treated parent. If breastfeeding is causing major stress, wrecking your sleep (the #1 recovery factor), or blocking a medicine that works - it is okay to use formula. A present, recovering parent matters more to your baby than breast milk.

**Whether to start, continue, or change medication.** Your prescriber recommends. You decide. Ask every question you have. "What happens if I don't take this?" and "What happens if I do?" are both fair.

**How to feed the baby while on medication.** Some parents breastfeed on medication. Some pump and supplement. Some use formula. All of these are valid choices made by good parents.

---

## Questions to Ask Your Treatment Team

Bring these to your next appointment:

1. "Which medication are you recommending, and why that one?"
2. "What does the breastfeeding safety data show for it?"
3. "If it doesn't work, what would we try next?"
4. "How long will I take it? What does tapering look like?"
5. "Which side effects should I watch for, and which ones mean I should call you?"
6. "What should my OB and the baby's pediatrician know about it?"

---

## Common Misconceptions

**"I should be able to handle this without medication."**
Perinatal depression is a medical condition with real biological causes - hormone shifts, sleep loss, and brain chemistry. You would not treat diabetes with willpower instead of insulin. Medication is a tool, not a failure.

**"Any amount of medication in breast milk is dangerous."**
The dose reaching the baby is almost always tiny - a fraction of a percent. For well-studied medicines like sertraline, decades of data show no developmental concerns in breastfed infants. In most cases, untreated depression poses a greater risk to the baby's development than trace medication in milk.

**"I should stop my medication as soon as I feel better."**
Most guidelines advise continuing for 6-12 months after you feel well. Stopping early is the most common cause of relapse. When it is time, your prescriber will taper slowly - never stop abruptly.

**"Natural supplements are safer than prescription medication."**
"Natural" does not mean "safe in pregnancy." St. John's Wort, for example, has major drug interactions and *less* pregnancy safety data than most prescription antidepressants. If you want to try supplements, tell your prescriber - they need to know.

---

## A Note for Partners and Family

If you are the partner or a family member: your job is not to make the medication decision. Your job is to support the decision that is made. That means:

- No pressure to take or not take medication
- No scary internet articles shared without context
- Going to appointments when invited, and asking your own questions
- Watching for side effects (the recovering parent may not notice their own changes)
- Helping with timing - a daily alarm, a visible pill organizer, a gentle reminder

The recovering parent already carries enough guilt. "Are you sure you should take that while breastfeeding?" does not help. "I trust you and your doctor to make the right call" does.

---

When you're recovering from perinatal depression, your brain needs predictability. But you have a newborn - and newborns are the opposite of predictable. They eat when they're hungry, sleep when they're tired, and have no concept of your schedule.

## What a "Good Enough" Day Looks Like

Not a perfect day. Not a productive day. A good enough day.

**Recovering parent:**
- Got one solid sleep block (4-5 hours uninterrupted)
- Ate at least two real meals sitting down
- Took medication
- Went outside, even briefly
- Had some time that was theirs alone
- Had at least one moment of feeling something other than exhaustion or sadness - even if it was brief

**Partner:**
- Took a sleep shift
- Handled the baby solo for at least one hour
- Checked in with the recovering parent (not about logistics - about how they're feeling)
- Did one thing for their own wellbeing (called a friend, exercised, read something)
- Didn't try to fix everything

**That's it.** If you did those things, today was a success. The dishes can wait. The laundry can wait. The thank-you notes can wait. Recovery and keeping the baby alive - that's the whole job right now.

- **Week 1-2:** You're figuring out what works. Change anything that's not working. The shift schedule may need tweaking based on the baby's feeding patterns.
- **Month 1:** You should have a rhythm that mostly holds. If you don't, bring this template to your treatment team and troubleshoot together.
- **Month 2-3:** You can start adding more to the day - a new parent group, a short outing, a small goal. Don't rush this.
- **Month 3+:** The routine should feel sustainable, not heroic. If it still feels like survival mode at 3 months, that's important information - tell your treatment team.

  intervention. Sleep restoration must be planned around infant care, not against it.
- **L2 modifier:** the regulatory domain is structurally disrupted by the newborn -
  "predictable rhythm" is redesigned around feeds and night care, with explicit load-sharing.
- **L3 configuration:** partners and new grandparents are the operative system; address
  partner exhaustion and role renegotiation directly. Screen the relational field for
  judgment and blame around mothering.
- **L4 modifier:** identity work centers the transition to parenthood; functional goals are
  dyadic (care capacity), not only individual.
- **Cautions:** intrusive thoughts are common and must be differentiated from psychosis or
  intent with care; escalate per L5 when in doubt. Bonding language must never shame.

# Perinatal Psychiatry on the Inpatient Unit

> **Review status:** AI-drafted, evidence-anchored - **pending Dr. Moss's review/attestation before learner use.**

**In one line** - Inpatient perinatal work means treating two patients at once: rapidly distinguishing a postpartum mood or anxiety disorder from postpartum psychosis (an emergency), making every medication decision an explicit risk-benefit conversation, and protecting sleep while you do it.

**How it presents on the unit** - Two pictures dominate. Perinatal mood and anxiety disorders (PMADs) span major depression in pregnancy or postpartum, anxiety, and postpartum OCD - the last classically presenting with ego-dystonic intrusive thoughts of harm to the infant that horrify the parent and drive avoidance rather than action. These patients are distressed but reality-testing is intact. Postpartum psychosis is the other presentation and a different animal entirely: abrupt onset (often within the first two weeks), confusion, mood lability, delusions frequently centered on the infant, and a characteristic waxing-and-waning course. It is strongly linked to bipolar disorder, so screen for a personal or family history of mania.

**Differential & can't-miss mimics** - POSTPARTUM PSYCHOSIS IS A PSYCHIATRIC EMERGENCY: rapid onset, waxing/waning presentation, and real risk to both mother and infant - admit and treat urgently. Rule out medical drivers before anchoring on a primary psychiatric diagnosis: thyroid dysfunction (postpartum thyroiditis), infection/sepsis, and eclampsia-spectrum disease can all mimic an acute psychiatric picture. Then place the patient on the spectrum: baby blues (mild, self-limited, resolving within ~2 weeks, no functional impairment) versus a PMAD (persistent, impairing, treatment-warranting) versus psychosis (perceptual/thought disturbance, an emergency). A lucid interval does not rule out psychosis - the waxing/waning course is part of the illness, not reassurance.

**Initial workup** - Focused and decision-relevant: full MSE with explicit suicide assessment, EPDS (Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale) screening, and active screening for psychotic and manic symptoms, since the EPDS does not detect psychosis. Order TSH (plus CBC, CMP, and infection workup as the picture dictates) to catch medical mimics. The safety assessment must explicitly include the infant: assess risk to the baby, the parent's current access to the baby, and available supervision. Obtain collateral - partner or family history reframes the differential, especially regarding prior mania or prior postpartum episodes.

**Acute inpatient management** - Anchor each decision to the evidence. Postpartum psychosis requires admission and urgent treatment; consider lithium, antipsychotics, and ECT. For postpartum depression, zuranolone is an oral option. Every medication choice in pregnancy and lactation is an individualized risk-benefit discussion, not a reflexive "stop everything" - note specific cautions around lithium and valproate, and weigh that untreated maternal illness itself carries real risk to mother and infant. Protect sleep aggressively: sleep loss both worsens mood and can precipitate or worsen psychosis, so build the milieu and orders around restoring it. Involve the partner and family throughout - for collateral, for the safety plan, and for the supervised support the patient will need after discharge.

**What the student does** -
- Administer and document the EPDS, and explicitly screen for psychotic and manic symptoms beyond it.
- Perform a daily focused MSE with serial suicide assessment that names risk to the infant and current supervision.
- Verify TSH and the infection/medical workup are back and reviewed before the team anchors a diagnosis.
- Gather collateral from the partner or family, asking specifically about prior mania and prior postpartum episodes.
- Round on sleep nightly and confirm the safety plan and family-supervision arrangements are current.

**Disposition & discharge essentials** - Readiness is functional and safety-based: resolving acute risk to mother and infant, restored sleep, a tolerated medication plan, and confirmed supervision and supports at home. Discharge with a timely outpatient follow-up, clear medication monitoring (lithium levels and renal/thyroid follow-up where relevant), a written safety plan with crisis contacts (988; 911 for imminent danger), means-restriction counseling, and an engaged partner or family who understand the warning signs - especially that postpartum psychosis can recur and that future pregnancies warrant advance planning with the team.

**High-yield pearls** -
- Postpartum psychosis is an emergency and is bipolar-spectrum until proven otherwise - screen for mania and admit.
- A clear hour does not rule out psychosis; the waxing/waning course is the illness.
- The EPDS screens depression, not psychosis - ask about delusions and perceptual disturbance directly.
- Postpartum OCD intrusive thoughts are ego-dystonic and not command hallucinations - distinguish them to avoid both over- and under-reaction.
- Stopping all medication is itself a risk decision; untreated perinatal illness endangers mother and infant too.

FULL CITATION
Gunderson, J. G., Herpertz, S. C., Skodol, A. E., Zanarini, M. C., & Livesley, W. J. (2018). Borderline personality disorder. Nature Reviews Disease Primers, 4(1), 18029.

DOI: Not available (comprehensive review)

THE ONE SENTENCE
Borderline personality disorder is a highly heritable disorder of emotion regulation and identity with dimensional (not categorical) phenotype spanning affective instability, impulsivity, self-harm, and relational dysfunction-requiring integrated pharmacological, psychotherapeutic, and systems-based management.

WHY THIS PAPER MATTERS
Gunderson's comprehensive 2018 review synthesized decades of BPD research into current understanding: BPD is not a purely psychological disorder (arising from trauma/invalidating environment) or purely biological (arising from genetic predisposition), but biopsychosocial. Twin studies show 40-60% heritability; trauma history is present but not universal; brain imaging shows structural/functional abnormalities in emotion regulation circuits (amygdala, prefrontal cortex).

The review emphasized that BPD is dimensional, not categorical. Patients vary widely in severity and symptom profile. It highlighted that BPD is treatable (contrary to historical pessimism), that psychotherapy effects are largest, and that medications are adjunctive (no medication is specifically effective for BPD as a whole, though specific medications may target specific symptoms).

Gunderson's review became the standard reference for BPD pathophysiology and treatment, now cited in psychiatric education worldwide.

CLINICAL APPLICATION: WHAT TO DO MONDAY MORNING
1. When assessing a potential BPD patient, avoid categorical thinking ("does she have BPD?"). Instead, assess dimensionally: How severe is emotion dysregulation? Impulsivity? Identity disturbance? This guides targeted treatment.

2. When a BPD patient asks "Why do I feel this way?", explain: "Your brain's emotion regulation circuits-amygdala, prefrontal cortex-work differently. This is partly genetic, partly from life experience. Both matter."

3. When prescribing for BPD, remember: no medication treats BPD directly. Medications target specific symptoms (SSRI for depression, low-dose antipsychotic for anger, mood stabilizer for impulsivity). Psychotherapy is primary.

4. When a BPD patient's family blames parenting or trauma as the sole cause, explain: "Genetics loads the gun; environment pulls the trigger. Your genes make you susceptible; experiences shape how it manifests."

5. When treating comorbid conditions (depression, PTSD) in BPD patients, address them but don't neglect BPD-specific interventions (psychotherapy targeting emotion regulation, identity).

TEACHING SCRIPT (10-MINUTE VERSION)
HOOK: "A BPD patient asks: 'Am I just broken, or can I get better?' Gunderson's review answers that question from biology, psychology, and treatment data."

CONTEXT: "BPD was historically attributed to either 'bad parenting' (psychological blame) or 'personality defect' (pessimism). Gunderson integrates biology and psychology."

KEY FINDING: "BPD is biopsychosocial: 40-60% heritable, associated with trauma/invalidation, involves amygdala hyperactivity and prefrontal hypoactivity. Dimensional phenotype (not categorical): emotion dysregulation severity varies. Treatable with psychotherapy (primary) and adjunctive medications."

CLINICAL TRANSLATION: "BPD isn't a character flaw or parenting failure. It's a neurobiological vulnerability shaped by environment. Understanding biology removes shame; psychotherapy develops skills."

DISCUSSION QUESTION: "If BPD is partly genetic, why do psychotherapy and life experience matter so much for outcome?"

KEY NUMBERS TO REMEMBER
- Heritability: 40-60% (twin/adoption studies)
- Trauma/invalidation history: 50-80% (not universal)
- Response to psychotherapy: 60-80% achieve improvement/remission
- Medication efficacy: modest; no medication has large effect
- Amygdala volume: reduced; hyperactivity on emotion processing tasks
- Prefrontal cortex: hypoactive; impaired emotion regulation

CONTROVERSY AND LIMITATIONS
Critics argue that heritability estimates vary by study design. Second, "invalidating environment" is difficult to operationalize and measure objectively. Third, Gunderson's framework emphasizes biology, which some argue reductionist-social/relational factors are equally important. Fourth, the dimensional approach is more scientifically accurate but less clinically practical (DSM still uses categorical criteria).

However, the biopsychosocial framework is now accepted as standard. Gunderson's review is considered the authoritative reference.

CONNECTIONS TO OTHER PAPERS IN THE LIBRARY

Paper 31 (Zanarini MSAD, 2005) shows BPD has good prognosis; Gunderson explains why (psychotherapy works, natural remission occurs). Paper 39 (Kernberg Personality Organization, 1984) provides structural personality framework; Gunderson bridges structure and neurobiology.

2. Does understanding BPD as biological reduce stigma or increase fatalism?

# Borderline Personality Disorder: Understanding Your Diagnosis

Audience: PAT (Patient)
APA Alignment: Borderline Personality Disorder resources; Psychiatric Evaluation Guideline (3rd Ed)
Reading Level: 6th Grade
Last Updated: 2026-04-22

---

## What Is Borderline Personality Disorder?

Borderline personality disorder (BPD) is a mental health condition. It affects how you feel about yourself, how you relate to others, and how you handle strong emotions.

BPD is not your fault. It is not a character flaw. It often grows out of genes, temperament, and painful experiences - especially in early life. Your brain learned to feel things deeply and to expect hurt in relationships. That made sense given what you lived through. Now it causes pain you did not choose.

About 1 to 2 out of 100 adults have BPD. Many more have some traits of it.

**BPD can get better.** This is important. Older ideas said BPD was lifelong and untreatable. Newer research shows that most people with BPD get much better with the right treatment. Many no longer meet the diagnosis after a few years.

## Signs of BPD

You may have some or all of these:

- Strong fear of being left or rejected
- Relationships that feel very close one moment and very bad the next
- An unstable sense of who you are - values, goals, or self-image keep changing
- Impulsive choices that hurt you (spending, risky sex, substances, reckless driving, binge eating)
- Thoughts of suicide, self-harm, or suicide attempts
- Mood swings that shift fast (hours, not days)
- Chronic feelings of emptiness
- Intense anger, or trouble controlling it
- Feeling disconnected from yourself or reality under stress

To be diagnosed, you need five or more of these. They must cause distress and affect your life.

**A note on stigma:** BPD has been wrongly labeled. You are not "manipulative" or "attention-seeking." You are in pain and using the tools you have. Better tools can be learned.

## Your Treatment Plan

### Therapy and Skills - The Main Treatment

Therapy is the main treatment for BPD. Medicine plays a smaller role. The therapies that work best are:

- **Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT)** - teaches skills in mindfulness, emotion regulation, distress tolerance, and interpersonal effectiveness. The most-studied and widely available.
- **Mentalization-Based Therapy (MBT)** - helps you understand your mind and others' minds under stress
- **Transference-Focused Psychotherapy (TFP)** - uses the therapy relationship to understand your patterns
- **Schema Therapy** - works on deep patterns from early life
- **Good Psychiatric Management (GPM)** - a structured approach any trained clinician can offer

Any of these help. The best one is the one you will stick with. Commitment matters more than choosing "the perfect" therapy.

### Medication and Physical Health

There is **no medicine approved just for BPD**. Medicines are used to help with specific symptoms or with other conditions (depression, anxiety, PTSD, bipolar).

**Sometimes used off-label:**

- **SSRIs** (like sertraline, escitalopram) - may help if you have depression or anxiety
- **Mood stabilizers** (like lamotrigine, valproate) - may help with impulsivity or mood swings
- **Atypical antipsychotics** (like quetiapine low-dose, aripiprazole) - may help with anger, sleep, or paranoia under stress

**What to know:**

- Medicine is not the main treatment. Therapy is.
- Less is usually more - avoid piling on many medicines
- If you take medicines for other conditions, keep taking them
- Tell your doctor about side effects

Talk to your doctor about your choices. Your input matters.

> **Related medication guides in your library:**
> Sertraline, Escitalopram, Lamotrigine, Quetiapine, Aripiprazole

### Assessment and Safety

Your care team will:

- Take a careful history, including any trauma
- Screen for other conditions like depression, PTSD, substance use, or eating disorders
- Ask about self-harm and suicidal thoughts - this is not judgment, it is care
- Make a safety plan with you
- Use skills like chain analysis to look at moments of crisis

**A note about self-harm and suicidal thoughts:** Many people with BPD have these. They are signs of deep pain, not weakness. Please tell your team. Together you can build tools to cope without hurting yourself.

### Family and Relationships

Your relationships are often where the most pain happens. They can also be where the most healing happens.

- **Family involvement** - if your family is willing, family skills classes help them support you better
- **Partner involvement** - couples skills can reduce conflict
- **Repair after rupture** - fights do not end relationships. Repair does. This is a learnable skill.

## Recovery and Moving Forward

Getting better is possible. It takes time. Most people with BPD see major improvement within a few years of good treatment.

- **Practice skills daily** - especially when you do not "need" them. Skills you use in calm times are there when you need them.
- **Build a life worth living** - recovery is not just reducing symptoms. It is adding meaning, connection, and purpose.
- **Expect setbacks** - they are not failures. They are data. Chain analysis turns them into learning.
- **Know your warning signs** - poor sleep, skipping meals, relationship stress, stopping meds
- **Keep your treaters in the loop** - transitions and gaps in care are risky times

## When to Seek Help

Call your team or go to the ER if:

- You are thinking about suicide, especially with a plan
- You have urges to self-harm you cannot resist
- You are using substances to cope in risky ways
- You feel out of touch with reality
- You have a new or worsening medical problem

**In a crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide and Crisis Lifeline). Or go to your nearest ER.**

## Questions to Ask Your Treatment Team

- What therapy do you suggest, and how do I start?
- Is there a DBT program near me?
- What medicines would help which symptoms?
- What are my warning signs, and what is my safety plan?
- Can my family or partner be part of my care?
- How do we handle setbacks without ending treatment?
- What does recovery look like for me?

# Personality Disorders on the Inpatient Unit

> **Review status:** AI-drafted, evidence-anchored - **pending Dr. Moss's review/attestation before learner use.**

**In one line** - Personality pathology (most often borderline personality disorder, BPD) drives a large share of inpatient distress and crisis, and your job is to treat the acute problem while avoiding the iatrogenic harms of overlong, overmedicalized, or poorly coordinated care.

**How it presents on the unit** - Patients are usually admitted in crisis, not because the personality disorder itself responds to hospitalization. Distinguish *chronic suicidality* (a long-standing, fluctuating baseline of suicidal thoughts as a way of coping with unbearable affect) from *acute-on-chronic* escalation (a sharp rise above baseline, often after an interpersonal rupture or loss). Watch for non-suicidal self-injury, intense and rapidly shifting emotions, fear of abandonment, and interpersonal crises that play out on the unit. Splitting-experiencing staff as all-good or all-bad-is common and is a feature of the illness, not a character flaw or manipulation.

**Differential & can't-miss mimics** - Before attributing presentation to personality, rule out a treatable acute episode: major depression, bipolar mood states (especially mixed features), and primary or substance-induced psychosis. Screen for intoxication and withdrawal. Complex PTSD overlaps heavily with BPD (affect dysregulation, relational instability, dissociation) and may be the more actionable frame. The cardinal error is *diagnostic overshadowing*: letting a personality label cause you to under-treat a real, acute, treatable illness. A BPD diagnosis does not protect a patient from also having a serious mood, psychotic, or medical condition.

**Initial workup** - Focused and proportionate. Collateral history (prior records, outpatient clinician, family) is often the highest-yield step. Targeted labs as the presentation dictates (toxicology, TSH, basic metabolic, pregnancy where relevant); medication reconciliation; review prior admissions and what helped or harmed. Clarify the *function* of the current crisis-what changed in the last days-rather than only cataloguing symptoms.

**Acute inpatient management** - Anchor care in a few durable principles. Use a DBT-informed stance (Linehan): pair genuine *validation* of the patient's distress with a steady *push toward change* and skill use. Keep admissions brief and goal-focused; long stays can foster regression and dependency and may worsen outcomes in chronic BPD, so define discharge criteria on day one. Apply consistent, transparent limit-setting and communicate as a unified team-shared notes and handoffs blunt splitting and prevent staff from being divided into "good" and "bad." Minimize polypharmacy; no medication treats BPD itself, and accumulating agents adds risk without benefit. For chronic suicidality, collaborative safety planning is often preferable to repeated or prolonged admission, which can reinforce the hospital as the primary coping strategy. Throughout, notice and manage your own countertransference-frustration, rescue urges, dread-because it shapes decisions and can leak into care.

**What the student does** -
- Obtain and document collateral; reconcile medications and prior-admission outcomes.
- Sit with the patient and build a collaborative safety plan in their words.
- Bring observed splitting or team-division to rounds so the plan stays unified.
- Track suicidality against the patient's chronic baseline, not in the abstract.
- Name your countertransference to your resident or attending and use supervision.

**Disposition & discharge essentials** - Plan discharge from admission. Secure a concrete outpatient follow-up (ideally a structured therapy such as DBT) with a named clinician and date, a written safety plan, means-restriction counseling, and family or support-system involvement when appropriate. Communicate the plan clearly to outpatient providers so continuity is real, not assumed.

**High-yield pearls**
- Hospitalization treats the acute crisis, not the personality disorder-define the goal and the exit early.
- Splitting is a symptom; the antidote is team consistency and communication, not confrontation.
- Always exclude a treatable acute mood, psychotic, or substance disorder before invoking personality.
- Chronic suicidality is managed primarily in the outpatient/safety-planning frame, not by serial admissions.
- Your countertransference is clinical data-track it and use supervision.

**Pair with** - the [suicide-risk & safety pocket card](?page=pg_suicide.md), the <a href="tools/reflection.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Reflection & Identity tool</a>, and the [Family Therapy Modalities](?page=family_modalities.md) material.

# Psychotic Disorders on the Inpatient Unit

> **Review status:** AI-drafted, evidence-anchored - **pending Dr. Moss's review/attestation before learner use.**

**In one line** - Psychosis is a syndrome, not a diagnosis; your job on the unit is to rule out a medical or substance cause, stabilize safety, start an antipsychotic chosen to fit the patient, and - especially in first-episode illness - build the engagement and family scaffolding that determines long-term trajectory.

**How it presents on the unit** - Patients arrive with positive symptoms (delusions, auditory hallucinations, disorganized speech or behavior), negative symptoms (flat affect, alogia, avolition), and disrupted insight. Many do not believe they are ill. Presentations range from the floridly agitated patient requiring rapid stabilization to the withdrawn, internally preoccupied patient who is easy to overlook. First-episode patients are often young, frightened, and brought in by family during a crisis.

**Differential & can't-miss mimics** - Always exclude secondary/medical psychosis before settling on a primary disorder. Think delirium first in any acute, fluctuating presentation with inattention or clouded consciousness. Screen for substance-induced psychosis (stimulants, cannabis, hallucinogens, withdrawal states). Do not miss autoimmune/limbic encephalitis (e.g., anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis - subacute psychosis with seizures, dyskinesias, autonomic instability) and seizure-related phenomena (ictal/postictal psychosis). Then distinguish a mood disorder with psychotic features (psychosis confined to mood episodes) from a primary psychotic disorder. Schizophreniform, brief psychotic disorder, and schizophrenia are duration-based distinctions you refine over time.

**Initial workup** - History (including timeline, substance use, family history, prior episodes), collateral, and full mental status and neurologic exams. Labs: CBC, CMP, TSH, urine toxicology, and where relevant pregnancy test, infectious workup, and substance levels. First-episode psychosis specifically warrants a medical workup because a treatable organic cause is more likely when there is no established psychiatric history; obtain neuroimaging (MRI preferred) when the exam is focal, onset is atypical or abrupt, age is unusual, or there are seizures, and consider EEG and autoimmune/LP workup when encephalitis is on the table. Establish a baseline metabolic panel and weight before starting an antipsychotic.

**Acute inpatient management** - Combine medication, milieu, and safety. Choose the antipsychotic by side-effect profile, not by "newer is better": CATIE showed first- and second-generation agents had broadly comparable effectiveness, with differences driven by tolerability. Huhn 2019 and Leucht 2013 document real, drug-specific efficacy and tolerability differences across antipsychotics - use them to match a drug to the patient's metabolic, motor, and sedation risks. Use the milieu (structure, low stimulation, consistent staff, sleep restoration) and graded safety measures (observation level, environmental safety, least-restrictive de-escalation before PRNs or restraint). For first-episode patients, RAISE supports coordinated specialty care with active family involvement and early psychosocial intervention, not medication alone. Engagement is its own clinical task: many patients have anosognosia, so use Amador's LEAP approach (Listen, Empathize, Agree, Partner) rather than arguing about whether they are ill. For treatment-resistant schizophrenia (inadequate response to two adequate antipsychotic trials), clozapine is the evidence-based agent - pair it with the required hematologic and metabolic monitoring.

**What the student does** - (1) Gather collateral and build a clean symptom and substance timeline. (2) Perform and document serial mental status exams to track trajectory. (3) Track the metabolic baseline and monitoring (weight, glucose, lipids) for any patient on an antipsychotic. (4) Practice a LEAP-style engagement conversation and observe a family meeting. (5) Help reconcile medications and confirm the discharge follow-up is actually booked.

**Disposition & discharge essentials** - Confirm symptom stabilization, adequate insight or supervision for adherence, and a concrete safety plan. Arrange timely outpatient follow-up (coordinated specialty care for first-episode), engage family with consent, reconcile medications with clear monitoring instructions, and address housing, substance use, and any medical comorbidity before the patient leaves.

**High-yield pearls**
- Psychosis is a syndrome - exclude delirium, substances, and autoimmune/seizure causes before calling it primary.
- First-episode psychosis earns a medical workup  neuroimaging; the organic yield is highest here.
- Per CATIE, pick the antipsychotic by side-effect fit; "newer" is not automatically better.
- Anosognosia is not denial - use LEAP, not debate, to build the alliance.
- Two failed adequate antipsychotic trials means consider clozapine, with mandatory monitoring.

**Pair with** - the [Family Meeting Playbook (90-min)](?page=family_playbook.md), the protocol library (benzo taper, clozapine), the Decisional Capacity tool, and the Differential Diagnosis scaffolds.

# The biological-first sequence breaks down when substances are active

**Active substance use creates neurobiological feedback loops that make strict sequential biological->regulatory stabilization ineffective, and every major clinical guideline now recommends concurrent intervention.** The evidence is unambiguous on the architectural question: a clinical manual built on a strict biological->regulatory->relational->functional layer sequence requires a substance use modifier - not merely an exception, but a structural re-routing of the treatment logic. Drake, Mueser, SAMHSA, NICE, and the APA all converge on the position that when substances are in play, regulatory and coping skill work must begin *during* biological stabilization because substances undermine the very neurobiological substrate that biological interventions target. The practical implication is that biological stabilization often cannot be achieved without concurrent regulatory intervention to reduce or manage substance use.

## Why the sequential model collapses under active substance use

The traditional sequential model assumes a stable neurobiological baseline that medications can modulate. Active substance use destroys this assumption through three converging mechanisms.

**Neurotransmitter system hijacking.** Each substance class disrupts the same systems psychiatric medications target. Cannabis triggers mesolimbic dopaminergic hyperactivity while downregulating CB1 receptors, directly opposing antipsychotic mechanisms. Alcohol produces a hypoGABAergic/hyperglutamatergic state through compensatory receptor changes - GABAA receptors downregulate and shift subunit composition (1 replaced by 4/2) while NMDA receptors upregulate. Stimulants cause massive dopamine flooding followed by D2 receptor downregulation and stored dopamine depletion (**28% elevation** in DTBZ binding during early abstinence). Psychiatric medications calibrated for relatively stable neurotransmitter baselines cannot function reliably when these systems oscillate between intoxication and withdrawal states.

**Direct medication interference.** The evidence here is substance-specific and damning. SSRIs are not merely ineffective during active alcohol use - a January 2025 Therapeutics Letter concluded they "do not improve common psychological symptoms in people with substance use disorders" and may *increase* substance use. A Charney et al. RCT found citalopram 40mg produced **worse drinking outcomes** than placebo. For cannabis, THC increases P-glycoprotein transporter activity, physically pumping risperidone out of the brain and reducing its effective concentration. Cannabis also reduces clozapine and olanzapine blood levels through pharmacokinetic interaction. For stimulants, the DSM-5 itself acknowledges that reliable psychiatric diagnosis cannot be made until the patient has been abstinent for **at least four weeks** - the dopaminergic chaos is simply too great.

**Sleep architecture destruction.** A 2025 systematic review in *eClinicalMedicine* (The Lancet) confirmed significant sleep disturbances across all substance classes, persisting into abstinence. Cannabis suppresses REM sleep and produces severe rebound upon cessation. Alcohol disrupts slow-wave and REM architecture, with insomnia persisting up to **two years** into recovery. Stimulant binges produce days without sleep followed by crash hypersomnia. Critically, current addiction pharmacotherapies do not reverse these sleep dysfunctions. Since sleep is foundational to every major psychiatric recovery process, this alone creates a feedback loop that undermines biological-first approaches.

## What NICE, SAMHSA, and the APA actually recommend

All three major guideline bodies have converged on concurrent/integrated treatment as the standard of care, explicitly criticizing sequential approaches.

**NICE CG120** (Coexisting severe mental illness and substance misuse, 2011; last reviewed 2024) states that "for most adults with psychosis and coexisting substance misuse, treatment for both conditions should be provided by healthcare professionals in secondary care mental health services." The evidence review is blunt: sequential and parallel approaches produce "disjointed" care where "accountability and governance is dispersed." NICE's critical nuance on sequencing appears in Recommendation 1.4.21: clinicians should "tailor the plan and the sequencing of treatments to the person" based on severity, social context, and readiness for change - but this means flexible sequencing of *specific interventions within* a concurrent framework, not sequential treatment of disorders. For substance-specific guidance, NICE identifies cannabis and alcohol as the most common co-occurring substances, recommends **joint working with specialist substance misuse services** for severe alcohol dependence and opioid/cocaine dependence, and mandates that detoxification occur in inpatient settings as part of an overall treatment plan.

**SAMHSA TIP 42** (updated 2020) designates integrated treatment as "the standard of care regardless of the treatment setting." It defines three approaches - sequential, parallel, and integrated - and explicitly states that sequential treatment's "effectiveness is dubious and may lead to worse outcomes given that, in some conditions, treatment of one disorder can worsen symptoms of the other." The foundational principle: "SUDs and mental disorders are treated concurrently to meet the full range of clients' symptoms." SAMHSA's Four Quadrant Model determines treatment intensity by severity, not which disorder gets addressed first. In a validation study, **54% of patients** presenting to a county hospital fell into Quadrant IV (high severity on both dimensions), requiring intensive integrated services.

**The APA** rejects "arbitrary waiting periods of sobriety" before psychiatric assessment or treatment. Across its substance use disorder, schizophrenia, and alcohol use disorder guidelines, the consistent message is concurrent integrated care. Kenneth Minkoff's influential formulation in *Psychiatric Services* captures the position: "each disorder should be considered as primary, and integrated dual primary treatment should be provided."

## Drake, Mueser, and the staged-not-sequential distinction

The Drake/Mueser Integrated Dual Disorder Treatment (IDDT) model makes a distinction that is architecturally critical for the manual question. IDDT uses **four stages** - engagement, persuasion, active treatment, relapse prevention - but these stages track the *client's readiness*, not which disorder gets treated. At every stage, both mental illness and substance use are addressed simultaneously. A client might be in the persuasion stage for substance use while in active treatment for their mental illness.

Drake et al. (2001) identified the sequential approach as the primary failure mode: "the parallel but separate mental health and substance abuse treatment systems deliver fragmented and ineffective care. Most clients are unable to navigate the separate systems or make sense of disparate messages." They documented that patients with substance abuse were **8.1 times more likely** to be noncompliant with psychiatric medications - meaning biological stabilization through medication alone fails at enormously high rates when substance use is unaddressed.

The IDDT model insists that "attention to substance abuse as well as mental illness is integrated into all aspects of the existing mental health program" - including hospitalization, crisis intervention, medication management, and housing. Even inpatient hospitalization is framed as supporting "movement toward recovery by providing diagnosis, stabilization, AND linkage with outpatient dual diagnosis interventions during acute episodes." The model explicitly positions regulatory and coping skill work (motivational interventions, CBT, skills training) as concurrent with and necessary for biological stabilization.

Lubman and colleagues at Monash/Turning Point reinforce this position. Cheetham, Arunogiri & Lubman (2023) found "considerable evidence to support the effectiveness of integrated approaches," particularly for high-prevalence co-occurring disorders. Their practical contribution is demonstrating that integration can occur at the clinician level even without organizational restructuring. Horsfall et al. (2009), in a widely-cited *Harvard Review of Psychiatry* paper, argued pragmatically that the model matters less than the quality of delivery - well-coordinated, multidisciplinary, specialist-trained teams with long-term follow-up produce good outcomes regardless of whether they call their approach "integrated" or "parallel."

## Substance-specific timelines reshape the stabilization window

The neurobiological normalization timelines for each substance class have direct implications for how the biological layer should be architected.

**Cannabis** requires **4-12 weeks** for endocannabinoid system normalization after cessation, with CB1 receptor upregulation beginning within days but structural brain changes (particularly hippocampal gray matter loss in early psychosis patients) potentially taking months to years. The Finnish NEMESIS registry found that nearly **50% of cannabis-induced psychosis cases** were later rediagnosed as schizophrenia - a transition rate that argues strongly for concurrent psychiatric and substance use intervention rather than waiting to see if psychosis resolves. Ongoing cannabis use maintains a dopaminergic disruption pattern that overlaps directly with the neurocircuitry of psychosis, creating a feedback loop where the substance perpetuates the very condition medications are trying to stabilize.

**Alcohol** produces the most dramatic acute neurobiological disruption. The GABA/glutamate rebalancing window is **1-4 weeks** for acute normalization, with GABAA receptor binding normalizing at approximately one month and full receptor subunit composition recovery potentially taking **1-2+ years** in heavy drinkers. Crucially, depression and anxiety symptoms in alcohol use disorder frequently **resolve spontaneously with abstinence** (Brown 1995; Nunes 2004) - meaning that medicating these symptoms during active drinking may be treating substance-induced phenomena unnecessarily and ineffectively. The Nunes & Levin (2004) meta-analysis of 14 RCTs found antidepressants showed only a **modest effect (d=0.38)** for depression in combined populations, with the strongest effects appearing in studies that required abstinence before depression diagnosis.

**Stimulants** present the longest recovery timeline. DAT distribution requires **60-90 days** to normalize (rat data), and primate studies show that after prolonged cocaine exposure, **40% showed no D2 receptor recovery even after one year**. Methamphetamine DAT levels approach near-normal only at **12-18 months** (Volkow et al., 2001 PET data). The protracted anhedonia and dysphoria lasting months in methamphetamine users mimics depression, creating diagnostic confusion that undermines biological-layer treatment targeting. The ASAM/AAAP 2024 guideline acknowledges this explicitly: psychiatric comorbidity "is dynamic and should be reassessed throughout the continuum of care."

## The inpatient evidence and outcome data

Evidence from inpatient psychiatric settings reinforces the concurrent approach. Scott & Gorey (2025, *Journal of Dual Diagnosis*) published a meta-analysis of 13 studies showing a **pooled relative risk of 1.71** (95% CI 1.38-2.13) for adverse outcomes - including substance relapse, rehospitalization, and death - among dual-diagnosis patients receiving unintegrated treatment. Swanson et al. (1999) demonstrated that adding brief motivational interviewing to standard inpatient treatment more than doubled aftercare attendance (**47% vs. 21%**), suggesting that even minimal concurrent regulatory intervention during the biological stabilization phase significantly improves trajectory.

The UK Dual Diagnosis Good Practice Guide (Department of Health, 2002) concluded that "integrated care by a single team delivers better outcomes than serial care or parallel care." Hakobyan et al.'s 2020 systematic review of concurrent disorder management guidelines stated plainly: "current evidence seems to suggest that the sequential model is obsolete."

It is worth noting an important caveat on evidence quality. The 2019 Cochrane review (Hunt et al.) found "no high-quality evidence in support of any one psychosocial intervention over standard care" for dual diagnosis, and a 2023 systematic review of 11 RCTs found integrated treatment improved psychiatric symptoms but not substance use outcomes compared to non-integrated treatment. However, these findings reflect methodological limitations in the study base - not evidence favoring sequential approaches. No study has demonstrated that sequential treatment outperforms integrated treatment for any outcome.

## Architectural implications for the clinical manual

The evidence supports a specific architectural modification rather than a simple exception. The manual's biological->regulatory->relational->functional sequence assumes that biological stabilization can be achieved as a foundation upon which subsequent layers build. When active substance use is present, this assumption fails because:

- **The biological layer cannot stabilize without regulatory input.** Patients need coping and motivational skills to reduce substance use, but substance reduction is a prerequisite for biological stabilization. This creates a circular dependency that the sequential architecture cannot resolve. Drake and Mueser's staged model addresses this by running biological and regulatory interventions in parallel, with intensity matched to readiness.

- **The feedback loop is substance-specific but universally present.** Cannabis disrupts the endocannabinoid and dopaminergic systems, alcohol disrupts GABA/glutamate balance, and stimulants disrupt dopamine homeostasis. Each creates a distinct neurobiological pattern that undermines medication efficacy, but all share the common property of preventing the stable neurochemical baseline that biological intervention requires.

- **The regulatory layer becomes a precondition for the biological layer, not a successor.** This is the core architectural insight. When substances are active, motivational interviewing, harm reduction strategies, basic coping skills, and psychoeducation about substance-psychiatric interactions are not "layer two" interventions - they are prerequisites for layer one to function. The IDDT model operationalizes this by treating engagement and persuasion as concurrent with psychiatric stabilization.

The recommended modification is a **concurrent entry point** for the biological and regulatory layers when substance use is active, with the treatment plan acknowledging that biological stabilization will be partial and iterative until substance use is sufficiently reduced. This aligns with NICE's recommendation to "tailor the sequencing of treatments to the person" within an integrated framework, SAMHSA's staged model, and Drake/Mueser's explicit rejection of sequential approaches. The relational and functional layers can retain their sequential position, but the first two layers must be capable of running in parallel when substances are in play.

## Conclusion

The evidence base - spanning NICE CG120/NG58, SAMHSA TIP 42, APA guidelines, Drake and Mueser's IDDT model, Lubman's integrated care research, and Horsfall's service delivery framework - converges on a single architectural conclusion: **strict biological-first sequencing is contraindicated when active substance use is present**. The neurobiological feedback loops are real, substance-specific, and well-documented. SSRIs fail during active drinking. Antipsychotic brain levels drop during cannabis use. Dopamine systems remain chaotic for months after stimulant cessation. The sequential model was declared "obsolete" in a 2020 guideline review, and a 2025 meta-analysis found a **71% increase in adverse outcomes** in unintegrated treatment. The manual needs not an exception but a structural modifier: when substances are active, biological and regulatory layers must run concurrently, with the regulatory layer providing the motivational and coping scaffolding without which biological stabilization cannot take hold.

# Substance Use & Withdrawal on the Inpatient Unit

> **Review status:** AI-drafted, evidence-anchored - **pending Dr. Moss's review/attestation before learner use.**

**In one line** - On the inpatient unit, substance use disorders show up as intoxication, withdrawal, or a confounder of every other psychiatric presentation, and your job is to keep the patient physiologically safe while engaging them, without judgment, in treatment that continues after discharge.

**How it presents on the unit** - Three overlapping patterns. *Intoxication* can mimic or unmask psychiatric illness (stimulant-induced paranoia, alcohol-related mood lability, sedative disinhibition). *Withdrawal* is the time-sensitive one: alcohol and benzodiazepine withdrawal can progress to seizures and delirium tremens and can kill, whereas opioid and stimulant withdrawal are intensely uncomfortable but rarely lethal. *Co-occurring psychiatric illness* is the rule, not the exception - depression, anxiety, PTSD, and psychotic disorders frequently travel with substance use, and you cannot reliably diagnose a primary mood or psychotic disorder while the patient is acutely intoxicated or withdrawing.

**Differential & can't-miss mimics** - Altered mental status in a patient who uses substances is never automatically "just withdrawal." Keep these live: **Wernicke encephalopathy** (confusion, ophthalmoplegia, ataxia - often incomplete, so a low threshold is correct); **head injury / subdural hematoma** (falls and trauma are common); **occult infection / sepsis** (meningitis, pneumonia, aspiration); **hepatic encephalopathy** in the patient with alcohol-related liver disease (asterixis, elevated ammonia); and **polysubstance use masking** a more dangerous co-ingestant or an evolving metabolic, electrolyte, or anticholinergic picture. Anchor every withdrawal assessment with vitals and a neuro exam.

**Initial workup** - Focused and pragmatic: full vitals with a neurologic exam, fingerstick glucose, basic metabolic panel and magnesium, liver function tests, CBC, a urine drug screen and blood/breath alcohol, and a pregnancy test where relevant. Add ECG when QTc-affecting agents or methadone are in play. Image the head if there is trauma, a focal deficit, or unexplained or out-of-proportion altered mental status.

**Acute inpatient management** - For **alcohol withdrawal**, score serially with the **CIWA-Ar** and treat symptom-driven with **benzodiazepines**, escalating vigilance for **seizures and delirium tremens** in high-risk patients. Give **thiamine before glucose** - administering carbohydrate to a thiamine-depleted patient can precipitate Wernicke encephalopathy. For **opioid withdrawal**, track severity with the **COWS**, and begin **buprenorphine induction only once objective withdrawal is present (roughly COWS greater than or equal to 8 to 12)** to avoid precipitated withdrawal from displacing residual full agonist too early. For ongoing **alcohol use disorder**, offer **naltrexone or acamprosate**. Frame the whole encounter through the **Volkow brain-disease model** of addiction - naming addiction as a chronic, treatable brain condition rather than a moral failing directly combats stigma - and use **motivational interviewing** to meet ambivalence with curiosity rather than confrontation.

**What the student does**
- Perform and document serial **CIWA-Ar** or **COWS** scores at the bedside and flag trends to the team.
- Lay hands on the patient: vitals, hydration status, pupils, gait, asterixis - and report concerning changes early.
- Verify **thiamine was given before any glucose-containing fluids**.
- Take a non-judgmental substance history (substances, routes, last use, prior withdrawal/seizures) using open questions.
- Practice one motivational-interviewing reflection and bring it to rounds.

**Disposition & discharge essentials** - Discharge is the intervention that prevents the next admission. Provide **naloxone and overdose-prevention education** to any patient at opioid risk and to their family. Arrange **linkage to medication for opioid use disorder (MOUD)** - buprenorphine or methadone - before the patient leaves, since the post-discharge window carries elevated overdose risk. Confirm follow-up, a warm handoff where possible, and continuation of any alcohol use disorder pharmacotherapy started inpatient.

**High-yield pearls**
- Alcohol and benzodiazepine withdrawal can be lethal; opioid and stimulant withdrawal are miserable but rarely fatal - match your urgency accordingly.
- Thiamine before glucose, every time.
- Do not start buprenorphine until objective withdrawal is on board (COWS roughly greater than or equal to 8 to 12) or you may precipitate withdrawal.
- A patient who uses substances with new confusion gets a real differential - Wernicke, trauma, infection, hepatic encephalopathy - not a reflex withdrawal label.
- Naloxone plus MOUD linkage at discharge is a concrete, evidence-based way to save a life.
