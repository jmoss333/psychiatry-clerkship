# MS3 Adult Inpatient Psychiatry Orientation Packet

Generated: 2026-06-27

Audience: third-year medical students on a six-week adult inpatient psychiatry rotation.

## Welcome

This rotation teaches psychiatric assessment, safety reasoning, formulation,
treatment planning, documentation, and communication in the setting where
psychiatric symptoms, medical risk, family stress, and systems barriers converge.

Your job is not to know everything on Day 1. Your job is to be curious,
prepared, safe, honest about uncertainty, and responsive to feedback.

## The Single Safety Rule

If you are worried about immediate safety, tell the resident or attending now.
Do not wait for rounds. Do not carry it alone.

Escalate immediately for:

- Current suicidal intent, new plan, access to lethal means, or sudden worsening.
- Threats toward others or escalating agitation.
- Confusion, fluctuating consciousness, fever, rigidity, autonomic instability, or possible delirium/catatonia.
- Alcohol, benzodiazepine, opioid, or other withdrawal concern.
- Medication adverse effect that could be urgent.
- Any patient, family, or staff interaction that feels unsafe.

## PHI Discipline

- No real patient details leave the clinical system.
- Do not put patient details into AI tools, personal notes, cloud docs, texts, or email.
- Teaching cases must be synthetic or carefully de-identified composites.
- When in doubt, ask before sharing.

## Daily Rhythm

| Time | Student Task |
|---|---|
| Before rounds | Review assigned patients, vitals, sleep, PRNs, nursing notes, labs, safety events |
| Rounds | Listen for risk, diagnosis, medication changes, family/discharge barriers |
| After rounds | See patients, gather collateral with permission, update note draft |
| Midday | Teaching, consults, family meetings, discharge planning |
| Afternoon | Present updates, revise notes, identify questions for next day |
| End of day | Clarify follow-up tasks and safety concerns |

Local times and locations should be inserted by the clerkship director.

## What Students Should Practice Each Week

| Week | Focus | Observable Skill |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Orientation, interview, MSE | Present a focused psychiatric interview and MSE |
| 2 | Diagnosis, differential, formulation | Build a differential beyond the primary psychiatric diagnosis |
| 3 | Treatment | Explain why a medication and one non-medication intervention fit the formulation |
| 4 | Acute/consult psychiatry | Formulate suicide/violence risk and recognize delirium/catatonia/withdrawal |
| 5 | Family/discharge/systems | Draft a family-meeting agenda and discharge barrier map |
| 6 | Integration | Present a full case with formulation, risk reasoning, and plan |

## How To Think On This Rotation

Use four questions for every patient:

1. What is the current syndrome or problem?
2. What medical, substance, sleep, medication, or neurologic factors could be driving it?
3. What family, trauma, social, legal, or discharge context will change the plan?
4. What must be safer, clearer, or more connected before discharge?

## Expected Competencies By End Of Rotation

By Week 6, the student should be able to:

- Gather a psychiatric history and MSE.
- Prioritize a differential diagnosis after a psychiatric encounter.
- Present a concise oral case summary.
- Write a note that includes reasoning, not just checklist facts.
- Recognize urgent safety concerns and escalate.
- Describe basic medication rationale and monitoring.
- Participate in collateral gathering and family meetings under supervision.
- Map discharge barriers and outpatient follow-up needs.

EPA alignment: AAMC Core EPAs 1, 2, 5, 6, 8, and 10.

## What Good Student Presentations Sound Like

Start with:

> "This is a [age]-year-old [person] admitted for [reason], now hospital day
> [X], with current priority of [risk/diagnostic/treatment/disposition issue]."

Then cover:

- Overnight safety events, sleep, PRNs, vital signs, labs.
- Patient report and MSE changes.
- Risk update: what is better, worse, unchanged.
- Differential or formulation update.
- Plan for medication, therapy/milieu, family/collateral, discharge.

## How Feedback Works

Ask for feedback on one behavior at a time:

- "Can you watch my MSE language today?"
- "Can you tell me if my risk formulation separates chronic and acute risk?"
- "Can you help me make my presentation more concise?"
- "Can you review whether my note shows reasoning?"

You should receive brief daily feedback and a midpoint check-in.

## Student Boundaries

You may:

- Interview patients under supervision.
- Gather collateral when the patient gives permission and the team approves.
- Draft notes according to local policy.
- Present differential, formulation, and plan suggestions.
- Observe or participate in family meetings with a defined role.

You may not:

- Make independent safety or discharge decisions.
- Promise discharge, medication changes, privileges, or family access.
- Provide unsupervised psychotherapy.
- Contact family without team approval and patient consent unless an emergency/legal exception applies.
- Put patient information into personal systems.

## Day 1 Checklist

- Know how to contact the attending/resident.
- Know where rounds happen.
- Confirm EMR access.
- Learn unit safety expectations.
- Identify assigned patients.
- Read the interview/MSE pocket guide.
- Ask what note type, if any, you should draft.
- Ask when and how feedback happens.

## Day 2 Preparation

Read:

- Interview/MSE pocket guide.
- Week 1 section of the reading map.
- One short landmark summary: Engel biopsychosocial model or Brown expressed emotion.

Bring:

- One question about diagnosis.
- One question about safety.
- One question about discharge or family context.

Plain-English note: this packet tells students how to be useful and safe on the
unit before they know the medicine deeply. It turns the rotation into observable
skills rather than vague exposure.

# Formulation And Differential Diagnosis Pocket Guide

Generated: 2026-06-27

Audience: MS3 students.

## The Difference

Differential diagnosis asks:

> "What could this be?"

Formulation asks:

> "Why this patient, why now, and what does that mean for the plan?"

Students often stop after diagnosis. In inpatient psychiatry, that is not enough.

## Five-Part Differential

For every new patient, consider:

1. Primary psychiatric syndrome.
2. Substance or medication-induced condition.
3. Medical or neurologic contributor.
4. Trauma, stressor, or developmental context.
5. Family/system/disposition factor affecting safety and recovery.

## Common Inpatient Presentations

### Psychosis

Consider:

- Primary psychotic disorder.
- Mood disorder with psychotic features.
- Substance/medication-induced psychosis.
- Delirium or neurologic illness.
- Trauma-related dissociation or hypervigilance.

Ask:

- Timeline of psychosis vs mood symptoms.
- Sleep change.
- Substance use and last use.
- New medications.
- Orientation and attention.

### Mania Or Severe Agitation

Consider:

- Bipolar mania.
- Substance intoxication or withdrawal.
- Medication effect, including steroids or stimulants.
- Delirium.
- Akathisia.
- Trauma/fear response.

Ask:

- Decreased need for sleep vs insomnia with fatigue.
- Grandiosity, pressured speech, risky behavior.
- Stimulants, cannabis, antidepressants, steroids.
- Vital signs, attention, fluctuating course.

### Severe Depression Or Suicidality

Consider:

- Major depressive episode.
- Bipolar depression.
- Substance-induced mood disorder.
- Grief, trauma, adjustment disorder.
- Medical contributors.
- Personality pathology and interpersonal crisis.

Ask:

- Episodicity and past mania/hypomania.
- Alcohol and sedative use.
- Sleep, appetite, energy.
- Intent, plan, means, preparation.
- Collateral and protective relationships.

### Confusion Or Disorganization

Consider:

- Delirium.
- Intoxication or withdrawal.
- Psychosis.
- Mania.
- Catatonia.
- Dementia or neurologic disorder.

Ask:

- Acute vs chronic.
- Fluctuating vs stable.
- Attention.
- Vitals, infection, hypoxia, medications.
- Baseline from collateral.

### Refusal Of Care

Consider:

- Capacity issue.
- Fear, mistrust, trauma, or communication failure.
- Psychosis, mania, depression.
- Delirium or intoxication.
- Values-based refusal.

Ask:

- Exact decision.
- Understanding, appreciation, reasoning, choice.
- What the patient is afraid will happen.
- Whether attention or psychosis is impairing the decision.

## Four-Layer Formulation

Use plain language:

1. Body and brain: sleep, substance, medications, medical/neurologic factors.
2. Psychiatric syndrome and treatment: diagnosis, symptoms, therapy/medication needs.
3. Relationship and environment: family, trauma, housing, supports, conflict, culture.
4. Recovery and role: identity, function, meaning, goals, discharge life.

Template:

> "[Patient] is admitted with [syndrome/problem] in the setting of [body/brain
> contributors], [psychological/diagnostic factors], and [family/social/system
> factors]. The immediate risk is [X]. The plan should prioritize [safety/medical
> stabilization], [treatment], [family/collateral/disposition], and [recovery
> goal]."

## One-Minute Formulation Example

> "This is a young adult admitted with first-episode mania with psychotic
> features. Body/brain contributors include severe sleep loss and daily cannabis
> use. Psychiatric treatment priorities are mood stabilization, antipsychotic
> treatment, and monitoring adverse effects. Family context matters because the
> family is frightened and may respond with over-control, which could make
> discharge planning harder. Recovery work includes returning to school in a
> staged way rather than treating discharge as immediate return to baseline."

## Faculty Questions That Improve Reasoning

- What would make this diagnosis wrong?
- What medical or substance cause would be dangerous to miss?
- What changed in the last week?
- What collateral would change the plan?
- What is the discharge barrier?
- What is the modifiable risk target today?

## Student Checklist

Before presenting, confirm:

- I have a differential, not just a diagnosis.
- I included substance/medication and medical contributors.
- I separated chronic and acute risk.
- I know what collateral is missing.
- I can name one family/system factor.
- I can say what must change before discharge.

Plain-English note: this guide gives students a repeatable thinking structure.
It keeps them from diagnosing too early and helps them connect the diagnosis to
the treatment and discharge plan.

# Psychiatric Interview And MSE Pocket Guide

Generated: 2026-06-27

Audience: MS3 students.

## The Interview Frame

Open with role, purpose, time, and consent.

Example:

> "Hi, I am a medical student working with the psychiatry team. I would like to
> understand what brought you here, how things have been going, and what feels
> most important today. We have about 20 minutes. Is it okay if we talk now?"

## The interview as a circle

Think of the interview less as a straight line and more as a **circle with the patient's story at the centre.** You move outward to gather each domain - HPI, safety, psychiatric history, substance use, medical and medications, trauma and development, family and social, function and strengths - but you can **always return to the middle and flow with the conversation** wherever it naturally leads. Treat the list below as a *coverage map* (make sure every section gets touched), not a script to read in order.

<iframe src="tools/interview-circle.html?embed=1" title="The Interview Circle (interactive)" style="width:100%;height:1080px;border:1px solid var(--border);border-radius:14px;margin:16px 0;display:block;background:var(--bg)"></iframe>

**Working the circle.** Open in the centre with the patient's narrative, then let their words pull you to whichever domain they open; follow that thread, come back to the centre, and pick up the next one. Gather safety the moment the door opens to it - and always before you close. By the end, every section of the circle should be covered, even though the path through it was the patient's, not yours.

Cover all of these - in whatever order the conversation allows:

1. Opening narrative.
2. Current symptoms and timeline.
3. Safety: suicide, self-harm, violence, vulnerability.
4. Psychiatric history.
5. Substance use and withdrawal risk.
6. Medical/neurologic contributors.
7. Medications, adherence, adverse effects.
8. Trauma and developmental context, asked gently.
9. Family, supports, living situation, collateral.
10. Function, strengths, values, discharge barriers.
11. Close with summary and next step.

## High-Yield Opening Questions

- "What was happening before you came to the hospital?"
- "What feels most urgent to you today?"
- "When did this start to feel different from your usual baseline?"
- "What has helped even a little?"
- "Who knows you well enough to notice when you are not yourself?"

## Safety Questions

Ask directly and calmly.

Suicide:

- "Have you wished you would not wake up?"
- "Have you had thoughts of killing yourself?"
- "Have you thought about how you would do it?"
- "Have you taken any steps toward that?"
- "What has stopped you so far?"
- "Do you have access to firearms, stockpiled medications, or other lethal means?"

Violence:

- "Have you felt afraid you might hurt someone?"
- "Has anyone felt unsafe around you recently?"
- "Have you made threats or had thoughts of retaliation?"
- "Do you have access to weapons?"

Vulnerability:

- "Has anyone hurt, threatened, coerced, or exploited you?"
- "Do you feel safe returning to where you were living?"

## Substance And Withdrawal Screen

Ask about:

- Alcohol: amount, frequency, last drink, morning drinking, prior withdrawal, seizures, delirium tremens.
- Benzodiazepines: prescribed or non-prescribed, daily dose, last use.
- Opioids: last use, route, tolerance, withdrawal symptoms, overdose history.
- Stimulants: amount, route, sleep loss, paranoia, crash/depression.
- Cannabis: daily use, potency, psychosis/anxiety connection.

Escalate if there is possible alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal risk.

## Collateral

Ask permission explicitly:

> "Would it be okay if we spoke with someone who knows you well? It helps us
> understand what changed and how to support you after discharge."

If the patient says no:

- Reflect the reason.
- Ask what they worry would happen.
- Offer a limited call with the patient present.
- Escalate to the team if safety requires an exception.

## Trauma-Informed Moves

- Ask permission before sensitive topics.
- Explain why you are asking.
- Give control where possible.
- Avoid pressing for details that are not needed for immediate care.
- Thank the patient for telling you.

Example:

> "I ask everyone some safety and trauma questions because those experiences can
> affect sleep, trust, and discharge planning. You do not have to share details
> today."

## MSE Structure

| Domain | What To Observe Or Ask | Example Language |
|---|---|---|
| Appearance | hygiene, dress, apparent age, distress | "Appears stated age, hospital attire, mildly disheveled" |
| Behavior | cooperation, eye contact, psychomotor activity | "Guarded but cooperative; intermittent eye contact" |
| Speech | rate, rhythm, volume, latency | "Soft, slowed, increased latency" |
| Mood | patient quote | Mood: "empty" |
| Affect | range, reactivity, congruence | "Constricted, mood-congruent, minimally reactive" |
| Thought process | linear, tangential, circumstantial, disorganized | "Tangential but redirectable" |
| Thought content | SI, HI, delusions, obsessions, preoccupations | "Persecutory ideas without fixed delusional conviction" |
| Perception | hallucinations, illusions | "Reports auditory hallucinations; no visual hallucinations" |
| Cognition | orientation, attention, memory when relevant | "Oriented to person/place; impaired attention on months backward" |
| Insight | understanding of illness/situation | "Partial insight into depression; limited insight into alcohol contribution" |
| Judgment | decision-making in context | "Impaired around safety planning; accepts inpatient care" |

## MSE Words To Use Carefully

Avoid vague or stigmatizing words:

- "Manipulative" -> describe the behavior and context.
- "Noncompliant" -> "missed doses because..." or "declined because..."
- "Attention-seeking" -> "sought staff support repeatedly when distressed."
- "Poor historian" -> "timeline limited by disorganization/intoxication/anxiety."
- "Denies" alone -> include what you asked and how risk was reasoned.

## Mini Cognitive Screen

Use when delirium, dementia, intoxication, withdrawal, catatonia, or medical illness is possible.

- Orientation: person, place, date, situation.
- Attention: days of week backward or months backward.
- Fluctuation: "Has this changed during the day?"
- Arousal: alert, drowsy, hypervigilant, fluctuating.

Delirium is a clinical emergency until proven otherwise.

## Closing The Interview

End with:

- Summary in the patient's language.
- One correction invitation.
- One next step.

Example:

> "Let me make sure I understood. The last two weeks have been marked by very
> little sleep, more drinking, and thoughts of not wanting to live, especially
> at night. I may have missed something. What should I correct? The next step is
> that I will share this with the team and we will talk with you about safety
> and sleep today."

## What To Present After

Use this 30-second update:

1. One-line ID and reason for admission.
2. Current syndrome and timeline.
3. Safety/risk update.
4. MSE highlights.
5. Differential or formulation.
6. One question for the team.

Plain-English note: this guide keeps the student from bouncing between random
questions. It gives a sequence, exact language, and a way to turn the interview
into a presentation.

# Suicide Risk And Safety Pocket Card

Generated: 2026-06-27

Audience: MS3 students.

This is an educational framework for supervised clinical work. Follow local
policy and supervising clinician direction.

## Ask Directly

Passive death wish:

- "Have you wished you would not wake up?"

Suicidal thoughts:

- "Have you had thoughts of killing yourself?"

Plan:

- "Have you thought about how you would do it?"

Intent:

- "How likely do you feel you are to act on those thoughts?"

Preparation:

- "Have you taken any steps, like writing a note, gathering pills, searching
  methods, giving things away, or going somewhere you might act?"

Means:

- "Do you have access to firearms, stockpiled medication, or other ways you
  could seriously harm yourself?"

Past behavior:

- "Have you ever tried to end your life or come close?"

## Separate Chronic And Acute Risk

Chronic risk:

- Prior attempts.
- Family history of suicide.
- Chronic psychiatric illness.
- Substance use disorder.
- Trauma history.
- Demographic and social risk factors.

Acute risk:

- Current intent or plan.
- Recent preparation.
- Recent loss or humiliation.
- Intoxication or withdrawal.
- Severe insomnia.
- Agitation.
- Psychosis or command hallucinations.
- New access to lethal means.
- Sudden loss of supports.

Protective factors:

- Reasons for living.
- Connected relationships.
- Treatment engagement.
- Future orientation.
- Cultural/spiritual beliefs.
- Restricted means.

Protective factors are not magic. They matter only if they are credible and
available during the risk window.

## Risk Formulation Template

> "Chronic suicide risk is [low/moderate/high] because [factors]. Acute risk is
> [low/moderate/high] today because [current drivers]. Protective factors include
> [specific factors], but [limits]. Modifiable targets are [sleep/substance/
> psychosis/family conflict/means/follow-up]. Current plan is [level of care and
> safety steps]."

## Means Safety

Ask specifically. Do not say only "we discussed safety."

Include:

- Firearms.
- Stockpiled medication.
- Sharps or ligatures when relevant.
- Access to high places, vehicles, or other patient-specific means.
- Who will remove/secure means.
- How it will be confirmed.

## Safety Planning Elements

A strong safety plan includes:

1. Warning signs.
2. Internal coping strategies.
3. People or places that provide distraction.
4. People to ask for help.
5. Professionals/crisis resources.
6. Means safety.

Do not treat a safety plan as a discharge permission slip. It is one component
of a broader risk and disposition plan.

## What To Escalate Immediately

- Current intent.
- Preparatory behavior.
- Inability or unwillingness to collaborate on safety.
- Intoxication or withdrawal.
- Severe agitation or psychosis.
- Access to lethal means.
- Collateral that contradicts patient report.
- Sudden improvement after severe suicidality without clear explanation.

## Documentation Sentence

Strong:

> "Acute suicide risk remains high due to recent preparatory behavior,
> persistent insomnia, alcohol escalation, and firearm access not yet confirmed
> secured; chronic risk is elevated by prior attempt and family history; plan is
> continued inpatient care, collateral with sibling, and confirmed means
> restriction before any discharge planning."

Weak:

> "Denies SI/HI. No acute safety concerns."

## Student Role

You can:

- Ask direct questions.
- Report what you heard.
- Draft a risk formulation.
- Identify missing collateral.
- Ask for help.

You cannot:

- Clear a patient for discharge independently.
- Promise confidentiality when safety exceptions may apply.
- Treat "denies SI" as the end of the assessment.

Plain-English note: this card helps students ask the uncomfortable questions
directly and then turn answers into a real risk formulation.

# Acute And Consult Psychiatry Expansion Module

Generated: 2026-06-27

Audience: MS3 students on adult inpatient psychiatry.

Scope: consult psychiatry, capacity, delirium, catatonia, withdrawal, and urgent
escalation. This is an educational guide. Follow local protocols and supervising
clinician direction for diagnosis and treatment.

## Consult Psychiatry: The Student Frame

A good psychiatry consult starts with the question.

Ask:

1. Who is asking?
2. What decision is blocked?
3. What changed today?
4. What medical/substance/medication causes must be considered?
5. What would be different after psychiatry gives an answer?

Poor consult question:

> "Please evaluate."

Better consult question:

> "Please assess decision-making capacity to refuse IV antibiotics in a patient
> with fluctuating attention and paranoid concerns about poisoning."

## Capacity Basics

Capacity is decision-specific and time-specific. A patient can have capacity for
one decision and not another.

Assess four abilities:

1. Communicate a stable choice.
2. Understand relevant information.
3. Appreciate how the information applies personally.
4. Reason about options and consequences.

Capacity is not the same as:

- Agreeing with the team.
- Having a psychiatric diagnosis.
- Having good judgment in general.
- Being calm or likeable.
- Competence, which is a legal determination.

Student task:

- State the exact decision.
- Summarize the patient's words.
- Identify which ability is intact or impaired.
- Ask whether delirium, intoxication, psychosis, mania, depression, pain, or fear is impairing the decision.

## Delirium: Think Medical Until Proven Otherwise

Core pattern:

- Acute change.
- Fluctuating course.
- Inattention.
- Altered level of awareness or disorganized thinking.

High-yield triggers:

- Infection.
- Hypoxia.
- Metabolic disturbance.
- Medication effect or anticholinergic burden.
- Withdrawal.
- Pain.
- Sleep deprivation.
- Urinary retention or constipation.
- Recent surgery or ICU stay.

Student escalation triggers:

- New confusion or fluctuating consciousness.
- Visual hallucinations with disorientation.
- New agitation in an older or medically ill patient.
- Inattention that was not present before.
- Fever, autonomic instability, rigidity, or abnormal vitals.

Student role:

- Do a brief attention screen.
- Review vitals, labs, medications, recent PRNs, sleep, infection signs.
- Tell the resident/attending promptly.

Guideline anchor: NICE CG103 covers delirium prevention, diagnosis, and
management in hospital and long-term care. APA announced an updated delirium
practice guideline in 2025.

## Catatonia: Do Not Miss Immobility Or Excitement

Catatonia can look like withdrawal, psychosis, depression, delirium, medication
effect, or behavior. It may be hypokinetic or excited.

Look for:

- Mutism.
- Stupor or immobility.
- Posturing.
- Waxy flexibility.
- Negativism.
- Echolalia or echopraxia.
- Stereotypy or mannerisms.
- Grimacing.
- Agitation not explained by environment.
- Poor oral intake.

Red flags:

- Fever, rigidity, autonomic instability.
- Dehydration, malnutrition, immobility complications.
- Recent antipsychotic exposure with worsening rigidity or fever.

Student role:

- Describe observed motor/behavioral signs without arguing intent.
- Ask about intake, mobility, autonomic signs, and medication exposure.
- Escalate promptly.
- Do not assume "refusal" or "oppositional" behavior.

Guideline anchor: the British Association for Psychopharmacology published an
evidence-based consensus guideline for catatonia in 2023.

## Withdrawal: The First Questions

Alcohol/benzodiazepine withdrawal can be medically dangerous.

Ask:

- What substance?
- How much and how often?
- Last use?
- Prior withdrawal?
- Prior seizure or delirium tremens?
- Current tremor, sweating, anxiety, nausea, hallucinations, insomnia?
- Vital sign changes?

Alcohol withdrawal red flags:

- Prior withdrawal seizure or delirium tremens.
- Heavy daily use with recent abrupt stop.
- Autonomic instability.
- Confusion or hallucinosis.
- Severe tremor or agitation.

Opioid withdrawal is usually not life-threatening by itself but is very
distressing and changes risk, engagement, and discharge planning.

Ask:

- Last opioid use.
- Route.
- Overdose history.
- Medication treatment history.
- Current symptoms: rhinorrhea, lacrimation, yawning, GI upset, myalgias, piloerection.

Student role:

- Identify risk and escalate.
- Do not invent a withdrawal protocol.
- Know whether your unit uses CIWA-Ar, COWS, or other local tools.

Guideline anchor: ASAM alcohol withdrawal management guideline and pocket guide.

## Consult Note Skeleton

Use this structure:

1. Consult question.
2. Relevant timeline.
3. Safety and medical acuity.
4. MSE and cognitive screen.
5. Differential diagnosis.
6. Capacity/risk/diagnostic reasoning.
7. Recommendations, with what needs supervision or medical-team action.

## One-Minute Student Presentation For A Consult

> "The consult question is [specific decision/problem]. The key change is
> [timeline]. On exam, I am most concerned about [attention/catatonia/withdrawal/
> psychosis/risk]. The differential includes [top three]. The immediate safety or
> medical issue is [X]. I think we need [next step] and I want help with [specific
> uncertainty]."

## Quick Differential By Presentation

| Presentation | Must Consider |
|---|---|
| New psychosis | substance/medication, delirium, mania, trauma, primary psychotic disorder |
| Agitation | delirium, intoxication/withdrawal, mania, psychosis, pain, fear, akathisia |
| Mutism/withdrawal | catatonia, severe depression, psychosis, delirium, trauma response, neurologic illness |
| Refusal of care | capacity, fear/mistrust, psychosis, delirium, values, communication failure |
| Hallucinations | delirium, substance, psychosis, mood disorder, trauma, sensory impairment |

## Faculty Review Checklist

- Confirm local legal language for capacity and involuntary care.
- Confirm local withdrawal protocols.
- Confirm local delirium and catatonia workflows.
- Add hospital-specific paging/escalation instructions.

Plain-English note: this module teaches students what to notice, how to ask the
right question, and when to escalate. It deliberately avoids pretending students
should independently manage high-risk consults.

# Family, Discharge, And Systems Module

Generated: 2026-06-27

Audience: MS3 students.

Purpose: translate family systems, internal manuals, ED psychiatry, and discharge
materials into plain clerkship-level skills.

## Core Idea

Inpatient psychiatry does not end at symptom reduction. A patient leaves into a
system: family, housing, medications, transportation, work/school, finances,
follow-up, stigma, and safety. If the system cannot hold the plan, the plan is
not done.

## What Students Should Notice

Family/support context:

- Who noticed the change?
- Who brought the patient in?
- Who is trusted?
- Who is feared?
- Who minimizes risk?
- Who escalates conflict?
- Who can help with medication, sleep, appointments, transportation, means safety?

Discharge context:

- Where will the patient sleep?
- Who will be there?
- What follow-up is scheduled?
- What medication access exists?
- What transportation exists?
- What warning signs should trigger help?
- What crisis plan is realistic?

## Family Meeting Agenda Template

1. Purpose of meeting.
2. Patient voice: what the patient wants discussed.
3. Current clinical status in plain language.
4. Safety and warning signs.
5. Medication and treatment plan.
6. Family/support roles and limits.
7. Discharge barriers.
8. Follow-up and crisis plan.
9. What remains uncertain.
10. Next steps and who owns each task.

## Student Role In A Family Meeting

Before:

- Ask the team what your role is.
- Review the patient consent/permission plan.
- Write 2-3 questions you may ask.
- Know what not to promise.

During:

- Listen for emotion and mismatch.
- Track concrete tasks.
- Use plain language.
- Do not take sides.
- Ask permission before sensitive topics.

After:

- Summarize what changed.
- Identify unresolved barriers.
- Document only according to local policy.
- Debrief with supervisor.

## Useful Student Phrases

Validation:

> "It makes sense that everyone is exhausted and scared."

Clarifying role:

> "Our goal is not to make the family responsible for treatment. Our goal is to
> understand what support is realistic and what would be unsafe to assume."

Warning signs:

> "What did you notice before hospitalization that told you something was changing?"

Means safety:

> "Who can confirm that medications/firearms/other high-risk items are secured
> before discharge?"

Discharge realism:

> "What part of the plan feels hardest to actually do at home?"

## Discharge Barrier Map

| Domain | Question | Barrier | Plan |
|---|---|---|---|
| Safety | What could become dangerous? |  |  |
| Housing | Where will the patient sleep? |  |  |
| Medication | Can the patient obtain and take meds? |  |  |
| Follow-up | Who is seeing the patient and when? |  |  |
| Substance use | What use/withdrawal risk remains? |  |  |
| Family/support | Who can help and what are limits? |  |  |
| Transportation | Can the patient get to appointments? |  |  |
| Work/school | What role demands resume immediately? |  |  |
| Crisis plan | What happens if symptoms return? |  |  |

## Family Education Handout Use

Before giving a handout:

- Ask what the patient/family already understands.
- Ask permission to share written material.
- Choose one relevant handout, not a packet dump.

When giving it:

- Say why this handout fits.
- Highlight one section.
- Invite disagreement or questions.

After:

- Ask what felt useful or not.
- Document education only according to local policy.
- Do not substitute a handout for a conversation.

## Common Failure Modes

- Family is supportive but overwhelmed.
- Family agrees in meeting but cannot do the plan.
- Patient consents to collateral but later feels exposed.
- Team assumes housing is stable because an address exists.
- Discharge plan depends on a follow-up appointment that is not actually scheduled.
- Safety plan exists but means safety is unconfirmed.
- Patient improves on unit but sleep plan at home is unrealistic.

## Student Assignment

Using a synthetic or de-identified case:

1. Write a family meeting goal.
2. Name three questions for family/supports.
3. Fill out the discharge barrier map.
4. Identify one assumption that needs verification.
5. Write one sentence for the progress note.

Plain-English note: this module translates the larger family systems and
discharge work into specific student behaviors: ask, map, verify, and debrief.

# Treatment Basics Digest For MS3 Inpatient Psychiatry

Generated: 2026-06-27

Audience: MS3 students.

This is a conceptual guide, not a prescribing protocol. Use attending guidance,
local policy, and current references for medication choice, dosing, and monitoring.

## Treatment Planning Starts With The Formulation

For each plan item, ask:

- What problem is this treating?
- What risk does it reduce?
- What adverse effect or monitoring issue matters?
- What patient value or barrier could affect adherence?
- What happens after discharge?

## Medication Classes Students Should Recognize

| Class | Common Uses | Student Should Watch For |
|---|---|---|
| SSRIs/SNRIs | depression, anxiety, OCD, PTSD | activation, GI effects, sexual side effects, serotonin syndrome risk, mania switch |
| Antipsychotics | psychosis, mania, agitation, adjunctive mood treatment | EPS, akathisia, metabolic effects, sedation, QTc issues, NMS concern |
| Mood stabilizers | bipolar disorder, mania prevention | lithium renal/thyroid/level monitoring, valproate hepatic/platelet/teratogenic concerns |
| Benzodiazepines | acute anxiety, catatonia, withdrawal protocols | sedation, falls, respiratory risk, dependence, withdrawal |
| Stimulants/non-stimulants | ADHD | insomnia, appetite, anxiety, misuse, cardiovascular considerations |
| Sleep agents | short-term sleep support | falls, sedation, anticholinergic burden depending on agent |

## Inpatient Medication Questions

Ask:

- What has the patient tried before?
- What helped?
- What caused adverse effects?
- What is the family history of medication response?
- What medical conditions change medication choice?
- What substances could interfere?
- What monitoring is needed?
- What medication belief or fear needs to be addressed?

## Supportive Psychotherapy Basics

Students are not expected to provide independent psychotherapy. They should
practice supportive moves:

- Listen before advising.
- Name the emotion.
- Reflect the dilemma.
- Clarify the immediate problem.
- Strengthen reality testing without humiliating the patient.
- Support adaptive coping already present.
- Avoid premature reassurance.

Example:

> "Part of you wants to leave because the unit feels overwhelming, and another
> part knows things were unsafe last night. Can we slow down and look at what
> would need to be different for discharge to be safer?"

## Milieu And Behavioral Treatment

Inpatient treatment is more than medication.

Watch for:

- Sleep schedule.
- Meals and hydration.
- Group attendance.
- PRN use.
- Staff splitting or conflict.
- Isolation vs overstimulation.
- Family calls that worsen or improve regulation.
- Discharge avoidance or discharge pressure.

## Family As Treatment Context

Students should ask:

- Who noticed the change?
- Who can support safety?
- Who escalates conflict?
- Who understands the diagnosis?
- Who controls medications, transportation, housing, or follow-up?
- What does the patient want family to know?

Family involvement is not automatically good or bad. It is clinical data.

## How To Present A Treatment Rationale

Template:

> "The main target is [symptom/risk]. I think [intervention] fits because
> [formulation reason]. The main risk or adverse effect to monitor is [X].
> The discharge implication is [Y]."

Example:

> "The main target is manic insomnia and psychosis. Antipsychotic treatment fits
> because sleep loss and paranoia are driving acute risk. We should monitor
> sedation, EPS/akathisia, metabolic risk, and acceptance. The discharge
> implication is that family needs education about early warning signs and the
> patient needs rapid follow-up."

## Student Pitfalls

- Recommending a medication without naming the target symptom.
- Forgetting adverse-effect monitoring.
- Treating therapy as "referral" without specifying why.
- Ignoring substance use.
- Ignoring family beliefs about medication.
- Assuming discharge equals recovery.

## Faculty Mini-Teaching Prompts

- "What is the medication target?"
- "What adverse effect would change your plan?"
- "What non-medication intervention matters today?"
- "What would make this plan fail after discharge?"

Plain-English note: this digest gives students a safe way to discuss treatment
without pretending they are independently prescribing. It links medications,
therapy, milieu, family, and discharge back to the formulation.

# Student Documentation And Oral Presentation Guide

Generated: 2026-06-27

Audience: MS3 students.

## Core Principle

Good psychiatric documentation shows reasoning. It does not merely list symptoms.

Every note should help the next clinician answer:

1. What are we treating?
2. What else could this be?
3. What is the risk today?
4. What changed since yesterday?
5. What has to happen before discharge?

## Student Progress Note Template

### One-Liner

`[Age]-year-old [person] admitted for [reason], hospital day [X], with current
priority of [risk/diagnosis/treatment/disposition].`

### Interval Events

- Sleep.
- PRNs.
- Safety events.
- Medication changes/adverse effects.
- Nursing/milieu observations.
- Family/collateral/discharge updates.

### Subjective

Use patient language. Include:

- Mood/anxiety/psychosis/mania/trauma/substance symptoms as relevant.
- SI/HI/self-harm update.
- Medication effects/adverse effects.
- Goals or concerns for the day.

### MSE

Focus on changes and clinically relevant findings.

### Formulation Update

Use a short paragraph:

> "Today, the leading formulation remains [syndrome/problem], with [medical/
> substance/sleep] contributors, [psychological/interpersonal] contributors,
> and [discharge/system] barriers. The main change today is [X], which affects
> risk/plan by [Y]."

### Risk

Separate:

- Chronic risk.
- Acute risk.
- Protective factors.
- Modifiable targets.
- Means restriction/safety planning when relevant.

Avoid:

- "Denies SI/HI, no risk."
- Copy-forward risk language that ignores today's context.

### Plan

Organize by problem:

1. Safety.
2. Medications/medical/substance/sleep.
3. Psychotherapy/milieu/groups.
4. Family/collateral.
5. Disposition/follow-up.

## Admission Presentation Template

Use 4-6 minutes.

1. One-liner.
2. Chief concern and timeline.
3. Safety reason for admission.
4. Psychiatric symptoms by syndrome.
5. Substance/medical/medication contributors.
6. Psychiatric history.
7. Family/social/discharge context.
8. MSE.
9. Differential diagnosis.
10. Formulation and plan.

## Daily Rounds Presentation Template

Use 60-90 seconds.

1. "Hospital day X for Y."
2. Overnight: sleep, PRNs, safety, vitals/labs if relevant.
3. Patient today: one subjective point and one MSE point.
4. Risk update.
5. Plan question: medication, safety, family, discharge, or diagnosis.

Example:

> "Hospital day 4 for mania with psychosis. Slept 5 hours, no IMs, accepted
> lithium and olanzapine, mild tremor this morning. He reports thoughts are
> slower but still believes neighbors are monitoring him. MSE shows less
> pressured speech, still grandiose. Acute violence risk is lower than admission
> because sleep and agitation improved, but discharge risk remains high because
> he refuses family collateral. My question is whether today is the right time to
> revisit collateral with a limited call."

## Oral Case Presentation Rubric

| Skill | Strong Performance |
|---|---|
| Concision | Leads with the clinical problem, not every fact |
| Organization | Timeline is clear |
| MSE | Uses observable language |
| Differential | Includes medical/substance contributors |
| Risk | Separates chronic and acute risk |
| Formulation | Integrates biological, psychological, family/social, and systems factors |
| Plan | Matches the formulation |
| Uncertainty | Names what is not yet known |

## Common Student Pitfalls

- Over-presenting distant history.
- Treating "denies SI" as a full risk assessment.
- Forgetting sleep and substances.
- Forgetting collateral.
- Treating family as logistics rather than clinical context.
- Listing diagnoses without a differential.
- Copying assessment language from prior notes without updating reasoning.
- Presenting medication changes without adverse-effect monitoring.

## Handoff Template

Use when handing off a psychiatric patient:

> "This is [patient], admitted for [reason]. Tonight, the main risks are [safety/
> withdrawal/agitation/medical]. If [trigger], do [plan]. Avoid [known
> escalating intervention]. The patient responds best to [approach]. Family/
> collateral issue to know: [X]."

## Faculty Feedback Prompts

- "What was the clinical question in that presentation?"
- "What did you leave out, and why?"
- "What would make you change the risk level?"
- "Where did the formulation change the plan?"
- "What would the overnight covering clinician need to know?"

Plain-English note: this guide helps students stop writing and presenting as if
psychiatry is a checklist. It gives them a structure that reveals their thinking.

# Advanced Psychopharmacology on the Inpatient Unit
> **Review status:** AI-drafted, evidence-anchored - **pending Dr. Moss's review/attestation before learner use.**

**In one line** - On a 4-week adult inpatient rotation, your job is not to know every drug but to sequence therapy rationally, recognize when first-line trials have genuinely failed, escalate to definitive treatments without unnecessary delay, and own the safety monitoring that keeps patients alive between doses.

**Treatment-resistant depression** - "Resistance" is a measurement claim, so make it measurable: confirm adequate dose *and* adequate duration of prior trials, verify adherence, and track response with a repeated scale (PHQ-9 or QIDS) rather than gestalt. STAR*D's enduring lesson is humility - remission rates fell with each successive step and roughly a third never remitted, so neither switching nor augmenting is clearly superior; the move that helps *this* patient is empirical. Augmentation options with the strongest support include lithium, a second-generation antipsychotic (aripiprazole is the prototypical adjunct), and T3; esketamine (intranasal, REMS-restricted) and IV ketamine offer rapid effect in TRD including with acute suicidality. Do not let an algorithm delay ECT when the clinical picture demands it: severe, psychotic, or catatonic depression, food/fluid refusal, pregnancy where pharmacology is constrained, and high acute suicide risk are all settings where ECT is reasonably *first*-line, not a last resort.

**MAOIs** - They retain a niche in atypical and genuinely refractory depression, and an inpatient stay is often the safest place to start one because you control diet and timing. Counsel on tyramine restriction (aged cheeses, cured/fermented meats, tap/draft beer, soy/fermented products) to prevent a hypertensive crisis presenting as thunderclap headache and severe hypertension. Respect washout windows in both directions - a serotonergic washout before starting an MAOI (longer for fluoxetine given its long-lived metabolite) and a washout after stopping one before reintroducing serotonergic agents - to avoid serotonin syndrome. Treat the combination of MAOI plus serotonergic or sympathomimetic agents (including many OTC cold remedies and meperidine) as contraindicated.

**Bipolar maintenance** - Lithium remains the maintenance benchmark and carries a distinct anti-suicidal signal not matched by alternatives; the trade-off is a narrow therapeutic index and real organ stewardship - monitor renal function, TSH, and calcium/PTH, and remember that NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors/ARBs, and thiazides raise levels toward toxicity. Valproate is effective, particularly for mixed/rapid-cycling presentations, but is teratogenic (neural tube defects) and associated with PCOS and weight gain - generally avoid in people who can become pregnant when alternatives exist. Lamotrigine is the maintenance agent for the depressive pole; its value is gated by a slow titration to mitigate Stevens-Johnson syndrome risk, and dosing must be adjusted when combined with valproate or estrogen-containing contraceptives.

**Schizophrenia and clozapine** - Among non-clozapine antipsychotics, efficacy is broadly comparable, so select by side-effect liability: weigh metabolic burden, EPS/akathisia and tardive risk, prolactin elevation, sedation, and QTc against the individual patient. The pivotal decision is recognizing treatment-resistant schizophrenia - failure of two adequate antipsychotic trials - because clozapine is the *only* agent with demonstrated superiority in TRS and also carries an anti-suicidal indication; it is routinely under-used and started too late. Clozapine's practicalities define its use: ANC monitoring for agranulocytosis, vigilance for myocarditis (especially early), dose-related seizure risk, and severe constipation that can progress to ileus and be fatal (treat bowel regimens as non-optional). Add metabolic monitoring, manage sialorrhea, and remember the CYP1A2 interaction - smoking *induces* clearance, so abrupt smoking cessation (common at admission) can drive levels up and require dose reduction.

**Long-acting injectables and neuromodulation** - Consider an LAI when nonadherence drives relapse, when covert nonadherence is suspected, or to prevent the readmission cycle; conceptually, several second-generation agents (and a depot first-generation option) are available across multiple dosing intervals, and the inpatient stay is a natural place to initiate and bridge. For neuromodulation beyond ECT: ECT also serves as effective continuation/maintenance therapy after acute response, and rTMS is an evidence-based, non-anesthetic option for treatment-resistant *unipolar* depression in patients who can attend a daily outpatient course.

**Cross-cutting safety** - Distinguish NMS (idiosyncratic, days-long onset, lead-pipe rigidity, hyporeflexia, markedly elevated CK) from serotonin syndrome (rapid onset, *hyperreflexia* and clonus, often GI prominence) - the tempo and the neuromuscular exam separate them. Audit cumulative QTc burden when stacking agents, monitor metabolic parameters on a defined schedule for antipsychotics, hold a low threshold for suspecting lithium toxicity during any volume or interacting-drug change, and keep the high-yield CYP interactions in view (1A2 with clozapine/smoking; fluoxetine/paroxetine as 2D6 inhibitors). Peripartum: no decision is risk-free - weigh treated illness against medication exposure collaboratively, favor agents with the most reproductive-safety data, and loop in maternal-fetal and pharmacy expertise rather than defaulting to discontinuation.

**What the resident does**
- Quantify prior trials (dose, duration, adherence) before labeling depression treatment-resistant; track response with a repeated standardized scale.
- Name the failure threshold for schizophrenia (two adequate trials) and proactively raise clozapine rather than cycling further agents.
- Identify the patient for whom ECT is first-line and advocate for it without algorithm-driven delay.
- Build the monitoring scaffolding at order entry - lithium levels and renal/thyroid/PTH, clozapine ANC and bowel regimen, antipsychotic metabolic panel, QTc on stacking.
- Reconcile interaction risks at admission, especially smoking-cessation effects on clozapine and serotonergic washouts around MAOIs.

**High-yield pearls**
- Lithium and clozapine share a distinction nothing else has: a real anti-suicidal effect - let that weight your risk-benefit math.
- Clozapine-associated constipation is a lethal, under-recognized complication; a bowel regimen is part of the prescription, not an afterthought.
- Smoking cessation raises clozapine levels (CYP1A2) - the patient who quits at admission may need a dose *decrease*, not the reflexive increase.
- Tempo plus reflexes triages the hyperthermic patient: hyporeflexic and slow-onset points to NMS; hyperreflexic with clonus points to serotonin syndrome.
- Lamotrigine's benefit lives on the depressive pole and is bought with a slow titration; rushing it buys rash.
- "Failed an antidepressant" usually means underdosed or under-duration - verify before you escalate.

## Format and Methodology

This list was compiled by systematically surveying the evidence landscape across all major psychiatric subdomains, identifying papers that meet one or more of the following criteria: (1) changed clinical practice, (2) established a new treatment paradigm, (3) provided definitive evidence on a contested question, (4) introduced a foundational concept or framework, or (5) represent the best available synthesis of a critical topic. The list is weighted toward the modern era (2000-present) while including classic landmarks that remain essential knowledge. Papers are ranked #1-25 overall, then organized by subdomain with internal ranking.

## TOP 25: THE ESSENTIAL CANON

These are the 25 papers that, taken together, define the intellectual foundation of modern psychiatry. A graduating resident who deeply understands these 25 papers possesses the conceptual scaffolding for the entire field.

**1. STARD - Rush et al., "STARD: Revising Conventional Wisdom," CNS Drugs 2009 (summary of all phases)**

The largest and most influential effectiveness trial in psychiatry. Enrolled 4,041 outpatients with MDD across sequential treatment steps. Demonstrated ~33% remission with initial citalopram, declining rates with each subsequent step, and a cumulative remission of ~35-67% (depending on analytic method). Established that no single antidepressant is clearly superior, both switching and augmentation are reasonable strategies, and measurement-based care is feasible. Every treatment algorithm in depression traces back to STARD.

**2. CATIE - Lieberman et al., "Effectiveness of Antipsychotic Drugs in Patients with Chronic Schizophrenia," NEJM 2005**

The landmark NIMH-funded trial (N=1,460) that shattered the assumption of second-generation antipsychotic superiority. Found 74% discontinuation before 18 months, perphenazine comparable to most SGAs, and olanzapine modestly superior but with significant metabolic costs. Phase 2 demonstrated clozapine's superiority for treatment-resistant cases. Fundamentally changed how psychiatrists think about antipsychotic selection.

**3. Cipriani et al., "Comparative Efficacy and Acceptability of 21 Antidepressant Drugs," Lancet 2018**

The definitive network meta-analysis (522 RCTs, 116,477 participants) establishing that all 21 antidepressants are more effective than placebo, with agomelatine, escitalopram, mirtazapine, and venlafaxine among the most efficacious. The single most cited reference for antidepressant comparative effectiveness.

**4. Felitti et al., "Relationship of Childhood Abuse and Household Dysfunction to Many of the Leading Causes of Death in Adults: The ACE Study," Am J Prev Med 1998**

Surveyed 9,508 adults and demonstrated a graded dose-response relationship between adverse childhood experiences and adult disease, with >=4 ACEs conferring 4-12 increased risk for depression, suicide attempt, alcoholism, and drug abuse. Transformed understanding of the developmental origins of psychiatric illness.

**5. Kessler et al., "Lifetime Prevalence and Age-of-Onset Distributions of DSM-IV Disorders in the NCS-R," Arch Gen Psychiatry 2005**

The NCS-R (N=9,282) established that 46.4% of Americans meet criteria for a mental disorder in their lifetime, with half of all cases beginning by age 14 and three-quarters by age 24. Defined the epidemiological landscape of psychiatry and underscored the critical importance of early intervention.

**6. Kane et al., "Clozapine for the Treatment-Refractory Schizophrenic," Arch Gen Psychiatry 1988**

The pivotal trial (N=268) demonstrating clozapine's 30% response rate vs. 4% for chlorpromazine in treatment-resistant schizophrenia. Led to FDA approval and established clozapine as the gold standard for TRS - a position it has held for nearly four decades.

**7. Turner et al., "Selective Publication of Antidepressant Trials and Its Influence on Apparent Efficacy," NEJM 2008**

Compared 74 FDA-registered antidepressant trials with their published counterparts, finding that 94% of published trials appeared positive vs. 51% per FDA assessment, with 32% effect size inflation. Essential for understanding publication bias and critical appraisal of the psychiatric literature.

**8. Caspi et al., "Influence of Life Stress on Depression: Moderation by a Polymorphism in the 5-HTT Gene," Science 2003 - paired with Border et al., "No Support for Historical Candidate Gene Hypotheses for Major Depression," AJP 2019**

These two papers together tell the story of psychiatric genetics' evolution. Caspi's landmark GxE study launched a decade of candidate gene research; Border's definitive refutation (N=62,138-443,264) effectively ended the candidate gene era. Together they illustrate both the promise and the pitfalls of genetic research in psychiatry.

**9. Sachs et al., "Effectiveness of Adjunctive Antidepressant Treatment for Bipolar Depression," NEJM 2007 (STEP-BD)**

The STEP-BD antidepressant trial (N=366) found no benefit of adjunctive antidepressants for bipolar depression - durable recovery 23.5% vs. 27.3% with placebo. Changed clinical practice and guideline recommendations regarding antidepressant use in bipolar disorder.

**10. Linehan et al., "Two-Year Randomized Controlled Trial of DBT vs Therapy by Experts for Suicidal Behaviors and BPD," Arch Gen Psychiatry 2006**

The definitive DBT trial (N=101) demonstrating that DBT halved suicide attempts compared to expert community therapy in women with BPD. Established DBT as the first empirically validated treatment for BPD and the model for manualized psychotherapy research.

**11. Schizophrenia Working Group of the PGC, "Biological Insights from 108 Schizophrenia-Associated Genetic Loci," Nature 2014**

The landmark GWAS (36,989 cases, 113,075 controls) identifying 128 independent associations across 108 loci, including DRD2 and glutamatergic genes. Among the top 10 most cited NIMH-funded papers, this study validated the polygenic architecture of schizophrenia and opened the era of large-scale psychiatric genomics.

**12. Howes & Kapur, "The Dopamine Hypothesis of Schizophrenia: Version III - The Final Common Pathway," Schizophr Bull 2009**

The most cited modern formulation of the dopamine hypothesis, synthesizing >6,700 articles to propose that multiple risk factors converge on increased presynaptic striatal dopaminergic function, leading to psychosis via aberrant salience. Essential conceptual framework for understanding psychosis.

**13. Mann et al., "Suicide Prevention Strategies: A Systematic Review," JAMA 2005**

The foundational systematic review establishing that physician education in depression recognition and restricting access to lethal means are the only strategies with clear evidence of reducing suicide rates. The starting point for all evidence-based suicide prevention.

**14. Elkin et al., "NIMH Treatment of Depression Collaborative Research Program," J Consult Clin Psychol 1989**

The most influential comparative psychotherapy trial (N=239), comparing IPT, CBT, imipramine, and placebo. Established the template for psychotherapy research and sparked decades of debate about the relative efficacy of psychotherapy and pharmacotherapy.

**15. Hollon et al., "Prevention of Relapse Following Cognitive Therapy vs Medications," Arch Gen Psychiatry 2005**

Demonstrated that patients withdrawn from prior CT had 31% relapse vs. 76% for medication withdrawal, establishing the **enduring effect** of cognitive therapy. One of the most important findings in psychotherapy research - that psychotherapy teaches skills providing lasting protection.

**16. UK ECT Review Group, "Efficacy and Safety of ECT in Depressive Disorders," Lancet 2003**

The definitive meta-analysis establishing ECT's superiority over sham (SES -0.91) and pharmacotherapy (SES -0.80). The evidence base for psychiatry's most effective acute treatment for severe depression.

**17. Cipriani et al., "Lithium in the Prevention of Suicide in Mood Disorders," BMJ 2013**

Meta-analysis of 48 RCTs (6,674 participants) demonstrating lithium reduces completed suicides (OR 0.13) and deaths from any cause (OR 0.38). The strongest evidence for any pharmacological anti-suicide intervention.

**18. BALANCE Trial - Geddes et al., "Lithium Plus Valproate Combination Therapy vs Monotherapy for Bipolar I Disorder," Lancet 2010**

The definitive lithium vs. valproate comparison (N=330), establishing lithium's superiority over valproate monotherapy and supporting combination therapy. Anchors bipolar maintenance treatment guidelines worldwide.

**19. Moncrieff et al., "The Serotonin Theory of Depression: A Systematic Umbrella Review," Mol Psychiatry 2022**

The most influential recent challenge to the serotonin hypothesis, finding no consistent evidence that depression is associated with lowered serotonin. Essential for understanding the limitations of monoamine theories and the shift toward neuroplasticity frameworks.

**20. Rosenhan, "On Being Sane in Insane Places," Science 1973**

Eight pseudopatients admitted to psychiatric hospitals despite normal behavior after admission. Despite serious methodological concerns and allegations of fraud, this paper accelerated the development of DSM-III's operationalized criteria and remains essential for understanding diagnostic validity, labeling, and the history of psychiatric nosology.

**21. March et al., "Fluoxetine, CBT, and Their Combination for Adolescents with Depression (TADS)," JAMA 2004**

The landmark adolescent depression trial (N=439) establishing combination treatment (71% response) as superior to either monotherapy. The foundational reference for child/adolescent depression treatment.

**22. Whiteford et al., "Global Burden of Disease Attributable to Mental and Substance Use Disorders," Lancet 2013**

GBD 2010 data establishing mental disorders as the leading cause of years lived with disability worldwide (22.9% of all YLDs). Transformed the global health policy conversation about mental illness.

**23. Caspi et al., "Longitudinal Assessment of Mental Health Disorders Across 4 Decades (Dunedin Study)," JAMA Network Open 2020**

Across 4 decades, 86% of cohort members met criteria for a disorder at some point, with 692 unique life-history patterns among 1,037 participants. Introduced the p-factor concept and demonstrated that mental disorders are far more common, comorbid, and fluid than categorical diagnosis suggests.

**24. Anton et al., "Combined Pharmacotherapies and Behavioral Interventions for Alcohol Dependence (COMBINE)," JAMA 2006**

The definitive AUD pharmacotherapy trial (N=1,383) establishing naltrexone's efficacy and, surprisingly, acamprosate's lack of efficacy in a US population. The reference point for all AUD treatment decisions.

**25. Stanley et al., "Safety Planning Intervention With Follow-up vs Usual Care," JAMA Psychiatry 2018**

Demonstrated that the Safety Planning Intervention reduced suicidal behaviors by ~50% over 6 months. The most impactful brief intervention in suicide prevention and now standard of care.

## A Note on Scope

This list necessarily omits important papers in several areas that a comprehensive curriculum would also address, including: sleep medicine and psychiatry, consultation-liaison psychiatry (delirium management), forensic psychiatry, cultural psychiatry, telepsychiatry, and the social determinants of mental health. Several classic texts (Kraepelin's nosology, Freud's foundational works, Jaspers' General Psychopathology) are better assigned as book chapters than individual papers. The list also does not include the many excellent NEJM, JAMA, and Lancet review articles that serve as outstanding teaching tools for each disorder - these are better curated at the rotation level.

Would you like to explore any specific subdomain in greater depth, or discuss how this curriculum might be structured across the four years of residency training?

# Supervision, EPAs & Teaching the Student
> **Review status:** AI-drafted, evidence-anchored - **pending Dr. Moss's review/attestation before learner use.**

**In one line** - On adult inpatient you sit in the middle of the supervision chain: you earn graduated autonomy from your attending while becoming the first-line supervisor for the MS3 - and both directions run on the same skills of explicit expectations, active feedback, and knowing when to ask for help.

**The competency frame** - ACGME organizes residency around six core competencies (patient care, medical knowledge, practice-based learning, interpersonal/communication skills, professionalism, systems-based practice), tracked through psychiatry **Milestones** that describe behaviorally what progression from novice to ready-for-practice looks like. **Entrustable Professional Activities (EPAs)** are the unit of clinical work an attending decides you can be trusted to do - e.g., "perform and document an admission psychiatric evaluation," "manage agitation safely." Entrustment is graded: from *observe only*, to *direct supervision in the room*, to *indirect supervision* (attending available but not present), to *provide oversight to others*. Your job over a four-week block is to move along that gradient on specific activities - not vaguely "get better," but earn indirect supervision on the admission note, the family meeting, the agitation call.

**Using supervision actively** - Supervision is not a status report; it is a learning tool you drive. Come with an agenda and name your question explicitly: "I'm unsure whether this presentation is bipolar depression versus unipolar - can we reason through the discriminators?" beats "Bed 4 is the same." Tell your attending where you want autonomy and where you want eyes on you; calibrated self-disclosure of your learning edges is what lets them safely hand you more. Ask for the entrustment decision out loud: "Would you be comfortable with me running tomorrow's family meeting and you debriefing after?"

**Teaching the MS3** - On day one, orient them: physical geography of the unit, safety norms (where the exits and alarms are, never block the door, the agitation protocol), who's who, and what a good day looks like. Set explicit expectations - how to pre-round, when to present, what you want documented - and assign *real but appropriately scoped* responsibility (own 1-2 patients, draft the note, call collateral). When they present, use the **One-Minute Preceptor**: (1) get a commitment ("What do you think is going on?"), (2) probe for supporting reasoning ("What led you there?"), (3) teach one general principle, (4) reinforce what was done well, (5) correct errors specifically. Model the interview and the difficult conversation rather than only describing them - students learn the mental status exam and de-escalation by watching you do them.

**Feedback that lands** - Make it specific, behavior-based, timely, and actionable; "your suicide risk assessment didn't document protective factors or access to means - let's add those two next time" teaches, "good job" does not. Use **Ask-Tell-Ask**: ask for their self-assessment, tell them your observation and one concrete change, then ask them to plan how they'll apply it. Keep *feedback* (formative, in-the-moment, for growth) distinct from *evaluation* (summative, on the record) - and flag clearly when you're shifting registers. The same model works on the receiving end: solicit feedback on yourself the same way you give it.

**Running the team** - Lead rounds with a predictable structure and a clock; protect time for the sickest and don't let a stable patient consume ten minutes. Use **closed-loop communication** for anything safety-critical - orders, precaution changes, hand-offs read back and confirmed. Build **psychological safety**: the unit functions when nurses, the student, and you can voice a concern or a near-miss without fear, so explicitly invite dissent and thank people for catching things.

**Supervision for the harder parts** - Bring countertransference and difficult cases to supervision by name: the patient who makes you angry, anxious, or want to discharge early is clinical data, not a personal failing. Inpatient psychiatry carries real emotional load; watch yourself for burnout's signs - exhaustion, cynicism/depersonalization, a sense of ineffectiveness - and treat them as a reason to act, not push through. **Asking for help is a competency** (it lives under professionalism and practice-based learning), not a weakness; the unsafe resident is the one who doesn't escalate.

**Documentation & the medicolegal frame** - When you supervise the student, the attending remains the responsible physician and ultimately accountable; your oversight is real but operates under their license. Note co-signature requirements where they apply, document supervision and your own independent assessment rather than simply endorsing a student's note, and remember that "agree with above" over an exam you didn't perform is both a quality and a liability problem.

**What the resident does**
- Negotiate explicit entrustment with the attending: name which EPAs you want to advance and what level of supervision you're asking for.
- Drive each supervision session with a prepared agenda and a named clinical question.
- Orient the MS3 on day one to safety, expectations, and scope; assign real but bounded ownership.
- Teach at the bedside with the One-Minute Preceptor; give feedback via Ask-Tell-Ask.
- Run rounds on a structure and a clock; use closed-loop communication for all safety-critical items.
- Bring countertransference, stuck cases, and your own warning signs to supervision proactively.
- Document your independent assessment and supervision; honor co-signature and attending-of-record rules.

**High-yield pearls**
- "Get a commitment" before you correct - let the learner reason first, then teach to the gap.
- Feedback describes a *behavior* and a *next action*; if it could fit any learner, it's too vague.
- Separate feedback from evaluation out loud - surprise at the summative review is a supervision failure.
- Entrustment is earned by accurate self-disclosure: telling your attending what you *can't* yet do safely is what unlocks autonomy.
- Burnout is a signal to escalate, not a character flaw; the same is true for the student you supervise.
- The attending is the physician of record - your supervision adds a layer, it doesn't transfer the accountability.

# Inpatient Systems & Med-Legal
> **Review status:** AI-drafted, evidence-anchored - **pending Dr. Moss's review/attestation before learner use.**

**In one line** - on the adult inpatient unit your documentation, your handling of voluntary/involuntary status, and your disposition planning are clinical acts with legal weight; do them deliberately, and verify every statute-specific detail against current Maine law (Title 34-B) and Maine Medical Center policy before you act on it.

**Why this matters on the unit** - Inpatient psychiatry is where clinical judgment, the law, and the health system collide most visibly. You will hold people against their will, medicate over objection in narrow circumstances, restrain patients in emergencies, and decide where they go next - each step generating a record that an attorney, a surveyor, a payer, or a future treater may read. The skills below are systems-based practice and medical-legal literacy as much as psychiatry. Treat the legal specifics in this page as orientation, not authority: laws vary by state, Maine's procedures live in Title 34-B and are operationalized by hospital policy, and both change. When a real decision is in front of you, confirm the current timeline, form, and process with your attending, the social work/utilization team, and risk management.

**Documentation as a clinical and legal instrument** - Your admission H&P anchors the stay: presenting problem and timeline, psychiatric and substance history, medical comorbidity and reconciled medications, collateral, MSE, a formulation, and an initial risk and disposition plan. Daily progress notes are problem-oriented - interval events, exam changes, response to treatment, and an updated plan - not a copy-forward. Document risk explicitly: not just a screening score but your suicide and violence assessment and the reasoning behind your level-of-observation and intervention decisions (most patients who die by suicide are rated low risk at last contact, so the formulation, not the number, is what protects the patient and you). Capacity assessments are documented by the four abilities (understand, appreciate, reason, express a choice) tied to the specific decision. Restraint and seclusion require a time-limited order, the required face-to-face evaluation and monitoring, and a debrief, all charted per policy. Billing/E&M follows either total time or medical decision-making complexity - and the deeper point is that good documentation is simultaneously communication to the next treater and your medical-legal protection. If it isn't written, for these purposes it didn't happen.

**Voluntary vs. involuntary admission** - Most patients should be admitted voluntarily under the least-restrictive-alternative principle whenever they can engage. When someone meets criteria for emergency involuntary hospitalization, Maine commonly uses what clinicians call the "blue paper" - an application for emergency involuntary admission, paired with a supporting clinician certificate/examination. That emergency hold is the entry point, not the endpoint: there is a subsequent certification and judicial commitment pathway with defined patient rights (notice, hearing, counsel, periodic review). **The specific certifiers, timelines, forms, and hearing windows must be verified against current Maine statute (Title 34-B) and MMC policy - do not rely on remembered numbers, and ask the on-call attending and social work how the current process runs.** Throughout, the operating rule is least restrictive: hold only as long and as tightly as safety requires, and move to voluntary status as soon as the patient can participate.

**Capacity, competency, and medication over objection** - Capacity is a clinical, decision-specific determination you make at the bedside; competency is a legal status a court adjudicates. A patient can lack capacity for one decision and retain it for another, and a psychiatric diagnosis alone never settles it. Informed consent requires disclosing the nature, risks, benefits, and alternatives of treatment and confirming the patient can use that information. Critically, an involuntary hold is not the same as authorization to medicate over objection: forcing non-emergency medication on a refusing patient generally requires a separate legal or administrative process distinct from the commitment itself. Verify the local Maine/MMC pathway for involuntary medication before treating over objection - and document the emergency rationale clearly when you medicate acutely to prevent imminent harm.

**Duty to protect, mandatory reporting, and confidentiality** - When a patient makes a credible threat against an identifiable person, the Tarasoff lineage may create a duty to protect (which can include warning, notifying police, hospitalizing, or otherwise intervening) - but the precise trigger and permitted actions are state-specific, so confirm Maine's standard and loop in your attending and risk management before acting. You are also a mandated reporter for suspected abuse or neglect of children, elders, and dependent/vulnerable adults; the reporting thresholds, agencies, and timeframes vary by state and must be verified locally. None of this dissolves the default of confidentiality - disclosures should be the narrowest needed to meet the legal duty or protect safety.

**Disposition and systems-based practice** - Discharge planning starts at admission. Match the patient to the least restrictive level of care that is safe: inpatient -> partial hospitalization (PHP) / intensive outpatient (IOP) -> residential -> assertive community treatment (ACT) or community case management -> supportive housing or group home -> routine outpatient. Reconcile medications, schedule a concrete first appointment (not just a phone number - scheduling itself raises follow-up rates), and do a warm handoff to the receiving team; aim for follow-up within 7 days given the elevated post-discharge suicide risk. Expect utilization-review and payer pressure on length of stay, and know the hard dispositions that consume inpatient days: homelessness, the need for guardianship, patients leaving against medical advice (AMA), and psychiatric boarding while awaiting placement. Working these systemic barriers - not just the symptoms - is core to the role.

**What the resident does**
- Writes the admission H&P and problem-oriented daily notes; documents explicit risk reasoning, not just scores.
- Completes and charts four-abilities capacity assessments tied to the specific decision at hand.
- Initiates and correctly documents emergency involuntary holds (the "blue paper" + clinician certificate), then tracks the certification/commitment timeline with social work - verifying current Maine/MMC procedure each time.
- Writes time-limited restraint/seclusion orders and ensures the required monitoring, face-to-face evaluation, and debrief are documented.
- Recognizes when medication over objection requires a separate legal process and routes it correctly rather than treating under the hold alone.
- Identifies duty-to-protect and mandatory-reporting situations early and escalates to the attending and risk management.
- Drives disposition: reconciles meds, secures a dated follow-up appointment, does the warm handoff, and problem-solves boarding, housing, guardianship, and AMA scenarios.

**High-yield pearls**
- "Hold" = "treat" - an involuntary admission does not by itself authorize involuntary medication; that almost always needs its own legal/administrative process. Verify Maine/MMC.
- Capacity is clinical and decision-specific; competency is legal and court-determined. Diagnosis alone never decides either.
- Document the *reasoning* behind risk and observation level - a number without a formulation protects no one, since most who die by suicide screen low-risk.
- Least restrictive alternative is the through-line: in level of care, in voluntary-vs-involuntary status, and in restraint/seclusion.
- Schedule the actual follow-up appointment before discharge and target <=7 days - the post-discharge window is the highest-risk period for suicide.
- Statute specifics (timelines, certifiers, form names) vary by state and change - never quote a remembered number; confirm current Title 34-B and hospital policy.
