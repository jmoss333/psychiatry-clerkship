# Case Formulation on the Inpatient Unit

**In one line.** A formulation answers *why this patient, why now,* and connects the answer to a plan. This page uses the four Ps to organize factors by their role in the episode. The [Formulation & DDx pocket guide](?page=pg_formulation.md) offers a companion four-layer approach for rounds.

**Differential vs. formulation.** The differential asks "what could this be?" and yields a diagnosis. The formulation asks "why did this person develop this, at this moment, and what does that imply?" A student who stops at "major depressive disorder" has named the what; the formulation names the *why* that the treatment and disposition actually hang on. On inpatient psychiatry, the formulation is what separates a problem list from a plan.

**Two frameworks that fit together.** Build the formulation on the **biopsychosocial** axis — biological, psychological, and social contributors — and sharpen it with the **four P's**, which sort every contributor by *timing and function*:
- **Predisposing** — what made this person vulnerable (genetics, temperament, early trauma, chronic illness).
- **Precipitating** — what triggered *this* episode *now* (a medication stop, a loss, a substance, a role transition).
- **Perpetuating** — what keeps it going (ongoing substance use, high-expressed-emotion home, untreated pain, hopelessness, nonadherence).
- **Protective** — strengths to build on (supports, treatment engagement, responsibilities, insight).

Read across each row to consider biological, psychological, and social factors. The same fact can serve two roles. If the history leaves a gap, name what you would ask.

**A worked example (fictional composite).** A 34-year-old man with bipolar I disorder is admitted for mania. He lost his insurance three weeks ago and stopped taking lithium because he could no longer obtain it. He uses cannabis nightly. When he felt well, he said, "I'm fine, I don't need medication." His marriage is strained, and his spouse is willing to attend a family meeting.

**MS3 prompt.** Give a one-sentence formulation for rounds. What changed three weeks ago? What support could help now? Name one action the team could take today and the case detail that makes it worth doing.

**One formulation for rounds.** "Losing access to lithium likely contributed to this manic episode in a man with bipolar I, and his spouse's willingness to meet gives us a chance, with his permission, to plan how he'll get his medication."

Losing coverage helps explain *why now*. If he still cannot obtain medication, the same barrier also matters for follow-up. Ask which fill he can actually pick up. His comment about medication when well is worth exploring separately. He gave loss of access as his reason for stopping this time.

**Resident follow-up.** His coverage is reinstated, but his spouse can no longer attend the meeting. Revise your formulation in one sentence. Which part of today's plan would you change first, and what would you verify before relying on the revised plan?

"Losing coverage likely contributed to the episode, and although coverage is back, we still need to confirm he can get the medication and find out what support he wants now that his spouse cannot attend."

First, confirm with him and the pharmacist what the next fill will cost and whether he can collect it. Ask what benefit or burden he noticed on lithium when he felt well. A missed meeting leaves his spouse's wider role open. If he wants his spouse involved, ask whether another time or a phone call would work.

**What the student does.**
- Write one formulation sentence for every patient you follow, in the four-P structure — it is the single highest-yield habit for rounds and oral presentations.
- Ask what changed before admission and what helps the patient stay well. If either is unclear, tell the team what you would ask next.
- Let the formulation drive the plan: each perpetuating factor should map to an intervention, each protective factor to a lever.
- Revise it as collateral and the course change — a formulation is a working hypothesis, not a fixed label.

**High-yield pearls.**
- The differential gives the diagnosis; the formulation gives the "why now" and the plan.
- The four P's — predisposing, precipitating, perpetuating, protective — are the fastest scaffold; the precipitating and protective cells are the ones students forget.
- Ask about psychological and social contributors alongside biological ones. Record what is still unknown.
- Every perpetuating factor is a treatment target; every protective factor is a lever — that is how the formulation becomes a plan.
