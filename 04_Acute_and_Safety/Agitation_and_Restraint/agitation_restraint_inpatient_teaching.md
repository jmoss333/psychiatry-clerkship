# Agitation & Restraint on the Inpatient Unit

> **Review status:** Reviewed and attested by Joshua Moss, MD (2026-07-09). Educational; fictional composites only, no PHI.

## In one line

Agitation is a symptom with a cause, not a diagnosis. Your job is to manage it with the least-restrictive intervention that actually works, while keeping the patient, the other patients, and the staff safe. Everything below flows from that single sentence: find the driver, climb the ladder one rung at a time, and never reach for the top rung when a lower one would do.

## Find the driver

Treating the cause is part of managing the behavior, not a separate task you get to later. Before you decide what to do, ask what is producing this. The usual drivers are delirium (think medical until proven otherwise, especially in older or medically ill patients), intoxication or withdrawal, an acute psychotic or manic process, akathisia (a medication-induced inner restlessness that is easy to mistake for "the patient being difficult"), unrelieved pain, and a fear or trauma response to an environment that feels unsafe or coercive. A patient who is frightened needs something different from a patient who is in withdrawal, even if the surface behavior looks similar. Naming the likely driver out loud changes the whole plan.

## The least-restrictive ladder

Climb in order, and document why you moved up.

1. **Environment and milieu.** Reduce stimulation, give space, lower the noise, and remove triggers and available means. A quieter hallway and fewer people in the room often de-escalate before anyone says a word.
2. **Verbal de-escalation, first.** This is the default response, not a courtesy step. The Project BETA de-escalation framework (Richmond et al. 2012) centers respect, simple boundaries, listening, and offering choices rather than issuing commands.
3. **Offered, collaborative PRN medication.** Ask the patient, explain why, and let them participate in the choice when at all possible.
4. **Involuntary medication.** Reserved for imminent danger to the patient or others — not for refusal alone, and not for convenience.
5. **Physical restraint or seclusion.** The genuine last resort, only when lesser measures have failed or are clearly inadequate to prevent imminent harm.

## Medication principles

Treat the suspected cause. Prefer the oral, collaborative route over an intramuscular injection whenever the situation allows it — an offered medication the patient accepts is both safer and more therapeutic than a forced one. Avoid benzodiazepines in delirium and in older adults, where they tend to worsen confusion — except in alcohol or sedative withdrawal and in catatonia, where they are the treatment. Use caution when combining agents, and watch closely for over-sedation, which carries its own airway and aspiration risks (Wilson et al. 2012). Do not memorize specific drugs or doses from a teaching page — defer to your institution's agitation order set and your supervising clinician for what to give and how much.

## Restraint and seclusion

Restraint and seclusion are safety interventions, never treatments. They do not fix the underlying problem; they buy time. They require a time-limited physician order, close and continuous monitoring, and discontinuation the moment the patient is safe — not the end of a shift. A post-event debrief with the team (and, where appropriate, the patient) is part of the standard, not an extra. These practices are governed by CMS and Joint Commission standards, and the standards are specific rather than aspirational: for restraint or seclusion used for **violent or self-destructive behaviour**, the CMS Conditions of Participation (42 CFR §482.13(e)) require a **face-to-face** (in-person) evaluation by a physician/LIP or trained RN **within 1 hour** of initiation, cap a single order at **4 hours for adults 18 and over** (shorter for minors) with renewal only after re-evaluation, and **prohibit PRN and standing restraint orders** outright. The cap is a ceiling, not an allowance. Non-violent restraint used for medical purposes — the soft wrist restraint protecting a line — runs on a different rule set with no 1-hour face-to-face requirement, so do not carry the behavioural numbers onto a medical floor. They also carry real physical risk (injury, aspiration) and psychological risk (re-traumatization). Apply trauma-informed care throughout, and stay alert to the documented inequities in who gets restrained and how often: Black patients face roughly **1.85× the adjusted odds of a restraint event** on inpatient psychiatric units (Singal et al., *Psychiatric Services* 2024), and higher adjusted odds of physical and chemical restraint during emergency psychiatric evaluation in the ED as well (aOR ~1.35; Smith et al., *Psychiatric Services* 2022) — that disparity is a quality-of-care issue, not a footnote.

## What the student does

- Observe a real de-escalation and notice the techniques: tone, distance, the offering of choices.
- Never position yourself between the patient and the exit, and always know where your own exit is.
- Help the team identify the driver — pull recent vitals, labs, the medication and PRN history, and intake.
- Flag akathisia and possible delirium early; both are easy to miss and change the whole plan.
- Participate in the post-event debrief and listen for what could have prevented escalation.

## High-yield pearls

- Agitation is a symptom — always ask "what is driving this?" before "what do we give?"
- Verbal de-escalation is the default first move, not a nicety; medication is not automatically next.
- Benzodiazepines worsen delirium and are risky in older adults — match the agent to the cause.
- Restraint and seclusion are time-limited safety measures, never treatments, and demand monitoring plus a debrief.
- Watch yourself: stay near the door, never block the patient's exit, and call for help early.
