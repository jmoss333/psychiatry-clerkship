## 1. `qbx_mood_001` — Screen for prior mania before starting an antidepressant
**Category:** mood · **Type:** sba · **Intended difficulty:** standard (2) · **Exam:** both · **Setting:** ambulatory · **Age:** adult-18-64

**NBME:** Behavioral Health · Diagnosis · Ambulatory · 13 and older  
**COMAT:** Depressive/Bipolar/Related · History & Physical (incl. Dx)

**Learning objective:** Before treating an apparent depressive episode, establish whether the lifetime course is unipolar or bipolar, because antidepressant monotherapy can be harmful in bipolar disorder.  
**Tested decision:** The best next step before prescribing pharmacotherapy for a depressive episode with bipolar red flags.

**Vignette.** A 24-year-old woman presents to an outpatient clinic with 3 weeks of low mood, anhedonia, hypersomnia, and poor concentration. She has had two similar episodes since age 17. Her mother has bipolar I disorder. She recalls that an antidepressant started 2 years ago made her feel, for about a week, unusually energetic and talkative with a reduced need for sleep, after which it was stopped. She takes no medications now and denies current mania. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step before starting pharmacotherapy?

| | Option | |
|---|---|---|
| A | Start a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor and reassess in 4 weeks _(trap: Antidepressant monotherapy in unrecognized bipolar)_ |  |
| B | Take a structured history for prior manic or hypomanic episodes to clarify unipolar versus bipolar illness | ✅ **KEY** |
| C | Start a serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor because two prior episodes suggest recurrent unipolar depression _(trap: Recurrence = unipolar)_ |  |
| D | Order thyroid studies and defer any diagnostic reassessment until results return _(trap: Workup as a substitute for the interview)_ |  |

**Correct answer: B.**

**Why (concise):** The prior antidepressant-associated week of decreased need for sleep, increased energy, and pressured speech is a probable hypomanic/manic switch, and a first-degree relative has bipolar I — so 'antidepressant monotherapy in unrecognized bipolar' is the trap to avoid. 'Recurrence = unipolar' ignores that bipolar depression recurs, and a lab 'workup as a substitute for the interview' skips the decisive step. The history of past (hypo)mania is what distinguishes safe from harmful pharmacotherapy.

**Full explanation:** The single most consequential branch point in treating a depressive presentation is unipolar versus bipolar, because antidepressant monotherapy in bipolar disorder can precipitate mania or mixed states and accelerate cycling. This patient has two bipolar red flags — early onset with a probable antidepressant-induced hypomanic episode (a week of decreased need for sleep, increased energy, talkativeness) and a first-degree relative with bipolar I. The correct next step is to take a careful history for lifetime manic/hypomanic episodes (DSM-5-TR criteria) before choosing a drug. If bipolarity is confirmed, first-line treatment is a mood stabilizer or a second-generation antipsychotic, not an antidepressant alone.

**Why each distractor is wrong:**
- **A.** Starting an SSRI now is precisely the move that risks a manic switch in an undiagnosed bipolar patient; the diagnostic question must be resolved first.
- **C.** Two prior episodes do not establish unipolar illness — bipolar depression is also recurrent, and this patient carries specific bipolar risk markers.
- **D.** Thyroid studies are reasonable adjuncts but do not address the decisive unipolar-versus-bipolar question and should not delay the history.

**Key takeaway:** Rule out bipolarity by history before starting an antidepressant for depression.  
**Common reasoning error:** Treating the presenting depression at face value without screening the longitudinal course for mania.

**Safety/management caveat:** If bipolar depression is confirmed, avoid antidepressant monotherapy; use a mood stabilizer or SGA per guideline.

**Review flags:** medication, higher-intensity-review

- [⧗ confirm] Yatham LN, Kennedy SH, Parikh SV, et al. CANMAT and ISBD 2018 guidelines for the management of patients with bipolar disorder. Bipolar Disord. 2018;20(2):97-170. DOI 10.1111/bdi.12609

> ⚠ **Content note:** References suicidal ideation and its monitoring.

**Learning objective:** Select evidence-based first-line treatment for moderate adolescent major depressive disorder and attach appropriate safety monitoring.  
**Tested decision:** The most appropriate initial treatment for a moderately depressed adolescent.

**Vignette.** A 15-year-old boy is brought by his mother to a pediatric clinic with 2 months of persistent sadness, loss of interest in soccer, declining grades, and social withdrawal. He has no manic history, no active suicidal ideation, and no prior treatment. His safety plan is in place and his mother is engaged. Which of the following is the most appropriate initial treatment?

| | Option | |
|---|---|---|
| A | Paroxetine plus supportive check-ins in 8 weeks _(trap: Wrong SSRI in adolescents)_ |  |
| B | Bupropion monotherapy _(trap: Off-first-line antidepressant)_ |  |
| C | Fluoxetine combined with cognitive behavioral therapy | ✅ **KEY** |
| D | Watchful waiting with no active treatment for 3 months _(trap: Undertreating moderate depression)_ |  |

**Correct answer: C.**

**Why (concise):** For moderate adolescent MDD, the best-supported plan pairs an SSRI with the strongest pediatric evidence (fluoxetine) and CBT, with early monitoring for emergent suicidality. 'Wrong SSRI in adolescents' (paroxetine) and an 'off-first-line antidepressant' (bupropion) are the pharmacologic traps; 'undertreating moderate depression' with watchful waiting leaves impairing illness untreated.

**Full explanation:** Combination treatment with fluoxetine plus cognitive behavioral therapy has the best evidence for moderate-to-severe adolescent major depression, improving response over either alone. Because antidepressants carry a boxed warning for increased suicidal ideation/behavior in patients under 25, the plan must include close monitoring, especially in the first weeks and after dose changes, together with family engagement and a safety plan.

**Why each distractor is wrong:**
- **A.** Paroxetine is specifically avoided in youth because of an unfavorable benefit–risk profile; it is not the SSRI of choice here.
- **B.** Bupropion is not first-line for adolescent depression and lacks the pediatric evidence base the SSRIs have.
- **D.** Watchful waiting is appropriate only for mild, brief presentations; this functionally impairing episode warrants active treatment.

**Key takeaway:** Combine fluoxetine with CBT for moderate adolescent depression and monitor closely early in treatment.  
**Common reasoning error:** Choosing any antidepressant without recognizing that agent selection and suicidality monitoring differ in youth.

**Safety/management caveat:** Antidepressants carry a boxed warning for suicidal ideation in patients under 25; monitor closely and involve the family.

## 3. `qbx_psy_001` — Schizophreniform vs schizophrenia — the duration criterion
**Category:** psychosis · **Type:** sba · **Intended difficulty:** foundational (1) · **Exam:** shelf · **Setting:** ambulatory · **Age:** adult-18-64

**NBME:** Behavioral Health · Diagnosis · Ambulatory · 13 and older  
**COMAT:** Schizophrenia Spectrum/Psychotic · History & Physical (incl. Dx)

**Learning objective:** Apply the DSM-5-TR duration thresholds that separate brief psychotic disorder, schizophreniform disorder, and schizophrenia.  
**Tested decision:** The most likely diagnosis given the total duration of continuous illness.

**Vignette.** A 20-year-old man is evaluated in clinic for 3 months of continuous delusions of persecution, auditory hallucinations, and disorganized speech, with a decline in self-care and school performance. There is no substance use, no mood episode, and a normal medical workup. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?

| | Option | |
|---|---|---|
| A | Brief psychotic disorder _(trap: Ignoring the 1-month floor)_ |  |
| B | Schizophrenia _(trap: Overshooting the 6-month threshold)_ |  |
| C | Schizophreniform disorder | ✅ **KEY** |
| D | Schizoaffective disorder _(trap: Diagnosing mood where there is none)_ |  |

**Correct answer: C.**

**Why (concise):** Continuous psychosis of 3 months, with mood and substance/medical causes excluded, fits schizophreniform disorder (1–6 months). 'Ignoring the 1-month floor' overcalls brief psychotic disorder, 'overshooting the 6-month threshold' prematurely calls schizophrenia, and 'diagnosing mood where there is none' misreads schizoaffective disorder, which needs a concurrent mood episode.

**Full explanation:** The schizophrenia-spectrum disorders are separated largely by duration once substance-induced and mood-related psychoses are excluded. Brief psychotic disorder lasts at least 1 day but fully remits within 1 month; schizophreniform disorder spans 1 to 6 months; schizophrenia requires at least 6 months of continuous disturbance (including prodromal/residual signs) with at least 1 month of active symptoms. At 3 months with no mood episode and a negative medical/substance workup, this is schizophreniform disorder — a provisional diagnosis that converts to schizophrenia if signs persist beyond 6 months.

**Why each distractor is wrong:**
- **A.** Brief psychotic disorder must remit within 1 month; 3 months rules it out.
- **B.** Schizophrenia requires ≥6 months of continuous signs, which have not yet elapsed.
- **D.** Schizoaffective disorder requires a concurrent major mood episode, which is absent here.

**Key takeaway:** 1–6 months of primary psychosis is schizophreniform disorder.  
**Common reasoning error:** Anchoring on symptom severity rather than the duration criterion that defines the specific diagnosis.

**Safety/management caveat:** Schizophreniform is provisional; reassess at 6 months and confirm substance/medical causes remain excluded.

**Review flags:** none

**References:**

## 4. `qbx_psy_002` — Clozapine for treatment-resistant schizophrenia
**Category:** psychosis · **Type:** two-tier · **Intended difficulty:** standard (2) · **Exam:** both · **Setting:** ambulatory · **Age:** adult-18-64

**NBME:** Behavioral Health · Pharmacotherapy/Intervention/Management · Ambulatory · 13 and older  
**COMAT:** Schizophrenia Spectrum/Psychotic · Management

**Learning objective:** Recognize treatment-resistant schizophrenia (failure of two adequate antipsychotic trials) and identify clozapine as the indicated next agent, understanding why.  
**Tested decision:** The next pharmacologic step after two adequate failed antipsychotic trials.

**Vignette.** A 29-year-old man with schizophrenia has completed two separate antipsychotic trials — risperidone and then olanzapine — each at an adequate dose for 6 weeks with documented adherence, yet he continues to have persistent hallucinations and functional impairment. Which of the following is the most appropriate next pharmacologic step?

| | Option | |
|---|---|---|
| A | Combine risperidone and olanzapine at full doses _(trap: Antipsychotic polypharmacy reflex)_ |  |
| B | Start clozapine with the required absolute neutrophil count monitoring | ✅ **KEY** |
| C | Add a benzodiazepine to control the psychosis _(trap: The reflexive benzodiazepine)_ |  |
| D | Try a third first-line second-generation antipsychotic before considering clozapine _(trap: Delaying clozapine)_ |  |

**Correct answer: B.**

**Why (concise):** Two adequate antipsychotic trials have failed, meeting the definition of treatment-resistant schizophrenia, so clozapine (with ANC monitoring) is indicated. The 'antipsychotic polypharmacy reflex' and 'the reflexive benzodiazepine' are not evidence-based resistance strategies, and 'delaying clozapine' for a third ordinary agent withholds the only drug with demonstrated superiority in this setting.

**Full explanation:** Treatment-resistant schizophrenia is defined as persistent symptoms despite two adequate trials (dose and duration) of different antipsychotics. Clozapine is the only agent with proven superior efficacy in this population and is the guideline-indicated next step, accepting mandatory absolute neutrophil count monitoring because of agranulocytosis risk. Neither antipsychotic combinations nor benzodiazepines have efficacy evidence for resistant psychosis, and adding a third routine antipsychotic simply delays effective treatment.

**Why each distractor is wrong:**
- **A.** Antipsychotic polypharmacy is not supported for treatment resistance and increases adverse effects.
- **C.** Benzodiazepines do not treat primary psychosis and are not an antipsychotic strategy.
- **D.** The two-failed-trial criterion for resistance is already met; a third routine agent delays clozapine without evidence of benefit.

**Tier 2 — What is the best reason clozapine is the correct choice here?**
- A. It is the only antipsychotic with demonstrated superior efficacy specifically in treatment-resistant schizophrenia ✅
- B. Combining two antipsychotics is generally more effective than any single agent
- C. Benzodiazepines potentiate antipsychotic effect on hallucinations
- D. A third second-generation antipsychotic is statistically likely to succeed where two failed

_Tier-2 rationale:_ The rationale is efficacy in resistance specifically: clozapine uniquely outperforms other agents once two adequate trials have failed. The distractor rationales mirror the tier-1 traps — polypharmacy superiority, benzodiazepine potentiation, and the low-yield third routine agent — none of which is supported.

**Key takeaway:** Clozapine is the indicated agent for treatment-resistant schizophrenia.  
**Common reasoning error:** Cycling through additional ordinary antipsychotics or combinations instead of moving to clozapine once resistance is established.

**Safety/management caveat:** Clozapine requires ANC monitoring for agranulocytosis and carries risks of myocarditis, seizures, metabolic effects, and severe constipation.

**Review flags:** medication, higher-intensity-review

## 5. `qbx_anx_001` — PTSD vs acute stress disorder — the 1-month line
**Category:** anxiety · **Type:** sba · **Intended difficulty:** foundational (1) · **Exam:** shelf · **Setting:** ambulatory · **Age:** adult-18-64

**NBME:** Behavioral Health · Diagnosis · Ambulatory · 13 and older  
**COMAT:** Anxiety/Trauma/Dissociative/OCD · History & Physical (incl. Dx)

> ⚠ **Content note:** References a traumatic event.

**Learning objective:** Differentiate acute stress disorder from posttraumatic stress disorder by symptom duration after a qualifying trauma.  
**Tested decision:** The most likely diagnosis given symptom duration after a traumatic event.

**Vignette.** A 32-year-old woman presents to clinic 10 weeks after a serious motor-vehicle collision. Since the crash she has had intrusive memories and nightmares, avoids driving, feels persistently on edge and hypervigilant, and startles easily; these symptoms have continued throughout the interval and impair her work. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?

| | Option | |
|---|---|---|
| A | Acute stress disorder _(trap: Missing the 1-month ceiling)_ |  |
| B | Adjustment disorder with anxiety _(trap: Downgrading trauma to a stressor)_ |  |
| C | Generalized anxiety disorder _(trap: Ignoring the traumatic anchor)_ |  |
| D | Posttraumatic stress disorder | ✅ **KEY** |

**Correct answer: D.**

**Why (concise):** Trauma-linked intrusion, avoidance, and hyperarousal persisting beyond 1 month is PTSD. 'Missing the 1-month ceiling' overcalls acute stress disorder, 'downgrading trauma to a stressor' misassigns adjustment disorder, and 'ignoring the traumatic anchor' misreads GAD, whose worry is not trauma-cued.

**Full explanation:** Acute stress disorder and PTSD share trauma-related intrusion, avoidance, negative cognitions/mood, and arousal symptoms; they are distinguished by timing. Acute stress disorder is diagnosed from 3 days to 1 month after the event; when the full symptom picture persists beyond 1 month, the diagnosis is PTSD. At 10 weeks with continuous, impairing trauma symptoms, this is PTSD.

**Why each distractor is wrong:**
- **A.** Acute stress disorder is time-limited to the first month; symptoms at 10 weeks exceed that window.
- **B.** Adjustment disorder does not fit a Criterion-A trauma with the classic re-experiencing/avoidance/hyperarousal cluster.
- **C.** GAD is pervasive worry across life domains, not event-cued re-experiencing and avoidance.

**Key takeaway:** Trauma-related symptoms lasting more than 1 month are PTSD.  
**Common reasoning error:** Defaulting to 'acute stress disorder' for any post-trauma presentation without checking duration.

**Safety/management caveat:** Screen for co-occurring depression, substance use, and suicide risk in PTSD.

**Review flags:** none

## 7. `qbx_sud_001` — Thiamine before glucose in the malnourished patient
**Category:** substance · **Type:** two-tier · **Intended difficulty:** advanced (3) · **Exam:** comat · **Setting:** emergency-department · **Age:** adult-18-64

**NBME:** Behavioral Health · Pharmacotherapy/Intervention/Management · Emergency Department · 13 and older  
**COMAT:** Substance-Related/Addictive · Management · **Osteopathic:** Recognizing nutritional and metabolic vulnerability as part of whole-person emergency care.

**Learning objective:** Prevent Wernicke encephalopathy by giving thiamine before or with glucose in a malnourished patient with alcohol use disorder.  
**Tested decision:** The correct sequence of thiamine and glucose administration in a malnourished, altered patient.

**Vignette.** A disheveled 52-year-old man with a long history of heavy alcohol use is brought to the emergency department confused and unsteady. He appears malnourished. A fingerstick glucose is 44 mg/dL. Which of the following is the most appropriate immediate management?

| | Option | |
|---|---|---|
| A | Administer thiamine together with (or before) intravenous dextrose | ✅ **KEY** |
| B | Administer intravenous dextrose alone and recheck the neurologic exam _(trap: Glucose before thiamine)_ |  |
| C | Give lorazepam for presumed alcohol withdrawal before anything else _(trap: It's just withdrawal)_ |  |
| D | Obtain a head CT before providing any treatment _(trap: Imaging before resuscitation)_ |  |

**Correct answer: A.**

**Why (concise):** In a malnourished patient with alcohol use, thiamine must accompany or precede dextrose because a glucose load can consume marginal thiamine stores and trigger Wernicke encephalopathy. 'Glucose before thiamine' is the classic error; 'it's just withdrawal' skips the differential; 'imaging before resuscitation' delays two time-critical, low-risk treatments.

**Full explanation:** Thiamine is a cofactor for glucose metabolism, and administering a carbohydrate load to a thiamine-depleted patient can acutely precipitate Wernicke encephalopathy (confusion, ophthalmoplegia, ataxia). In a malnourished patient with alcohol use and hypoglycemia, the safe practice is to give thiamine together with — or before — dextrose, then continue evaluating for withdrawal and other causes. Both interventions are immediate and should not be delayed for imaging.

**Why each distractor is wrong:**
- **B.** Dextrose without thiamine risks precipitating Wernicke encephalopathy in a depleted patient.
- **C.** Benzodiazepines treat withdrawal but do not address the hypoglycemia or thiamine deficiency driving this presentation.
- **D.** Head CT may follow, but it must not delay correcting hypoglycemia and giving thiamine.

**Tier 2 — Why must thiamine be given before or together with the glucose?**
- A. Glucose metabolism consumes thiamine, so a carbohydrate load in a deficient patient can precipitate Wernicke encephalopathy ✅
- B. Thiamine itself raises the blood glucose and corrects the hypoglycemia
- C. Dextrose blocks thiamine absorption from the gastrointestinal tract
- D. Thiamine competitively antagonizes alcohol at its receptor

_Tier-2 rationale:_ Thiamine is a cofactor for glucose metabolism, so giving glucose to a depleted patient can exhaust the last thiamine stores and precipitate Wernicke encephalopathy. The distractor rationales invent pharmacology the mechanism does not support.

**Key takeaway:** Thiamine before (or with) glucose in the malnourished alcohol-using patient.  
**Common reasoning error:** Treating the low glucose in isolation and forgetting the thiamine dependency of glucose metabolism.

**Safety/management caveat:** Do not withhold glucose in true hypoglycemia — give both; ensure adequate parenteral thiamine dosing per protocol.

**Review flags:** emergency, medication, higher-intensity-review

**Learning objective:** Recognize the opioid overdose toxidrome and select naloxone as the immediate reversal.  
**Tested decision:** The immediate intervention for suspected opioid overdose with respiratory depression.

**Vignette.** A 26-year-old man is brought to the emergency department unresponsive. He has pinpoint pupils, a respiratory rate of 6 breaths per minute, and shallow breathing. A friend reports he uses heroin. After beginning airway support and oxygen, which of the following is the most appropriate next intervention?

|---|---|---|
| A | Flumazenil _(trap: Wrong antidote)_ |  |

| C | Intravenous lorazepam _(trap: Sedating a sedated patient)_ |  |

| B | Anticholinergic toxicity; give physostigmine _(trap: Wrong toxidrome)_ |  |

| D | Malignant hyperthermia; administer dantrolene as the primary treatment _(trap: Anesthesia-only reflex)_ |  |

**Correct answer: C.**

**Why (concise):** Rapid onset of hyperthermia, diaphoresis, inducible clonus, and lower-extremity–predominant hyperreflexia after adding linezolid (an MAO inhibitor) to sertraline is serotonin syndrome; the first step is to stop serotonergic agents and support the patient. - **D.** Malignant hyperthermia is triggered by volatile anesthetics/succinylcholine, not this drug combination.

- C. Dry skin, flushing, and urinary retention from muscarinic blockade
- D. A ryanodine-receptor response to volatile anesthetics or succinylcholine

_Tier-2 rationale:_ The serotonergic trigger plus clonus and hyperreflexia over hours is the serotonin-syndrome signature; the distractor rationales are the mechanisms of the tier-1 traps — NMS, anticholinergic toxicity, and malignant hyperthermia.

**Common reasoning error:** Confusing serotonin syndrome with NMS and missing the neuromuscular and temporal clues.

## 19. `qbx_oth_001` — Anorexia nervosa — refeeding syndrome monitoring
**Category:** otherdx · **Type:** sba · **Intended difficulty:** standard (2) · **Exam:** both · **Setting:** inpatient · **Age:** adult-18-64

**NBME:** Other Systems · Pharmacotherapy/Intervention/Management · Inpatient · 13 and older  
**COMAT:** Feeding-Eating-Elimination/Sexual-Paraphilic · Management

**Learning objective:** Anticipate refeeding syndrome in severe anorexia nervosa and monitor/replete falling phosphate during nutritional rehabilitation.  
**Tested decision:** The most important complication to monitor as nutrition is reintroduced in severe anorexia.

**Vignette.** An 18-year-old woman with severe anorexia nervosa (very low body weight, bradycardia) is admitted for nutritional rehabilitation. As enteral feeding begins, which of the following laboratory changes is most important to monitor for and correct?

| | Option | |
|---|---|---|
| A | Falling serum phosphate | ✅ **KEY** |
| B | Rising serum sodium from dehydration _(trap: Fixating on sodium)_ |  |
| C | Rising serum calcium _(trap: Wrong electrolyte)_ |  |
| D | Falling blood urea nitrogen _(trap: Irrelevant marker)_ |  |

**Correct answer: A.**

**Why (concise):** Reintroducing nutrition drives an insulin-mediated intracellular shift of phosphate (and potassium and magnesium), so falling phosphate is the key refeeding-syndrome signal to monitor and correct. 'Fixating on sodium,' 'wrong electrolyte,' and 'irrelevant marker' each miss hypophosphatemia as the danger.

**Full explanation:** Refeeding syndrome occurs when carbohydrate reintroduction after starvation triggers an insulin surge that drives phosphate, potassium, and magnesium into cells, depleting already low stores. Hypophosphatemia is the hallmark and can cause cardiac and respiratory failure, arrhythmia, rhabdomyolysis, and death. Management is to advance calories cautiously, monitor electrolytes closely, and supplement phosphate (and potassium/magnesium and thiamine) as needed. Sodium, calcium, and BUN are not the defining safety signals.

**Why each distractor is wrong:**
- **B.** Hypernatremia is not the hallmark of refeeding; hypophosphatemia is.
- **C.** Calcium is not the defining refeeding abnormality.
- **D.** BUN trends do not signal refeeding risk; phosphate does.

**Key takeaway:** Monitor and correct falling phosphate during refeeding in anorexia nervosa.  
**Common reasoning error:** Feeding aggressively to restore weight without anticipating the intracellular electrolyte shift.

**Safety/management caveat:** Start low and go slow with calories; supplement phosphate/potassium/magnesium/thiamine and monitor cardiac status on telemetry.

**Review flags:** emergency, medication, higher-intensity-review

## 20. `qbx_oth_002` — Functional neurological symptom disorder — a rule-in diagnosis
**Category:** otherdx · **Type:** sba · **Intended difficulty:** advanced (3) · **Exam:** comat · **Setting:** ambulatory · **Age:** adult-18-64

**NBME:** Nervous System & Special Senses · Diagnosis · Ambulatory · 13 and older  
**COMAT:** Psychiatric Illness due to Medical Condition/Somatic/Sleep-Wake · History & Physical (incl. Dx) · **Osteopathic:** Integrating physical examination findings with psychological context in whole-person assessment.

**Learning objective:** Recognize functional neurological symptom (conversion) disorder as a rule-in diagnosis supported by positive examination signs, not a diagnosis of exclusion.  
**Tested decision:** The most likely diagnosis when a positive Hoover sign demonstrates internal inconsistency of weakness.

**Vignette.** A 29-year-old woman presents with 2 weeks of right leg weakness that began after an acute psychosocial stressor. On examination, voluntary right hip extension appears absent when tested directly, but strong involuntary right hip extension is felt under her heel when she flexes the left hip against resistance (a positive Hoover sign). Reflexes, sensation, and imaging are normal. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?

| | Option | |
|---|---|---|
| A | Malingering _(trap: Assuming intentional feigning)_ |  |
| B | Functional neurological symptom disorder | ✅ **KEY** |
| C | Acute ischemic stroke _(trap: Forcing a structural lesion)_ |  |
| D | Somatic symptom disorder _(trap: Confusing with somatic symptom disorder)_ |  |

**Correct answer: B.**

**Why (concise):** A positive Hoover sign shows the weakness is internally inconsistent — extension returns involuntarily — which rules in functional neurological symptom disorder. 'Assuming intentional feigning' misattributes conscious deception, 'forcing a structural lesion' ignores the positive sign and normal imaging, and 'confusing with somatic symptom disorder' misclassifies a neurological-deficit presentation.

**Full explanation:** Functional neurological symptom disorder is a positive, rule-in diagnosis: clinical signs demonstrate that motor or sensory findings are internally inconsistent or incompatible with recognized neurological disease. The Hoover sign does this for leg weakness — voluntary hip extension seems absent on direct testing but returns involuntarily during contralateral hip flexion against resistance. This is not conscious feigning (which would be malingering or factitious disorder), not a structural lesion (imaging normal, sign positive), and not somatic symptom disorder (which is defined by distressing somatic symptoms with disproportionate thoughts/behaviors rather than an exam-incompatible deficit).

**Why each distractor is wrong:**
- **A.** Malingering requires intentional feigning for external gain; functional symptoms are not consciously produced.
- **C.** A positive Hoover sign and normal imaging are incompatible with a fixed corticospinal stroke.
- **D.** Somatic symptom disorder involves distressing symptoms with excessive thoughts/behaviors, not an exam-incompatible neurological deficit.

**Key takeaway:** A positive Hoover sign supports functional neurological symptom disorder.  
**Common reasoning error:** Treating functional disorders as diagnoses of exclusion or assuming the patient is feigning.

**Safety/management caveat:** Communicate the diagnosis positively and non-stigmatizingly; still exclude comorbid neurological disease when clinically indicated.

**Review flags:** osteopathic-content

## 21. `qbx_eth_001` — Decisional capacity — refusal is not incapacity
**Category:** ethics · **Type:** sba · **Intended difficulty:** standard (2) · **Exam:** comat · **Setting:** inpatient · **Age:** adult-18-64

**NBME:** Social Sciences · Diagnosis · Inpatient · 13 and older  
**COMAT:** Depressive/Bipolar/Related · Health Promotion/Prevention/Care Delivery · **Osteopathic:** Respecting patient autonomy within holistic, relationship-centered care.

**Learning objective:** Assess decisional capacity as four decision-specific abilities and recognize that disagreement alone does not establish incapacity.  
**Tested decision:** The best next step when a patient refuses recommended treatment.

**Vignette.** A 58-year-old man admitted with a diabetic foot infection declines a recommended below-knee amputation. He is alert, has no psychotic symptoms, and states he understands he could die without surgery but wants to try antibiotics first because he has seen a relative recover that way. His care team is frustrated by his refusal. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

| | Option | |
|---|---|---|
| A | Declare him to lack capacity because he is refusing a life-saving operation _(trap: Refusal = no capacity)_ |  |
| B | Seek a court order to proceed with surgery over his objection _(trap: Skipping to the courts)_ |  |
| C | Ask a family member to consent on his behalf _(trap: Bypassing the capacitated patient)_ |  |
| D | Assess his decision-making capacity for this specific treatment decision | ✅ **KEY** |

**Correct answer: D.**

**Why (concise):** The next step is a structured, decision-specific capacity assessment across the four abilities — understanding, appreciation, reasoning, and communicating a choice — because refusal alone is not incapacity. 'Refusal = no capacity,' 'skipping to the courts,' and 'bypassing the capacitated patient' all substitute frustration or shortcut for the required assessment.

**Full explanation:** Decision-making capacity is clinical, decision-specific, and defined by four abilities: to understand the relevant information, to appreciate how it applies to one's own situation, to reason with the information, and to communicate a choice. A patient who demonstrates all four may make a choice the team disagrees with and still retain capacity. Here the man articulates understanding and a coherent rationale, so the correct step is to complete a structured capacity assessment rather than override, litigate, or seek surrogate consent. Surrogate decision-making and legal escalation apply only if capacity is found lacking.

**Why each distractor is wrong:**
- **A.** Refusing a recommended operation is not, by itself, evidence of incapacity; capacity must be assessed.
- **B.** Court involvement is premature before a capacity assessment and a discussion of his reasoning.
- **C.** Surrogate consent applies only when the patient lacks capacity, which has not been established.

**Key takeaway:** Assess the four abilities for the specific decision before overriding a refusal.  
**Common reasoning error:** Inferring incapacity from disagreement with the recommended plan.

**Safety/management caveat:** If capacity is lacking, follow the surrogate/least-restrictive pathway; legal standards for competence vary by jurisdiction.

**Review flags:** legal-jurisdiction, higher-intensity-review

## 22. `qbx_eth_002` — Confidentiality vs duty to protect a third party
**Category:** ethics · **Type:** sba · **Intended difficulty:** standard (2) · **Exam:** both · **Setting:** ambulatory · **Age:** adult-18-64

**NBME:** Social Sciences · Pharmacotherapy/Intervention/Management · Ambulatory · 13 and older  
**COMAT:** Depressive/Bipolar/Related · Health Promotion/Prevention/Care Delivery

> ⚠ **Content note:** Describes a threat of violence without graphic detail.

**Learning objective:** Balance patient confidentiality against a clinician's duty to protect an identifiable third party from a serious, imminent threat.  
**Tested decision:** The best next step when a patient voices a specific, serious threat toward an identifiable person.

**Vignette.** During an outpatient visit, a 35-year-old man with escalating anger describes a specific, serious plan to harm a named coworker within days and has the means to do so. He asks you to keep everything he says confidential. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

| | Option | |
|---|---|---|
| A | Maintain strict confidentiality because he explicitly requested it _(trap: Confidentiality as absolute)_ |  |
| B | End the session and document the threat without any protective action _(trap: Documentation instead of action)_ |  |
| C | Protect the identifiable third party and manage acute risk, per local law | ✅ **KEY** |
| D | Immediately call the police to arrest the patient as the only necessary action _(trap: Single-channel response)_ |  |

**Correct answer: C.**

**Why (concise):** A serious, imminent threat to an identifiable person triggers a duty to take reasonable protective steps while managing acute risk, within local law. 'Confidentiality as absolute' and 'documentation instead of action' fail to act, and the 'single-channel response' collapses a range of protective options into one jurisdiction-dependent action.

**Full explanation:** Patient confidentiality is a strong but not absolute duty; when a patient poses a serious, imminent risk of harm to an identifiable third party, clinicians have a duty to take reasonable steps to protect the potential victim. What that requires — warning the person, notifying law enforcement, hospitalizing the patient, or intensifying treatment — varies by jurisdiction and case, so the correct step is to manage the acute risk and take reasonable protective action consistent with local legal requirements, rather than either preserving confidentiality absolutely or reflexively defaulting to a single action.

**Why each distractor is wrong:**
- **A.** Confidentiality is overridden by a serious, imminent threat to an identifiable person.
- **B.** Documenting the threat does not satisfy the duty to take protective action.
- **D.** Arrest is only one possible step and is jurisdiction-dependent; protection may require several coordinated actions.

**Key takeaway:** Take reasonable protective action for a credible, imminent threat, following local law.  
**Common reasoning error:** Treating confidentiality as absolute or assuming one uniform legal rule applies everywhere.

**Safety/management caveat:** Duty-to-protect/warn statutes vary by jurisdiction; involve supervising clinicians and follow institutional and local legal policy.
