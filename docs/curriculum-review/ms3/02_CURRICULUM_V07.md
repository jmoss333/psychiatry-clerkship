# Motivational Interviewing — A Student's Quick Guide


**For:** MS3 on the unit. MI is named in the clerkship objectives (psychotherapy principles + applying basic MI). It's the highest-yield communication skill you can practice this rotation — for substance use, medication adherence, and any "I don't want to change" conversation. Educational scaffold; fictional examples only.

## The one idea
People talk themselves into change. Your job is **not** to supply the arguments (the "righting reflex" — resist it) but to *evoke* the patient's own reasons. Pushing usually produces **sustain talk**; curiosity produces **change talk**.

## The spirit — PACE
- **Partnership** — work *with*, not *on*, the patient.
- **Acceptance** — respect autonomy and worth; affirm strengths.
- **Compassion** — act in their best interest.
- **Evocation** — draw out their motivations rather than installing yours.

## The four processes
1. **Engaging** — build the relationship ("What matters most to you right now?").
2. **Focusing** — agree on a target ("Would it be OK to talk about your drinking?").
3. **Evoking** — elicit change talk (the heart of MI).
4. **Planning** — only when readiness is there; build a concrete next step.

## Your core moves — OARS
- **Open questions** — "What worries you about your blood pressure and the alcohol?"
- **Affirmations** — name a real strength: "You came in on your own — that took resolve."
- **Reflections** — mirror meaning, especially change talk: "Part of you is tired of feeling this way."
- **Summaries** — gather the change talk back to them: "So you're frustrated, your family's worried, and you've quit before — what's the next small step?"

## Listen for change talk — DARN-CAT
Preparatory: **D**esire, **A**bility, **R**easons, **N**eed. Mobilizing: **C**ommitment, **A**ctivation, **T**aking steps. When you hear it, **reflect and reinforce it** — don't rush past it.

## A 30-second tool: the readiness ruler
"On a 0–10 scale, how important is changing this? … You said 4 — why not a 2?" (forces change talk) … "What would move you from a 4 to a 6?" Repeat for **confidence**.

## Sample inpatient snippet (composite)
> **Student:** "Would it be OK to talk about the drinking before discharge?"
> **Patient:** "I'm not an alcoholic."
> **Student:** "That label doesn't fit you. *(reflection)* And the drinking has landed you here twice this year. *(no argument — just the fact)* What's your read on that?"
> **Patient:** "…I guess I don't want a third time."
> **Student:** "You don't want a third time. *(reflect change talk)* What's one thing that might lower the odds?"

## Where it fits
Substance use, medication adherence, lifestyle/metabolic change, follow-up engagement. It pairs with — not replaces — diagnosis-specific treatment.

- **safety** — Keep bedside therapy within scope: stabilize, validate, problem-solve, and escalate risk or trauma flooding to supervision.
- **say** — We may not solve everything today, but we can make the next step clearer and less lonely.
- **collateral** — Ask the team what pattern they are seeing and align the intervention with the shared treatment frame.

# Brief Psychotherapy on the Unit


**In one line** — You will not complete a course of therapy on an inpatient unit, but almost every encounter is a *micro-intervention*. The skill is to read the mechanism driving this patient's crisis right now and match one brief technique to it — then hand the work off to outpatient care.

**Why brief therapy is worth doing inpatient** — Across trials, inpatient psychotherapy adds a small-to-moderate benefit over treatment-as-usual, and **no single modality is clearly superior** (small-to-moderate pooled effects with no clear winner across modalities — the specific meta-analyses are traced on the Evidence-Based Inpatient Psychiatry page). The practical takeaway is liberating: *stop agonizing over which "school" to use and match the technique to the mechanism in front of you.* Diagnosis and the driver of the crisis matter more than the brand of therapy.

## Match the skill to the mechanism

| What you see | Likely mechanism | Reach for | Why (evidence) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Withdrawn, inactive, anhedonic, "nothing matters" | Behavioral shutdown | **Behavioral activation (BA)** — schedule one small, valued activity | Superior to TAU for depression (SMD −0.78) and anxiety (−0.33), on par with CBT; **lowest cognitive demand → best inpatient fit** (Uphoff, *Cochrane* 2020) |
| Escalating, flooded, about to act | Acute emotional dysregulation | **DBT skills — TIPP** (temperature, intense exercise, paced breathing, paired relaxation) | Skills-containing DBT arms outperform for self-injury in dismantling trials (Linehan 2015) |
| Just self-harmed or nearly did | Behavior serving a function | **Chain analysis** — walk the sequence *with* the patient, non-punitively | Turns an incident into a formulation and a plan; core DBT move |
| Repeatedly returns in crisis, overwhelmed by problems | Problem-solving deficit | **Problem-solving therapy (PST)** — the 4-step format (define → brainstorm → choose → try) | Repeat self-harm re-presentation 13.5% vs 22.1%, **NNT 12** (Hatcher 2011) |
| Ambivalent about meds, treatment, or substance use | Motivational conflict | **Motivational interviewing (MI)** — roll with resistance, evoke change talk | Improves engagement and substance outcomes; see the [Motivational Interviewing](?page=motivational_interviewing.md) page |
| Confused or frightened by the illness itself | Knowledge/coping gap | **Psychoeducation** — illness model + warning-signs plan, include family | Among the best-supported inpatient psychological interventions: relapse **NNT 9**, readmission **NNT 5** — the latter on only 206 participants, in studies the reviewers called limited quality (Xia, *Cochrane* 2011) |
| Actively suicidal | Acute risk state | **Safety planning (Stanley-Brown)** + crisis response planning | Safety Planning Intervention with follow-up ≈ **50% fewer suicidal behaviors** (Stanley 2018); **abandon no-suicide "contracts"** — they don't work |

## The bedside frame (MGH-style)
When you have five minutes, not fifty: lead with **support**, name the **affect**, do a quick **chain analysis** of what just happened, and notice the **relational pattern** playing out on the unit (Stein & Jacobo). A useful daily scaffold — three quick **assessments** (safety, sleep, function), three **interventions** (one skill, one activity, one connection), three **discharge tasks** (appointment, means safety, warning-signs plan).

## What the student does
- Name the mechanism out loud on rounds before proposing a technique — "this looks like behavioral shutdown" beats "he seems depressed."
- Do one concrete thing at the bedside: schedule a single valued activity (BA), teach one TIPP skill, or walk a chain analysis after an incident.
- For any patient at risk, build a **collaborative** safety plan in the patient's words — never a no-suicide contract.
- Give psychoeducation as a conversation, not a packet dump; loop in family when you can.
- Write the handoff so the outpatient clinician can continue the thread — the point of inpatient therapy is to *start* it and secure the follow-up.

## High-yield pearls
- Match the skill to the mechanism; the modality matters less than the fit (no clear winner across schools).
- **Behavioral activation** is the highest-yield, lowest-effort inpatient move for the shut-down, depressed patient.
- **Psychoeducation** is among the best-supported inpatient psychological interventions, and it's free — but the readmission NNT of 5 rests on 206 participants in what the Cochrane reviewers (Xia et al., 2011) called *"hospital-based studies of limited quality."* Do it anyway; don't quote the number as though it were precise. ([Therapy on the Unit](?page=therapy_on_the_unit.md) carries the full figures and the caveat.)
- Chain analysis *replaces* a punitive response to self-harm — understand the behavior, don't just document it.
- Safety planning with follow-up beats a "contract for safety," which has no evidence and can create false reassurance.

# Therapy on the Unit — What You Can Actually Do This Week

> Learner level: **MS3 core**, with labeled **Resident extension** blocks (shown on both sites).

---

*You admit a 24-year-old the night after an overdose. She'll be here four days, maybe five. The attending says "we'll get her stabilized and set up outpatient therapy." You wonder: is anything that happens here — between the med changes and the discharge paperwork — actually therapy? Or is the unit just a waiting room for the real treatment somewhere else?*

*It's a fair question, and it has an evidence-based answer: the admission itself is a treatment window, and some of the most effective things done in it take ten minutes.*

---

## Objectives

By the end of this module you should be able to:

1. State what the evidence shows about whether psychotherapy delivered during a psychiatric admission works, and why the *modality* matters less than you'd expect.
2. Name the three interventions every inpatient should receive regardless of diagnosis, and the evidence behind each.
3. Deliver four 5–10 minute evidence-based micro-interventions: validation, a behavioral-activation "micro-dose," one distress-tolerance skill, and a motivational-interviewing readiness ruler.
4. Match the first-line brief intervention to the clinical presentation.
5. Describe the generalist stance for borderline personality disorder (Good Psychiatric Management) and what to do — and not do — during an affective storm.
6. Run the opening and closing of a 30-minute inpatient family meeting, and explain why "listening is not disclosing."

## In 30 seconds

Adding psychotherapy to standard inpatient care produces a real, modest benefit (standardized effects roughly 0.24–0.43), and **no modality is clearly superior — diagnosis and common factors (alliance, validation, structure) matter more than brand name** [1,2]. The three non-negotiables for every patient: a **personalized, high-quality safety plan** (associated with roughly 45% fewer suicidal behaviors when paired with follow-up contact in the landmark ED cohort study — a cohort comparison, not an RCT) [3], **structured psychoeducation** (in psychosis, number-needed-to-treat of about 5 to prevent one readmission) [4 ✓], and **discharge bridging** — because the weeks right after discharge are the highest-risk period in the entire episode of care [5]. Between those, every encounter is a chance for a micro-intervention: schedule one activity, teach one skill, roll with one ambivalence. What you should *not* do is equally evidence-based: no no-suicide contracts, no trauma processing mid-crisis, no starting a 16-session manual on day 2 of a 4-day stay.

---

## 1 · Does therapy on an inpatient unit actually work?

**The honest answer: yes, modestly — and the modality doesn't much matter.**

The best meta-analysis of inpatient psychotherapy for major depression (14 RCTs) found a pooled benefit of SMD 0.24 over standard care alone — NNT about 7 — with essentially *zero* heterogeneity across CBT, behavioral activation, psychodynamic, and interpersonal approaches [1 ✓]. A broader meta-analysis across diagnoses (37 samples) found d = 0.43 for psychotherapy's specific contribution, and again **therapy orientation had no significant effect — diagnosis moderated outcomes more than modality** [2 ✓].

Hold both halves of that finding. The effect is real: an admission is a treatment window, not a holding pattern. And the effect is *generic*: what seems to carry it is the common-factors core — alliance, validation, structure, a credible plan — which is exactly the part an MS3 can participate in from day one.

> **Resident extension — the redesign argument.** A 2026 JAMA Psychiatry viewpoint (Kleiman et al.) argues the field should stop treating units as "short-term outpatient" and instead build stand-alone, open-enrollment, transdiagnostic modules targeting four crisis mechanisms: emotion dysregulation, cognitive inflexibility, behavioral inactivity, and life-threatening behaviors [6 ✓]. That framework is a useful mental model *now*: before each encounter, ask which mechanism is primary, and pick the matching micro-intervention below.

## 2 · The non-negotiable three (every patient, every admission)

**2a. A safety plan that's actually personalized.** The Stanley–Brown Safety Planning Intervention with structured follow-up was associated with roughly 45% fewer suicidal behaviors and double the odds of outpatient engagement in a large ED cohort [3 ✓]. Two clinical points the evidence adds: **quality matters** — plan quality, especially the warning-signs step, predicts outcomes; a generic template pasted into the chart may provide false reassurance [7 ✓] — and **no-suicide contracts are obsolete**: they have no evidence of benefit and structured crisis planning is superior [8 ✓]. Watch how your team approaches the lethal-means conversation: it is counseling about securing the home environment, delivered collaboratively and matter-of-factly. Be honest about its evidence tier: the population-level wins (bridge barriers, pack-size limits, pesticide bans) are concrete — see the Steeg umbrella review in the go-deeper rail — but controlled studies of means-change interventions show a small reduction, if any, in deaths, and their ecological designs preclude individual-level causal inference [9 ✓].

**2b. Structured psychoeducation.** In schizophrenia-spectrum admissions, structured psychoeducation is among the best-supported psychological interventions and one of the few that is genuinely deliverable inside a short stay. Cochrane pooling across 44 trials (n = 5,142) found reduced relapse (RR 0.70; NNT 9) and reduced readmission (RR 0.71; NNT 5) [4 ✓]. Read those numbers with the reviewers' own caveat: these were *"hospital-based studies of limited quality"* and *"the true size of effect is likely to be less than demonstrated"* — and the readmission estimate rests on just 206 participants [4 ✓]. Do it anyway; the intervention is cheap, safe and probably helps. Just don't quote NNT 5 as though it were precise. The catch from practice surveys: most of what gets called psychoeducation is unstructured conversation. Structure it — diagnosis, medications, early warning signs, when and whom to call — and document that it happened [10 ✓].

**2c. Discharge bridging.** The weeks after discharge are the highest-risk period in the whole episode of care. Pooled across 100 studies and 4.7 million person-years, the post-discharge suicide rate was 484 per 100,000 person-years overall, 1,132 within the first three months, and 2,078 among patients admitted for suicidal ideas or behaviour [5 ✓]; in the first week alone the pooled rate is roughly 2,950 per 100,000 person-years [32 ✓]. Risk is highest early but stays elevated for years — bridging is the start of aftercare, not the end of the admission. Bridging is therapy, not paperwork: review the safety plan against the actual home environment, confirm (don't just recommend) follow-up within 7 days, rehearse the first likely stressor, and make sure crisis contacts are in the patient's phone, not just the chart. Something delivered *during* the admission can bend the post-discharge curve: brief CBT for suicide prevention delivered on the unit reduced post-discharge emergency utilization in a randomized trial's secondary analysis [11 ✓], and caring contacts after discharge are a cheap, patient-endorsed way to extend the tether [12 ✓].

## 3 · The 5-minute toolkit (things you can do on rounds today)

**Validation (the alliance is the intervention).** Reflect the emotion, name the experience, don't argue with it. In a setting marked by coercion and lost autonomy, the "human" relationship is what patients consistently say they value — and alliance quality tracks outcomes across settings [13 ✓]. This is not the soft part before the real work; per §1, it may *be* the active ingredient.

**Behavioral-activation micro-dose.** "What's one thing you used to enjoy that we could get on the schedule for today — even a shower and ten minutes in the day room?" Schedule it; ask about mood before and after tomorrow. BA is effective for depression at meta-analytic scale, works in group format (the format your unit actually runs), and its low cognitive demand makes it the right first move for the withdrawn, anergic patient [14 ✓, 15 ✓].

**One distress-tolerance skill.** Teach a single physiological down-regulation skill from the DBT toolkit (e.g., paced breathing with a long exhale, or brief intense exercise). Skills training appears to be the *active ingredient* of DBT for reducing self-injury — which is exactly why skills-only teaching is legitimate on a unit that can't run full DBT [16 ✓, 17 ✓].

**MI readiness ruler.** "On a 0-to-10 scale, how important is it to you right now to cut back? … Why a 4 and not a 2?" That second question elicits change talk. MI's outcome evidence in substance use is modest and honest calibration matters [18 ✓] — but for *engagement* of ambivalent and involuntary patients, the MI stance (open questions, affirmations, reflections, summaries; autonomy support; no confrontation) is the right default in every encounter, and it matters most in dual-diagnosis presentations, which are this unit's daily reality [19 ✓].

## 4 · Matching the intervention to the presentation

| Presentation | First move (time) | Second move | Key evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Suicidal ideation or recent attempt | Full 6-step safety plan (20 min) | Brief-CBT-style session if stay allows | [3 ✓, 11 ✓] |
| Self-harm, borderline features | Distress-tolerance skill + behavior chain review (10 min) | Skills group enrollment | [16 ✓, 17 ✓] |
| Depression, withdrawal, anergia | BA scheduling (10 min) | Psychoeducation | [14 ✓, 15 ✓] |
| Acute psychosis | Supportive contact + structured psychoeducation (10 min) | Brief CBTp-style formulation only if engaged | [4 ✓, 20 ✓] |
| Substance use, ambivalence | MI conversation (10–20 min) | Referral pathway before discharge | [18 ✓, 19 ✓] |
| Involuntary, disengaged | MI spirit + validation; avoid confrontation | Collaborative formulation | [13 ✓] |
| Everyone, pre-discharge | Safety-plan review + bridging (10–20 min) | Caring-contacts plan | [5 ✓, 12 ✓] |

*Cognitive-demand rule of thumb: the more acute the disorganization, the more behavioral and supportive the intervention. Restructuring-type work waits for a clearer sensorium.*

## 5 · BPD without a DBT unit

About one in five psychiatric admissions involves borderline personality disorder, so this is core inpatient skill, not specialist territory. The generalist frame is **Good Psychiatric Management (GPM)**: any competent clinician can treat BPD — with case management, psychoeducation about the interpersonal-hypersensitivity model, a life-outside-treatment focus, and conservative prescribing [21 ✓]. Four ward-level behaviors follow:

- **Keep admissions brief and goal-directed**, with the discharge date named early — so fears of abandonment get worked *with*, not avoided. Chronic suicidality without acute escalation is generally an outpatient problem; reflexive admission can reinforce the wrong contingencies [22 ✓].
- **Manage splitting at the team level, not the patient level:** one plan, communicated identically by every staff member, with the dynamic normalized in team formulation as the patient's internal world showing itself — not as staff failure [22 ✓].
- **Don't diagnose a comorbidity out of an affective storm.** Trigger-linked mood shifts lasting hours are BPD's expected physiology; treating each storm as new-onset MDD or GAD is how patients leave with five medications, none of which treats BPD [22 ✓]. Notably, teaching skills has been shown to *reduce* polypharmacy [23 ✓].
- **The evidence for hope is real and current:** head-to-head trials of specialist therapies (DBT vs schema therapy), DBT vs medication for suicidal behavior, and skills-only training all support the message that BPD is a treatable condition with multiple credible paths [24 ✓, 25 ✓, 26 ✓].

## 6 · The family meeting (your strongest under-used intervention)

Family intervention in schizophrenia reduces relapse and readmission with an NNT around 7 — statin territory — yet family involvement rates on real units are dismal, and the main barrier is clinician avoidance rationalized as confidentiality [27 ✓, 28 ✓]. Two teaching points fix most of it:

- **Listening is not disclosing.** You can always *receive* information from a family — observations, history, concerns — without the patient's permission. Consent governs what you share, not what you hear.
- **The inpatient meeting plants a seed; it doesn't harvest.** Relapse-prevention effects of family work show up at 12 months, not 6. The 30-minute inpatient meeting (agenda → family's account → jargon-free education → warning signs and plan → questions and resources) is the *entry point* to that dose, and the emotional climate it addresses is a measured, modifiable relapse predictor — high expressed emotion is a robust relapse predictor — in schizophrenia it carried roughly a five-fold increase in the *odds* of early (≤12-month) relapse, and about two-fold for later relapse (Ma et al., *Psychol Med* 2021) — and warmth is protective (OR ~0.35). Read those as odds, not risk: relapse is common enough that the increase in absolute risk is considerably smaller than "five-fold" suggests [29 ✓, 30 ✓].

> **Resident extension.** Run the full structure: pre-meeting consent-scope check → opening with role clarification → EE-informed listening (hear criticism and over-involvement without labeling them) → psychoeducation with numbers ("family involvement cuts relapse roughly in half") → explicit discharge triad (appointments · warning signs · who-to-call). De-escalation of a blaming room is the skill to deliberately practice; the family-engagement literature for BPD (integrating GPM principles) is the go-deeper read [31 ✓].

## 7 · What NOT to do (each of these is evidence-based, not etiquette)

No **no-suicide contracts** — no efficacy evidence; crisis/safety planning is superior [8 ✓]. No **detailed trauma processing during acute crisis** — stabilize, ground, defer exposure-based work to outpatient. No **full manualized courses compressed into a 4-day stay** — match dose to length of stay; single high-quality sessions beat truncated protocols. No **"psychoeducation" that's really an unstructured chat** — structure and document it [10 ✓]. No **medication escalation as the response to a BPD affective storm** [22 ✓]. And no therapeutic nihilism: "nothing real can happen here" is contradicted by every meta-analysis in §1.

## Self-check (Step 2 CK style)

**1.** A 31-year-old man is discharged tomorrow after an admission for a suicide attempt. Which period carries his highest risk of suicide?
A. Months 6–12 after discharge  B. The first 1–4 weeks after discharge  C. Weeks 6–8 after discharge  D. Risk is uniform across the first year

**Answer: B.** Post-discharge suicide risk is dramatically front-loaded: the pooled rate is about 2,950 per 100,000 person-years in the first week and 2,060 in the first month [32 ✓] — which is why safety-plan review, confirmed 7-day follow-up, and bridging contacts are discharge *treatments*, not clerical steps [11 ✓].

**2.** A 22-year-old woman with borderline personality disorder is admitted after self-injury. By day 3, night staff describe her as "manipulative," while her day nurse advocates strongly for extending the admission. The next best step in management is:
A. Assign her exclusively to the day nurse she trusts  B. A team meeting producing one plan that every staff member communicates identically  C. Transfer to another unit  D. Behavioral contract threatening discharge for further self-injury

**Answer: B.** This is splitting — the patient's polarized internal world reproduced in the team. The evidence-based response is team-level: shared formulation, a single consistent plan, and normalization of the dynamic so it informs rather than divides care [22 ✓]. Exclusive assignment (A) entrenches the split; punitive contingencies (D) escalate risk.

**3.** A 45-year-old man admitted with major depression spends most of the day in bed and says therapy "takes too much energy." The most appropriate initial psychotherapeutic intervention is:
A. Cognitive restructuring of automatic thoughts  B. Insight-oriented exploration of early relationships  C. Collaboratively scheduling one small activity for today  D. Exposure hierarchy construction

**Answer: C.** Behavioral activation has strong meta-analytic support, works as well as full CBT in head-to-head and network comparisons, and its minimal cognitive demand fits exactly the anergic patient who can't yet use restructuring [14 ✓, 15 ✓].

**4.** A 38-year-old woman admitted with alcohol use disorder says, "I don't really think drinking is my problem." The most effective opening response is:
A. Present the medical consequences of continued drinking  B. "On a scale of 0 to 10, how important is cutting back to you — and why that number and not a lower one?"  C. Recommend she attend AA after discharge  D. Defer the conversation to outpatient treatment

**Answer: B.** The readiness ruler is a core MI technique: the follow-up question elicits the patient's *own* change talk. Confrontation with consequences (A) predictably produces counter-argument. MI's strongest effects are in exactly this population — ambivalent, not treatment-seeking [18 ✓, 19 ✓].

**5.** Which intervention for a suicidal patient being discharged from acute care has the strongest evidence for reducing subsequent suicidal behavior?
A. A signed no-suicide contract  B. Unstructured supportive counseling  C. A personalized safety plan with structured follow-up contact  D. Referral placed for outpatient therapy

**Answer: C.** Safety planning with follow-up was associated with roughly 45% fewer suicidal behaviors and doubled treatment engagement [3 ✓]; no-suicide contracts (A) have no efficacy evidence and are no longer recommended [8 ✓]. A referral alone (D) fails the bridging test — only a minority attend early follow-up without active linkage.

# I Need Collateral: 10-Minute Workflow


**In one line** - Collateral is clinical data, not gossip: get permission when possible, ask only what changes diagnosis/risk/treatment/discharge, share the minimum necessary, and bring one clear update back to the team.

Use this when a patient is guarded, psychotic, manic, delirious, cognitively impaired, intoxicated/withdrawing, high-risk, or headed toward discharge with a plan that depends on other people.

## 0. Stop Before You Call

Name the purpose in one sentence:

- "I need baseline and timeline."
- "I need suicide/violence/means safety details."
- "I need medication adherence and side-effect history."
- "I need discharge reality: housing, transport, supervision, follow-up, and limits."

Ask your supervisor what you are allowed to disclose. If you are not sure, disclose less and ask more.

## 1. Consent And Boundaries

Best first move with the patient:

> "Collateral helps us understand what changed and what will be safe after discharge. Who would be helpful to call, and what are you comfortable with us sharing?"

Clarify:

- Who may be contacted.
- What may be shared.
- What the patient does not want shared.
- Whether there are safety exceptions or mandatory reporting issues.
- Whether a professional interpreter is needed.

Do not use family as an interpreter for confidential or high-risk topics.

## 2. Open The Call

Start with role, purpose, and boundary:

> "Hi, I am a medical student working with the inpatient psychiatry team. I am calling to understand baseline, recent changes, and what support is realistic after discharge. I may not be able to share details without the patient's permission, but I can listen and bring relevant information back to the team."

If the caller demands information:

> "I hear why you want answers. Right now, the safest thing I can do is gather what you have noticed and make sure the supervising team knows your concerns."

## 3. Ask In Four Buckets

### Baseline And Timeline

- What is the patient like when well?
- When did this episode start?
- What changed first: sleep, mood, thinking, behavior, substances, medication adherence, medical symptoms?
- Is this new, recurrent, or worse than usual?

### Risk And Safety

- Any suicidal statements, threats, preparations, giving things away, goodbye messages, or access to lethal means?
- Any violence threats, weapons access, paranoia-driven defensive behavior, stalking, aggression, or escalating agitation?
- Any vulnerability: inability to eat/drink, unsafe wandering, exploitation, falls, medication errors, or inability to care for self?

### Treatment And Function

- What medications helped or hurt before?
- Any adverse reactions, nonadherence, cost barriers, or substance interactions?
- What outpatient care exists, and what has made follow-up fail?
- What helps the patient calm, sleep, communicate, or accept help?

### Discharge Reality

- Where will the patient sleep the first night?
- Who will physically be there, and when?
- What family/support role is realistic, and what is not?
- Who can help with medications, transportation, appointments, sleep routine, and means safety?
- What would make discharge unsafe from the support person's perspective?

## 4. What Not To Do

- Do not promise discharge, admission, medication changes, diagnoses, or legal outcomes.
- Do not turn the family into the treatment team.
- Do not ask broad questions like "Any concerns?" and stop there.
- Do not document family opinions as facts without attribution.
- Do not share more patient information than the consent/policy permits.
- Do not ignore information that contradicts the patient's report.

## 5. Bring It Back To The Team

Use this 30-second update:

> "I spoke with [relationship/role]. They described baseline as ____. The key timeline point is ____. Risk-relevant collateral is ____. Discharge-relevant barrier/support is ____. This changes our plan because ____."

If nothing changed:

> "Collateral supported the current formulation: baseline ____, episode timeline ____, no new means/violence/discharge barriers reported beyond what we already knew."

## 6. Document Without Overreaching

Include:

- Who you spoke with and their relationship/role.
- Whether consent was obtained or the policy/safety rationale for contact.
- What information was reported, attributed to the source.
- What changed in diagnosis, risk, treatment, or discharge planning.
- What still needs verification.

Avoid:

- Family conflict details that do not change care.
- Speculation stated as fact.
- Sensitive disclosures beyond the clinical purpose.
- Patient identifiers in any learning exports, practice tools, or examples.

# Family, Discharge, And Systems Module

Generated: 2026-06-27

Audience: MS3 students.

Purpose: translate family systems, internal manuals, ED psychiatry, and discharge

## Core Idea

Inpatient psychiatry does not end at symptom reduction. A patient leaves into a
system: family, housing, medications, transportation, work/school, finances,
follow-up, stigma, and safety. If the system cannot hold the plan, the plan is
not done.

## Why It Matters — The Numbers

- **The window is highest right after discharge.** Suicide risk in the first week post-discharge is on the order of ~2,950 per 100,000 person-years — with days 0–3 the highest — roughly 200× the general-population rate (Chung et al., *BMJ Open* 2019; the overall post-discharge rate of 484 per 100,000 person-years is from Chung et al., *JAMA Psychiatry* 2017). The realism of the plan matters *most* in exactly the days after the patient leaves.
- **Follow-up is often the weak link.** About 12–15% of psychiatric patients are readmitted within 30 days (20–25% for schizophrenia), and only ~50% receive a mental-health visit within 7 days. A *scheduled* appointment (not "they'll call to arrange one") is itself the intervention — pre-discharge 7-day follow-up is associated with substantially lower suicide risk.
- **Means safety is a lethality intervention, not a formality.** Firearms are involved in ~50% of US suicides and are lethal in 85–90% of attempts; structured lethal-means counseling matters: in a large quasi-experimental cohort (Boggs et al. 2020), patients with documented lethal-means assessment had fewer suicide attempts and deaths at 180 days (0.83% vs 3.3%) — an association, not a randomized effect. This is why the "who can confirm items are secured?" phrase below is a clinical act.
- **Bringing the family in changes outcomes.** Family psychoeducation lowers relapse from ~37% to ~10% (OR 0.18), and family involvement during admission nearly triples the odds of 7-day follow-up (Haselden 2019).

> Structure the 90-minute meeting across five phases — open/join, share the picture, hear the family and read expressed emotion, problem-solve and plan, then summarize — convene early to plant a seed, not at discharge to harvest one.

**Key points (bulleted card):**

- Convene the family meeting early in the admission — the relapse-prevention benefit of family work accrues over the following 12 months, not on the unit; saving it for the day before discharge squanders the inpatient window.

# Family Meeting Playbook (90-min)


**In one line** — A structured 90-minute inpatient family meeting: prepare deliberately, join the family, share the picture in plain language, surface and de-shame expressed emotion, then build a concrete plan together. Use it as a scaffold, not a script — the family's needs set the pace.

## When to use it

Convene a family meeting for a first episode, a relapse, a high-expressed-emotion home, a complex disposition, a safety plan that depends on the family, or any time the people who will support the patient after discharge need a shared understanding. Earlier is better — don't save the meeting for the day of discharge. You are **planting a seed, not harvesting one**: the relapse-prevention benefit of family work accrues over the following ~12 months, and a single meeting rarely shows its payoff on the unit — which is exactly why starting it inpatient and securing the outpatient handoff matters (the dose that works is ≥10 sessions; ≤2 is ineffective).

## Before the meeting (≈15 min prep)

- **Confirm consent and scope.** The patient agrees to the meeting and to what may be shared; document it. Respect the patient's limits on disclosure (PHI is the patient's to share).
- **Set the goal in one sentence.** "Align on the diagnosis and the discharge plan," or "Lower the temperature at home and agree on warning signs." A meeting with one clear goal beats a meeting that tries to do everything.
- **Know your audience.** Who is coming, who is missing, who holds influence, what each person is most worried about.
- **Brief the team.** Agree on who leads, who takes notes, who watches the patient's distress, and the two or three messages everyone will keep consistent.
- **Logistics.** A private room, enough chairs, phones silenced, an interpreter if needed, ~90 minutes protected, and a plan for breaks.

## The 90-minute arc

**1 · Open and join — 0:00–0:15.** Introduce everyone and their role. Name the goal and the rough agenda, and set simple ground rules (one person at a time; everyone gets heard; we focus on what helps next). Start by asking the family what *they* most want from the meeting — and write it down.

**2 · Share the picture — 0:15–0:35.** Give the team's understanding in plain language: what the diagnosis is and isn't, what the course and treatment look like, and what to expect. Avoid jargon; check understanding as you go ("What's your understanding of what we just covered?"). Let the patient speak to their own experience.

**3 · Hear the family and read the emotional climate — 0:35–0:55.** Invite each member's perspective and validate it. Listen for **expressed emotion** — criticism, hostility, and emotional over-involvement — without correcting it in the moment. De-shame: frame symptoms and behavior as illness and regulation, not character or fault. This is the phase that most changes outcomes; protect the time for it.

**4 · Problem-solve and plan — 0:55–1:20.** Move to concrete, shared goals. Agree on roles, on a few communication skills to try at home, on early warning signs and what to do about them, on the crisis and safety plan, and on the discharge logistics (medications, follow-up, transport, supports). Keep it specific and realistic.

**5 · Summarize and close — 1:20–1:30.** Recap the plan in the family's own words, hand over a written version, name the next contact and follow-up, and answer remaining questions. End with one realistic, hopeful next step.

## The stance that makes it work

Multipartiality over neutrality — you can hold several truths at once. Take the **translator** role (structure ↔ control, anger ↔ fear). Keep a **de-shaming** lens (behavior as regulation, not character). Coach **influence, not control.** With an acutely psychotic or highly distressed patient, keep it brief, concrete, and low-stimulation — interpretation waits.

## Document it

Chart who attended, the consent and disclosure limits, the shared understanding reached, the agreed plan and roles, the warning signs and crisis plan, and the follow-up. A good note makes the meeting durable.

## Common pitfalls

Letting information-sharing eat the whole meeting; correcting expressed emotion instead of understanding it; promising what the system can't deliver; running the meeting *at* the family rather than *with* them; and convening so late that there's no time to act on what you learn.

# Family Therapy Modalities Commonly Used Inpatient

> **Source:** adapted from Dr. Moss's Family Therapy Inpatient Didactic ("The Toolbox" slide). Educational; fictional composites only, no PHI.

**The frame:** most inpatient family work is **brief, psychoeducational, and systemic** — you rarely complete therapy on the unit. The goal is to *start the work and secure the handoff.* Knowing the toolbox lets you **match the modality to the family** in front of you.

## The toolbox — match the tool to the family

| Modality | Core moves | Best inpatient use |
|---|---|---|
| **Family psychoeducation / multifamily groups** *(McFarlane)* | Structured illness education + coping & communication skills | The default, highest-evidence approach — start illness education and a shared warning-signs plan |
| **Behavioral family therapy** *(Falloon)* | Communication + problem-solving skills training; lowers expressed emotion | Coach one concrete communication or problem-solving skill before discharge |
| **Structural** *(Minuchin)* | Realign roles, subsystems, and boundaries; use enactment | Re-clarify roles when a parent/partner is over- or under-functioning |
| **Bowenian / intergenerational** | Differentiation, triangles, over-/under-functioning; genogram-driven | A quick genogram to see the pattern across generations and triangles |
| **Emotionally Focused Therapy (EFT)** *(Johnson)* | Attachment-based; de-escalate the negative cycle, rebuild the bond (couples) | Name and soften the pursue–withdraw cycle in a distressed couple |
| **CRAFT** (Community Reinforcement & Family Training) | The family changes its **own** behavior to invite treatment — no blame | Substance use: give the family concrete, blame-free moves that pull toward care |
| **CBT-informed / reduce accommodation** *(SPACE; ERP)* | Coach the family to stop accommodating anxiety/OCD; support graded exposure | Anxiety/OCD: identify and dial back family accommodation |
| **DBT-informed family skills** | Validation + limits; reduce invalidation; manage high-intensity dyads | Borderline-pattern crises: pair validation with consistent limits |
| **Solution-focused / single-session** | Amplify existing strengths in one high-yield consultation | When you get only one meeting — make it count on strengths and next steps |
| **Open Dialogue / network meetings** | Rapid, transparent network meetings during acute crisis | First-episode/crisis: convene the network early and transparently |

*Also commonly cited for bipolar maintenance: **Family-Focused Therapy (Miklowitz)** — psychoeducation + communication + problem-solving, mood-stabilizing alongside medication.*

## Why it's worth the friction — the evidence in numbers
- **OR 0.18** — family psychoeducation led 11 models for 12-month relapse prevention (Rodolico et al., *Lancet Psychiatry* 2022; 90 RCTs, >10,000 patients). **Dose matters:** ≤2 sessions is ineffective; NICE recommends ≥10 sessions over ≥3 months.
- **NNT 7** — treat seven families to prevent one relapse (Pharoah, *Cochrane* 2010; 53 RCTs).
- **OR 0.56 (95% CI 0.43–0.74)** — in bipolar disorder, adjunctive manualized psychotherapies reduced recurrence vs. control; **family/group-format psychoeducation was the standout for recurrence (OR 0.12, 0.02–0.94)**, and **family/conjoint therapy improved treatment retention (OR 0.46, 0.26–0.82)** and helped stabilize depressive symptoms (SMD −0.46, not significant) (Miklowitz et al., *JAMA Psychiatry* 2021; 39 RCTs) — the evidence anchor for Family-Focused Therapy above.
- **≈ 3×** — family involvement during admission nearly triples the odds of 7-day follow-up (Haselden 2019).
- **Reaches a minority of families** — structured family work is delivered in only ~0–53% of cases (historically <10%), and the main barrier is **clinician avoidance, not patient refusal** (Dixon 1999; Eassom 2014).
- **Expressed emotion:** high-EE roughly **quintuples relapse odds (OR ~4.87)**, while family *warmth* is protective (OR ~0.35) (Ma et al. 2021, 33 cohorts); Butzlaff & Hooley (*Arch Gen Psychiatry* 1998) established the EE–relapse link. Family climate is *modifiable* — meetings help lower it.

## The stance that makes any modality work
Multipartiality over neutrality ("not a courtroom; multiple truths") · the **translator** role (structure ↔ control; anger ↔ fear) · a **de-shaming** lens (behavior as regulation, not character) · coach **influence, not control**. With an acutely psychotic patient, keep it brief, concrete, low-stimulation — interpretation waits.
