# Case of the Week — Resident Edition
## Major Depressive Disorder: Treatment Selection, Sequencing & Augmentation

**Date:** 2026-07-20
**Learner level:** Psychiatry residents (PGY 1–4). Assumes DSM-5-TR fluency.
**Format:** ~20–30 min discussion. No required pre-reading.
**Citations:** Based on articles retrieved from PubMed. Full reference list at the end. All patient details are **synthetic and de-identified**.

---

### The Case (learner-facing stem)

A 41-year-old woman is referred to the outpatient clinic for a recurrent major depressive episode, moderate-to-severe, without psychotic features. This is her **third lifetime episode**. Two prior episodes remitted on sertraline. The current episode has lasted ~10 weeks despite the patient's PCP restarting **sertraline, now titrated to 200 mg/day for the past 7 weeks**.

Current symptoms: depressed mood, anhedonia, initial and terminal insomnia, psychomotor slowing, guilt, impaired concentration, and 4 kg weight loss. She endorses passive suicidal ideation without plan or intent. No history of mania or hypomania on structured review; no psychotic features; no active substance use disorder (occasional alcohol). Medical history: migraine, mild obesity (BMI 31), no cardiac disease. She is worried about weight gain and sexual side effects and asks whether "there's a genetic test that tells you which one will work."

Baseline QIDS-C is 15 (moderate). TSH, CBC, CMP unremarkable. She is engaged, works full-time, and has good social support.

---

### Guided Discussion Questions

**Q1. Is this an adequate first-line trial, and how do you stage her treatment resistance?**
*Teaching point:* Sertraline 200 mg × 7 weeks is an **adequate dose and duration** — this is a genuine non-response, not pseudoresistance. Because it's her first adequately dosed antidepressant *this* episode, she is at the threshold of **Stage I treatment resistance**; a second adequate failure would meet the common ≥2-trial definition of **treatment-resistant depression (TRD)**. Probe residents on the limits of TRD staging systems (Thase-Rush, Maudsley) — heterogeneous definitions complicate the augmentation-trial literature and generalizability of network meta-analyses [5,6].

**Q2. Switch, augment, or optimize — how does the evidence adjudicate, and what does VAST-D actually show?**
*Teaching point:* She is already near-maximal on sertraline, so "optimize" is largely exhausted. The **VAST-D** RCT (n=1,522, predominantly male VA sample) compared switch-to-bupropion vs. augment-with-bupropion vs. augment-with-aripiprazole after ≥1 failed antidepressant: **aripiprazole augmentation** yielded higher remission (28.9% vs. 22.3%; RR 1.30, 95% CI 1.05–1.60) and response (74.3%) than switching, but with more akathisia, somnolence, and weight gain [2]. Key nuances to draw out: modest absolute benefit (~6.6% remission difference), the male-predominant sample limits generalizability to this patient, and the weight-gain signal is directly relevant given her BMI and stated concerns.

**Q3. Rank augmentation agents by evidence, and reason about mechanism.**
*Teaching point:* Network meta-analyses of augmentation in TRD support **atypical antipsychotics** (aripiprazole, quetiapine, brexpiprazole, cariprazine) and **lithium** as among the better-evidenced options, with esketamine now a distinct adjunct [6,7,8]. Mechanistic framing:
- **Aripiprazole/brexpiprazole/cariprazine** — D2/D3 partial agonism + 5-HT1A partial agonism/5-HT2A antagonism; lower metabolic burden than olanzapine/quetiapine but **akathisia** is dose-limiting.
- **Quetiapine XR** — 5-HT2A antagonism and, via norquetiapine, NET inhibition; sedating, metabolically active.
- **Lithium** — augments serotonergic neurotransmission; the classic augmentation agent with anti-suicidal signal, but requires monitoring and has a narrow therapeutic index.
- **T3 (liothyronine)** — mechanism uncertain (possibly enhances cortical serotonergic tone); modest evidence, well tolerated.
Given her obesity and weight concern, a **low-metabolic-risk partial agonist** (aripiprazole/brexpiprazole/cariprazine) or **lithium** is more rational than quetiapine or mirtazapine.

**Q4. She asks about pharmacogenomic testing. What do you tell her, and what do the data support?**
*Teaching point:* The **PRIME Care** RCT found that pharmacogenomic testing modestly influenced prescribing and produced a **small, time-limited improvement in remission** — the effect was statistically detectable but clinically modest and attenuated over follow-up [9]. The **CPIC guideline** provides actionable dosing guidance where a genotype is *already known* (e.g., **CYP2C19** and **CYP2D6** metabolizer status for SSRIs — poor metabolizers may need dose reduction; ultrarapid metabolizers may underexpose) but does not endorse routine pre-emptive testing to *select* the drug class [10]. Bottom line for the patient: testing can refine dosing and flag metabolism outliers, but it is not a validated "which drug will work" oracle; shared decision-making about efficacy vs. tolerability trade-offs remains primary.

**Q5. When and how do you deploy esketamine/ketamine, and where does it sit in sequencing?**
*Teaching point:* Intranasal **esketamine** is FDA-approved as adjunct for TRD and for MDD with acute suicidal ideation/behavior. A 2025 systematic review/meta-analysis supports **efficacy against depressive symptoms and, in the acute setting, suicidality**, with dissociation, sedation, and transient blood-pressure elevation as the main tolerability issues and REMS-mandated monitoring [8]. Discuss sequencing: esketamine is typically reserved after ≥2 oral failures, requires in-clinic administration/observation, and does not replace safety planning. Contrast with racemic IV ketamine (off-label, rapid but access/durability limits).

**Q6. Integrate psychotherapy and relapse prevention into the plan.**
*Teaching point:* For a recurrent (3rd-episode) patient, **relapse prevention is a primary goal**, not an afterthought. The **sequential model** — adding structured psychotherapy (CBT, well-being therapy, MBCT) after pharmacologic response to target residual symptoms — reduces relapse/recurrence in meta-analysis [4]. Residual insomnia and anhedonia are high-yield relapse predictors worth targeting directly. Plan for **maintenance pharmacotherapy** given ≥3 episodes (many guidelines favor indefinite maintenance after ≥3 recurrences).

**Q7. Tailor the regimen to *this* patient's side-effect priorities.**
*Teaching point:* She flags **weight** and **sexual dysfunction**. Systematic side-effect mapping supports **personalized prescribing** [3]. Practical moves: prefer low-metabolic-risk augmentation (partial agonists) or lithium over quetiapine/mirtazapine; if sexual dysfunction is the SSRI-driven complaint on switch, consider **bupropion** (weight-neutral, low sexual dysfunction, but lowers seizure threshold and can worsen anxiety/insomnia — note her insomnia) or **mirtazapine** trade-off (helps sleep/appetite but causes weight gain). The art is negotiating the efficacy–tolerability frontier explicitly with her.

**Q8 (Safety).** She has **passive SI without plan/intent**. Structure the assessment and disposition.
*Teaching point:* Conduct a structured risk assessment (ideation, intent, plan, access to means, protective factors, prior attempts, recent losses), formulate **acute vs. chronic risk**, and build a **collaborative safety plan** with means-safety counseling. Consider lithium's anti-suicidal evidence when weighing augmentation. Keep the teaching oriented to recognition, formulation, and escalation — not method detail. Reassess risk at each visit during cross-titration.

---

### Ranked Differential / Diagnostic Reconsideration

1. **MDD, recurrent, moderate-severe, unipolar** — best fit; confirm no missed hypomania (collateral, mood chart) before committing to aggressive antidepressant augmentation.
2. **Bipolar II depression** — reconsider given recurrent episodes and treatment resistance; a missed bipolar diathesis reframes the entire strategy (mood stabilizer/atypical over antidepressant monotherapy).
3. **MDD with mixed features** — subthreshold manic symptoms alter drug choice and antidepressant risk.
4. **Persistent depressive disorder with intermittent MDE ("double depression")** — affects chronicity framing and maintenance planning.
5. **Contributory medical/substance factors** — reassess alcohol quantity, sleep apnea (obesity, insomnia, fatigue), and occult hypothyroidism trends despite a normal TSH.

---

### Workup & Management

**Workup / assessment:** Confirm adequacy of prior trials (dose, duration, adherence — verify via pharmacy refill history); structured mania screen and mood charting; measurement-based care (QIDS/PHQ-9 at each visit); metabolic baseline (weight, BMI, lipids, HbA1c) before an atypical antipsychotic; ECG if considering agents with QT liability; pregnancy planning discussion; consider genotype only if it will change dosing decisions.

**Management sequence for this patient:**
1. **Augment** the (adequately dosed) sertraline with a **low-metabolic-risk agent** — aripiprazole/brexpiprazole/cariprazine or lithium — chosen collaboratively around her weight and sexual-function priorities [2,6,7].
2. If augmentation fails or is not tolerated → **switch class** (e.g., SNRI or bupropion), or escalate to **esketamine** adjunct given emerging TRD status and passive SI [8].
3. Add **structured psychotherapy** (sequential model) for residual symptoms and relapse prevention [4].
4. **Reserve ECT** for severe, refractory, catatonic, psychotic, or high-acuity presentations; discuss rTMS as a non-pharmacologic option for moderate TRD.
5. **Maintenance:** given ≥3 lifetime episodes, plan long-term/indefinite maintenance and monitor for residual-symptom relapse predictors.

---

### Facilitator Notes (not for the learner handout)

- **Time:** ~30 min. Anchor on Q2–Q4 (switch/augment adjudication, agent ranking, and the honest limits of pharmacogenomics) — these separate resident-level from student-level reasoning.
- **Evidence caveats to surface:** VAST-D's male-predominant VA sample [2]; heterogeneity and shifting TRD definitions across augmentation NMAs [6,7]; pharmacogenomics' modest, attenuating effect in PRIME Care and the distinction between drug *selection* and dose *refinement* per CPIC [9,10]; esketamine's REMS/access and durability questions [8].
- **Metabolic stewardship:** use her BMI to make the trade-offs concrete — this is where "match the drug to the patient" [3] becomes a real prescribing decision, not a platitude.
- **Bipolarity vigilance:** recurrent + treatment-resistant depression should always re-trigger a bipolar rule-out before piling on antidepressant augmentation.
- **Safety framing (Q8):** structured formulation, means-safety, and escalation only — no method detail. Reassess across cross-titration.
- **Optional extension:** number-needed-to-treat vs. number-needed-to-harm for aripiprazole augmentation; how to present that trade-off to a patient in plain language.

# Case of the Week — Bipolar Mania: Recognition & Acute Management
### Resident Version (Advanced / Guideline- and Evidence-Forward)

**Date:** 2026-07-20
**Learner level:** Psychiatry residents (PGY 1–4). Assumes DSM-5-TR fluency.
**Format:** ~20–30 min case conference. No pre-reading required.
**Citations:** Based on articles retrieved from PubMed (see references). All patient details are **synthetic and de-identified** — a teaching composite, not a real patient.

---

## Learner-Facing Case Stem

A 24-year-old man is brought to the ED by his roommate after ~5 days of escalating behavioral change. Sleep has collapsed to 1–2 hours nightly without daytime fatigue; he describes his mood as "euphoric and unstoppable" but becomes sharply irritable when redirected. Speech is pressured with clang associations and flight of ideas; thought content includes a grandiose, fixed belief that he has solved the city's transit crisis and been "chosen" to implement it. He has spent beyond his means launching multiple ventures and has been sending pressured 4 a.m. messages to acquaintances.

On exam he is hyperkinetic, hyperverbal, and intrusive, with labile affect and impaired insight/judgment. No fever; HR 104, otherwise normal vitals. He denies substance use and takes no medications. Collateral: a discrete major depressive episode ~12 months ago (≈2 months of anergia, hypersomnia, anhedonia, passive death wishes, no treatment) and a maternal aunt with bipolar I disorder.

Consider: he mentions that during this episode he has also felt "wired but on edge," and at times tearful within the same hour — raising the question of mixed features.

---

## Guided Discussion Questions

**Q1. Establish the DSM-5-TR diagnosis and specifiers. What is the minimum evidence needed to commit to bipolar I?**
*Teaching point:* Criterion A for mania requires a distinct period (**≥1 week, or any duration if hospitalization is required**) of abnormally elevated/expansive/irritable mood **AND** persistently increased goal-directed activity or energy, with ≥3 (or ≥4 if mood is only irritable) B-criteria symptoms and marked impairment/psychosis/hospitalization. **A single manic episode is diagnostic of bipolar I** regardless of prior depression. Push residents to specify: **with psychotic features** (mood-congruent grandiose delusion), and to interrogate the **"with mixed features"** specifier given the intra-episodic tearfulness/dysphoria — mixed features carry higher suicidality and switch risk and shape drug selection. Note DSM-5-TR retains mania as diagnostically primary and folds prior "mixed episode" into the specifier framework.

**Q2. Build the differential beyond the obvious, including secondary mania.**
*Teaching point:* Beyond stimulant intoxication and hyperthyroidism, residents should generate: **antidepressant/steroid/dopaminergic-induced mania**, **secondary ("organic") mania** from frontal/temporal or right-hemisphere lesions, MS, epilepsy, HIV/neurosyphilis, autoimmune/limbic encephalitis (e.g., anti-NMDA-R), and delirium. Late/atypical age of onset, focal neuro findings, or a fluctuating sensorium should lower the threshold for **neuroimaging, LP, and autoimmune/infectious workup**. Also weigh **schizoaffective disorder** (requires ≥2 weeks of psychosis absent a mood episode) and cluster-B/ADHD overlaps.

**Q3. What is your workup, and how does it gate pharmacology?**
*Teaching point:* Baseline: UDS, TSH, CBC, CMP (renal/hepatic), **beta-hCG in patients of childbearing potential**; ECG when QT-prolonging antipsychotics or high-risk profiles are in play; consider ammonia if valproate encephalopathy is suspected later. **Renal function anchors lithium** dosing/safety; **hepatic function and CBC anchor valproate**; **pregnancy status is decisive** — both lithium (Ebstein anomaly risk, though lower than historically cited) and especially **valproate (neural-tube defects, ~9–11% major malformation and dose-dependent neurodevelopmental harm)** are avoided when pregnancy is possible.

**Q4. Monotherapy vs. combination for acute mania — what does the evidence say, and how do you choose?**
*Teaching point:* **CANMAT/ISBD 2018** first-line monotherapy: **lithium, quetiapine, divalproex, asenapine, aripiprazole, paliperidone, risperidone, cariprazine**; first-line **combinations** = lithium or divalproex **+** aripiprazole/asenapine/quetiapine/risperidone [1]. **Kishi et al. network meta-analysis (72 double-blind RCTs, n≈16,442)**: aripiprazole, asenapine, carbamazepine, cariprazine, haloperidol, lithium, olanzapine, paliperidone, quetiapine, risperidone, tamoxifen, valproate, and ziprasidone all beat placebo for response; however, only **aripiprazole, olanzapine, quetiapine, and risperidone** had better acceptability (all-cause discontinuation) than placebo, and **topiramate was worse** [2]. Practical drivers: **symptom severity and psychosis** (favor combination and an antipsychotic), **mixed features** (favor SGAs/valproate over lithium; avoid antidepressants), **prior response**, **metabolic/QTc/EPS risk tolerance**, and **maintenance intent**. Combination therapy generally yields faster/greater response at the cost of tolerability.

**Q5. Where do lithium and valproate each earn their place — mechanistically and by evidence?**
*Teaching point:* **Lithium**: unique **anti-suicidal signal** and robust maintenance/anti-manic efficacy; mechanisms implicate **inhibition of GSK-3β and inositol monophosphatase** with downstream neurotrophic effects. **Valproate**: an overview of systematic reviews found valproate superior to placebo across all phases, comparable to lithium on most acute-mania outcomes, similar to quetiapine, but **less effective than risperidone**, with conflicting head-to-head data vs. olanzapine [5]. **Carbamazepine** is effective for mania but a strong **CYP inducer** (autoinduction, drug–drug interactions) and generally lower-tier. Emphasize serum-level–guided dosing and the interaction landscape (NSAIDs/thiazides/ACE-I raise lithium; valproate inhibits lamotrigine metabolism — doubling lamotrigine levels).

**Q6. Address agitation and involuntary treatment in a way that is safe and rights-respecting.**
*Teaching point:* Stepwise, least-restrictive: environmental de-escalation → offered oral SGA ± benzodiazepine → IM options if imminent risk. Residents should articulate **capacity assessment, civil-commitment criteria, and the ethics/documentation of involuntary medication** for a patient whose insight is impaired by illness. Keep dangerousness discussion oriented to **recognition, escalation, and safety planning** — not method detail. Flag heightened suicide risk in **mixed features** and post-mania depressive switch.

**Q7. Design the maintenance plan and adherence strategy from day one.**
*Teaching point:* Agents effective acutely are generally continued into maintenance [1]. **Kishi et al. maintenance NMA (41 RCTs, n≈9,821)**: most active treatments beat placebo for any-episode recurrence; **quetiapine, lithium, olanzapine, aripiprazole+valproate** among those reducing depressive relapse, while lamotrigine is stronger for **depression prevention than mania** [3]. A real-world cohort showed **guideline-concordant first-line regimens cut 30-day rehospitalization (OR 0.21) and any readmission (OR 0.39)** vs. no treatment [4] — a compelling lever for shared decision-making, LAI consideration in poor adherence, psychoeducation, and relapse-signature planning.

**Q8. What are you monitoring longitudinally, and how do you sequence if first-line fails?**
*Teaching point:* Track **serum levels (lithium, valproate), renal/thyroid/parathyroid (lithium), CBC/LFTs (valproate), metabolic panel/weight/lipids and EPS/QTc (antipsychotics)**. For inadequate response: optimize dose/level and adherence, then **switch within or across first-line classes** or **move mono→combination**; second/third-line options (e.g., ECT for severe, treatment-refractory, catatonic, or pregnancy-relevant mania) per CANMAT/ISBD [1]. Reinforce that **lamotrigine and antidepressant monotherapy are not acute-mania treatments** [1,5].

---

## Ranked Differential

1. **Bipolar I disorder, current episode manic, severe with psychotic features; assess "with mixed features" specifier** — the anchor diagnosis.
2. **Substance-induced (stimulant) or medication-induced mania** — cocaine/methamphetamine, steroids, dopaminergics, antidepressant switch; exclude with UDS/history.
3. **Secondary/"organic" mania** — CNS lesion (frontal/right-hemisphere), MS, epilepsy, HIV/neurosyphilis, autoimmune (anti-NMDA-R) encephalitis; escalate workup if atypical onset or focal signs.
4. **Primary psychotic disorder (schizoaffective/schizophrenia)** — pursue if psychosis persists ≥2 weeks independent of mood; episodic mood-congruent course argues against here.
5. **Hyperthyroidism/thyrotoxicosis** — TSH screen; agitation, insomnia, tachycardia overlap.
6. **Delirium** — fluctuating attention/sensorium; less likely with clear consciousness and normal vitals/exam, but always considered for acute neuropsychiatric change.

---

## Workup & Management Summary

**Workup:** UDS; TSH; CBC; CMP (renal + hepatic); **beta-hCG if applicable**; ECG when using QT-prolonging agents; neuroimaging/LP/autoimmune-infectious panel if age/onset/exam atypical.

**Acute management:**
- **Safety & legal:** dangerousness and capacity assessment; least-restrictive containment; civil commitment/involuntary medication where criteria are met.
- **Discontinue** antidepressants/stimulants; correct precipitants.
- **Pharmacology:** first-line monotherapy (lithium/divalproex/first-line SGA) for moderate mania; **lithium or divalproex + SGA** for severe or psychotic mania [1,2]; agent choice individualized by mixed features, metabolic/QTc/EPS risk, pregnancy status, and maintenance intent.
- **Agitation:** oral SGA ± benzodiazepine, IM if imminent risk.
- **Refractory/severe/catatonic/pregnancy-relevant:** consider **ECT** [1].

**Maintenance & monitoring:** continue effective acute agent [1,3]; serum-level–guided dosing; longitudinal renal/thyroid/metabolic/hepatic/CBC/QTc surveillance; psychoeducation, adherence supports (consider LAI), relapse-signature planning; guideline concordance improves rehospitalization outcomes [4].

---

## Facilitator Notes (not for the learner handout)

- **Objectives:** (1) apply DSM-5-TR mania criteria including required increased-activity criterion and the mixed-features specifier; (2) generate a robust differential including secondary mania and know when to image/LP; (3) reason from the acute-mania and maintenance network meta-analyses to individualized drug choice; (4) integrate lithium/valproate/carbamazepine mechanism, interactions, and teratogenicity; (5) manage agitation and involuntary treatment ethically; (6) build a maintenance/monitoring plan.
- **Debate-worthy tensions:** monotherapy vs. combination (speed/efficacy vs. tolerability); lithium's anti-suicidal advantage vs. practical monitoring burden; when atypical presentation mandates a neuro workup; LAI timing.
- **Evidence nuance to surface:** in Kishi 2021, breadth of "efficacious" agents is wide, but the **acceptability** filter narrows practical first choices to aripiprazole/olanzapine/quetiapine/risperidone [2]; topiramate underperforms and is not an antimanic agent.
- **Pitfalls to correct:** treating mixed features with antidepressants; lamotrigine for acute mania; valproate in patients who could become pregnant; neglecting capacity documentation.
- **Safety framing:** all suicide/dangerousness content stays at recognition/escalation/safety-planning level.

## LEARNER-FACING STEM

You are the psychiatry resident on ED consult. A 74-year-old man is in a corner room, physically escalating: he is intermittently pulling at a peripheral IV, attempting to leave, and swinging at staff, interspersed with periods of drowsiness. He was "himself" 48 hours ago per family. History: hypertension, BPH (started **oxybutynin** one week ago), an alcohol-use history that the family is "not sure about," and possible mild cognitive impairment. He complains intermittently of dysuria.

Exam (obtained in fragments): **inattentive** (cannot complete "days of the week backward," distractible), disoriented to time/place, thought content notable for transient paranoia ("they're poisoning me"), no clear sustained hallucinatory behavior. Fluctuating level of arousal. Vitals: **T 38.3 °C, HR 108, BP 162/94, RR 20, SpO₂ 96% RA.** Mild tremor. Exam is limited by agitation; no clear focal deficit when calm. Fingerstick glucose 122. The ED attending has written for "haloperidol 5 IM + lorazepam 2 IM now" and asks you to weigh in before it's given.

---

## DISCUSSION QUESTIONS

1. **Apply DSM-5-TR: what makes this delirium rather than a primary psychotic or mood episode, and how would you subtype it? What single bedside sign carries the most diagnostic weight, and which validated instruments operationalize it in the ED/ICU?**

2. **The ordered combination is haloperidol 5 mg + lorazepam 2 mg IM. Critique this order specifically for *this* patient. Where does the benzodiazepine help, where does it harm, and how does the "not sure about alcohol" history change your calculus?**

3. **Build the mechanistic differential for the *agitation itself*. Contrast the neuropharmacology of (a) antipsychotic-responsive agitation, (b) GABAergic withdrawal states, (c) anticholinergic delirium, and (d) sympathomimetic/dissociative toxidromes — and how each drives drug selection.**

4. **Compare first-line agents by mechanism and safety: high-potency typical antipsychotics, second-generation agents (olanzapine, ziprasidone, risperidone), droperidol, and IM ketamine. Address the QTc question, the droperidol black-box history, and the specific hazard of combining IM olanzapine with a parenteral benzodiazepine.**

5. **What does the controlled evidence actually show about antipsychotics *treating* delirium (as opposed to transiently managing agitation)? Cite the trial data and state how it should change your framing to the primary team.**

6. **Structure the precipitant workup with pretest reasoning. Which "can't-miss" causes must be excluded before you accept "UTI-precipitated delirium," and when do you escalate to LP, EEG, or neuroimaging?**

7. **Design the restraint-minimization and monitoring plan, including QTc/telemetry, delirium precautions, and the handoff to the admitting team. What are your explicit targets and de-escalation-of-treatment criteria?**

## FACILITATOR NOTES *(not for distribution to learners before discussion)*

### Teaching point by question

1. **DSM-5-TR framing & subtyping.** Delirium requires: (A) a disturbance in **attention and awareness**; (B) **acute onset** and **fluctuating** course; (C) an **additional cognitive disturbance** (memory, orientation, language, perception); (D) not better explained by another neurocognitive disorder and not in the context of severely reduced arousal such as coma; and (E) evidence of a **causal medical/substance etiology**. Against a primary psychotic/mood episode: the *acuity, fluctuation, and attentional collapse with clouded consciousness* are the discriminators — primary psychosis and mania preserve level of consciousness and gross attention. Subtype here: **hyperactive/mixed** (he oscillates between combative and drowsy); the easily-missed **hypoactive** subtype is more common in older adults and carries worse prognosis. Highest-weight bedside sign: **inattention** (e.g., months-of-year backward, digit span, "days of week backward"). Validated tools: **CAM** and its derivatives — **CAM-ICU**, **3D-CAM**, brief-CAM/Delirium Triage Screen for the ED, the **4AT**, and ultrabrief screens — all built around demonstrating acute-onset inattention [1,3].

2. **Critique of haloperidol 5 + lorazepam 2 IM.** Two separate problems. (i) **Dose:** 5 mg haloperidol IM is aggressive for a 74-year-old — geriatric dosing favors substantially lower (e.g., ~0.25–1 mg) titrated, given age-related pharmacodynamic sensitivity, EPS/akathisia risk (akathisia can *look like* worsening agitation), and QTc. (ii) **The benzodiazepine is the higher-stakes error:** in delirium *not* due to withdrawal, benzodiazepines **deepen and prolong** the confusional state (pro-delirogenic via GABA-A potentiation, worsening the attentional/arousal disturbance) and can **disinhibit/paradoxically worsen agitation** in older adults — the umbrella-review signal is that midazolam and benzodiazepines carry disproportionate sedation/respiratory risk in the elderly [4]. **BUT** the "not sure about alcohol" history flips this: if this is **alcohol or sedative-hypnotic withdrawal**, benzodiazepines are *first-line and lifesaving*, and antipsychotics alone are inadequate (they lower seizure threshold and don't treat the GABA deficit). The resident move: **do not give the fixed combination reflexively** — first stratify withdrawal risk (history, prior DTs/seizures, autonomic hyperarousal, tremor), check a withdrawal-oriented exam, and let etiology drive the agent. Tremor + tachycardia + hypertension + fever here are genuinely ambiguous between sepsis/UTI and early withdrawal — that ambiguity is the point.

3. **Mechanistic differential for the agitation.**
   - **(a) Antipsychotic-responsive agitation** (delirium-associated psychosis, primary psychotic agitation): driven by dopaminergic dysregulation; **D2 antagonism** reduces the target symptom. High-potency agents (haloperidol) give minimal anticholinergic/hypotensive load but more EPS/QTc; SGAs add 5-HT2A antagonism with more sedation/metabolic effect.
   - **(b) GABAergic withdrawal** (alcohol, benzodiazepines): a hyperadrenergic, **GABA-underactive/glutamate-overactive** state — treat by **restoring GABA tone with benzodiazepines** (or phenobarbital); antipsychotics do not address the mechanism and lower seizure threshold.
   - **(c) Anticholinergic delirium** (relevant here — **oxybutynin**!): central muscarinic blockade → the classic **"mad as a hatter"** toxidrome (mydriasis, dry flushed skin, urinary retention, hyperthermia, tachycardia). Management is supportive, remove the offending agent; **benzodiazepines** for agitation; **physostigmine** only in select, clearly anticholinergic-toxic cases with appropriate monitoring — antipsychotics with anticholinergic properties (e.g., low-potency agents) can *worsen* it.
   - **(d) Sympathomimetic / dissociative toxidromes** (stimulants; PCP/ketamine): catecholaminergic or NMDA-mediated; **benzodiazepines** are the agitation anchor; antipsychotics are adjunctive and carry hyperthermia/QTc concerns. Mechanistic clarity here prevents the single most common error: reaching for the same drug regardless of cause.

4. **Agent comparison.**
   - **High-potency typicals (haloperidol):** potent D2 blockade, hemodynamically neutral, workhorse for delirium-associated agitation; watch **dose-dependent QTc prolongation**, EPS/akathisia, and (rare) NMS.
   - **SGAs:** *olanzapine* (fast, sedating; strong efficacy but more sedation), *ziprasidone* (IM, good tolerability, QTc caution), *risperidone* (oral/ODT, less sedation) — the umbrella review found **IM ziprasidone** faster-onset and better tolerated than haloperidol, **aripiprazole** effective with less sedation, and **olanzapine ≈ droperidol** for efficacy with droperidol faster to sedation [4].
   - **Droperidol:** rapid, effective ED sedative; the **FDA black-box warning (2001)** for QTc/torsades led to years of disuse, but subsequent ED safety data have supported its use with monitoring — a good resident discussion on how a boxed warning can outrun its evidence.
   - **IM ketamine:** **NMDA-receptor antagonism**; rapid onset for severe/undifferentiated agitation and useful when IV access is impossible, but carries **laryngospasm, hypersalivation, emergence phenomena, hypertension/tachycardia, and intubation risk**; a retrospective cohort found prehospital ketamine for severe agitation was **not** associated with increased psychiatric admission even in patients with schizophrenia, though non-psychiatric admissions in the comparator were largely intubation-driven — i.e., airway risk with deep sedation is real [6]. Ketamine's own toxicity profile (dissociation; with chronic use, cystitis/cholangiopathy) is worth knowing [7].
   - **Specific hazard:** **IM olanzapine + parenteral benzodiazepine** carries a recognized risk of **excessive sedation, respiratory depression, and hypotension** — they should not be co-administered reflexively; separate timing and monitor. QTc bottom line: **get a baseline ECG**, know the patient's QTc and electrolytes (K⁺/Mg²⁺), and avoid stacking QT-prolonging agents.

5. **What the evidence actually shows.** Antipsychotics may **transiently control agitation** but do **not treat the delirium syndrome**: the **MIND-USA** RCT (haloperidol vs. ziprasidone vs. placebo in critically ill delirious patients) found **no difference in days alive without delirium or coma, duration of delirium, or survival** [5]. This aligns with the delirium *Nature Reviews Disease Primers* consensus that **pharmacologic treatments are not effective** and reflect gaps in mechanistic understanding [1], and with geriatric reviews noting antipsychotics are **off-label, second-line, for refractory distressing psychosis/agitation posing imminent risk — with a taper plan** [3]. Framing for the primary team: *"We can use a low-dose antipsychotic to keep him safe, but it won't shorten his delirium — treating the UTI, stopping the oxybutynin, and delirium precautions are what actually fix this."*

6. **Precipitant workup with pretest reasoning.** Anchor on reversible, high-lethality causes first. Core panel: glucose (done), CBC, CMP (Na⁺/Ca²⁺/glucose/renal/hepatic), **UA + culture**, ± blood cultures/lactate if septic-appearing, TSH, B12 if indicated, ECG, medication reconciliation (**anticholinergic burden** — oxybutynin; opioids; sedatives), and a **withdrawal assessment** given the alcohol ambiguity (consider thiamine before glucose loading, ethanol level, prior withdrawal history). **Can't-miss before accepting "UTI delirium":** hypoglycemia/hypoxia (excluded early), **CNS catastrophe** (stroke, ICH, meningoencephalitis), **nonconvulsive status epilepticus**, **Wernicke encephalopathy**, and toxic/metabolic (hyponatremia, hypercalcemia, hepatic/uremic encephalopathy, anticholinergic toxidrome). **Escalate to head CT** for focal signs, trauma, anticoagulation, or delirium without an identified precipitant; **LP** if meningitis/encephalitis is plausible (fever + altered mentation without another source); **EEG** if seizure/NCSE suspected or if unexplained fluctuating encephalopathy persists. "UTI-precipitated delirium" is a reasonable *leading* hypothesis but should not foreclose the search — pyuria is common and incidental in older adults.

7. **Restraint-minimization & monitoring plan.** Targets: a **calm, arousable, redirectable patient** (e.g., RASS around 0 to −1), *not* iatrogenic obtundation that masks the fluctuating exam. Least-restrictive escalation: environment/1:1 sitter → verbal de-escalation → single lowest-effective enteral/IM agent → physical restraints only as time-limited last resort with frequent reassessment and documentation. Monitoring: **baseline and follow-up ECG/QTc**, telemetry if stacking QT agents or high antipsychotic exposure, electrolyte repletion (K⁺/Mg²⁺), continuous pulse oximetry after parenteral sedation, fall precautions. **Delirium precautions:** reorientation, sensory aids, day/night light-sleep cycling, early mobilization, remove tethers/Foley when possible, treat pain/constipation/retention, family presence. **Handoff to admitting team:** explicit problem framing ("hyperactive delirium, ?UTI vs. early withdrawal — reassess"), the medication rationale and *de-escalation criteria* (stop/taper antipsychotic once agitation resolves and precipitant treated; do not continue at discharge), thiamine and withdrawal-monitoring plan, and PCP follow-up for post-delirium cognitive screening [1,2,3].

### Ranked differential (resident depth)
1. **Hyperactive/mixed delirium, multifactorial** — UTI + **anticholinergic (oxybutynin)** + baseline cognitive vulnerability; best overall fit.
2. **Alcohol / sedative-hypnotic withdrawal** — the autonomic signs + "unsure alcohol" history make this a parallel must-exclude; would reprioritize benzodiazepines and thiamine.
3. **Anticholinergic toxidrome** from oxybutynin as a primary driver — check for peripheral antimuscarinic signs (dry skin, mydriasis, retention, hyperthermia).
4. **Sepsis/occult infection beyond the urinary tract** — pneumonia, bacteremia, intra-abdominal.
5. **CNS event / NCSE / CNS infection** — lower prior but high consequence; escalate imaging/LP/EEG if precipitant absent or focal signs emerge.
6. **Metabolic/toxic encephalopathies** — hyponatremia, hypercalcemia, hepatic/uremic, thyroid, Wernicke.
7. **Delirium superimposed on dementia** — vulnerability substrate, not the acute cause.
8. **Primary psychiatric illness (late-onset psychosis/mania)** — diagnosis of exclusion; late-onset psychosis mandates a secondary-cause search rather than presumption of a primary disorder [8].

### One-screen management summary
- **Stabilize/safety:** space, sitter, de-escalation; glucose/SpO₂/vitals; thiamine before glucose if any withdrawal/malnutrition concern.
- **Etiology-driven pharmacology:** *delirium-agitation* → low-dose high-potency antipsychotic, ECG/QTc-aware; *withdrawal* → benzodiazepine/phenobarbital + thiamine; *anticholinergic* → supportive, remove agent, benzodiazepine ± physostigmine in select cases; **avoid reflexive haloperidol+lorazepam combos and IM olanzapine+benzodiazepine co-administration.**
- **Diagnostics:** CBC, CMP, UA/culture, ± cultures/lactate, ECG, med rec, withdrawal assessment; CT/LP/EEG per pretest reasoning.
- **Treat the cause + delirium precautions;** antipsychotic is symptomatic only (MIND-USA) [5].
- **Disposition:** admit; explicit de-escalation-of-treatment criteria; no antipsychotic at discharge by default; PCP cognitive follow-up.

### Safety / escalation orientation
Dangerousness here is **agitation-related risk to self and staff**; keep teaching oriented to recognition, de-escalation, least-restrictive intervention, and escalation — no method detail. If suicidal ideation emerges once the sensorium clears, transition to a formal risk assessment and safety planning with the team.

## PART 1 — LEARNER-FACING CASE

### Clinical stem

A 47-year-old with schizoaffective disorder, bipolar type, is admitted to the medical service from a group home for 3 days of progressive rigidity, fever, and declining responsiveness.

**Recent medication history:**
- **Haloperidol** long-standing; the dose was **increased 6 days ago** for worsening psychosis.
- **Fluoxetine** for depressive symptoms, dose increased at the same visit.
- **Lithium** maintenance (last level, per records, 0.9 mEq/L two months ago).
- A PRN **metoclopramide** was given twice in the last week for nausea.

Over 72 hours the group-home staff noted the patient became "stiff," intermittently confused, then somnolent and mostly mutistic, with rising temperature.

**Vitals:** T 39.6 °C · HR 128 · BP fluctuating 170/100 → 118/70 over minutes · RR 24 · SpO₂ 96% RA

**Exam:**
- **Mental status:** Stuporous, minimally responsive, intermittent mutism; when roused, no clear thought content elicited.
- **Motor:** Generalized **"lead-pipe" rigidity** in all limbs and axial muscles; **no clonus**; **reflexes diminished** and symmetric. Waxy resistance without ratcheting.
- **Autonomic:** Profuse diaphoresis, sialorrhea, labile blood pressure.
- **Pupils:** Normal size and reactivity.
- **Abdomen:** Normoactive bowel sounds.

**Initial labs:** CK **9,800 U/L** · WBC 16.2 ×10⁹/L · AST/ALT mildly elevated · Cr rising from baseline · **serum iron low** · lithium level pending · urine tox negative · acetaminophen/salicylate negative.

### Discussion questions

1. **Frame the differential.** The patient is on *both* a dopamine antagonist and serotonergic agents. Which toxidrome does the constellation favor, and which discrete exam and laboratory features drive you there rather than to the alternative?
2. **Pathophysiology.** Contrast the receptor-level mechanisms of NMS and serotonin syndrome. Why does the *tempo* of onset differ, and how does that map onto this patient's 72-hour course?
3. **Diagnostic criteria.** How would you operationalize the diagnosis? Compare the **Hunter Serotonin Toxicity Criteria** with the DSM-5-TR / consensus approach to NMS, and name the limits of each in a mixed-exposure patient.
4. **Malignant catatonia overlap.** How do NMS and malignant catatonia relate, and how does that overlap change your workup and your threshold for specific treatments (benzodiazepine challenge, ECT)?
5. **Targeted pharmacotherapy.** Justify agent selection — **dantrolene, bromocriptine, amantadine** for NMS vs. **cyproheptadine** for SS — by mechanism. 6. **Contributing agents and de-prescribing.** Which of this patient's medications plausibly contributed, and how do lithium and metoclopramide factor in? What is your medication-reconciliation plan?

## PART 2 — FACILITATOR GUIDE
*(Keep separate from the learner handout.)*

### Formulation
The dominant picture — **subacute onset over days, generalized lead-pipe rigidity, hyporeflexia, hyperthermia, autonomic instability, stupor/mutism, CK ~10,000, leukocytosis, low serum iron** — is **neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS)**, driven by central **D2 blockade** from a recently up-titrated high-potency antipsychotic (haloperidol) and compounded by metoclopramide (a peripheral *and* central D2 antagonist). The serotonergic agents (fluoxetine) are a deliberate red herring: the exam **lacks** the clonus/hyperreflexia and mydriasis that would mark serotonin syndrome. The case is built to force explicit weighing of both, because the two toxidromes overlap and are frequently confused in polypharmacy patients [1][3][7][10].

### Teaching points by question

**Q1 — What tips the balance to NMS.**
Both syndromes share hyperthermia, autonomic instability, and altered mental status, so the **neuromuscular signature and tempo** carry the diagnostic weight [3][7]:
- **Favoring NMS:** "lead-pipe" rigidity, **hyporeflexia**, bradykinesia/akinesis, mutism/stupor, insidious multi-day onset, very high CK, leukocytosis, **low serum iron** (a reproducible NMS-associated finding), normal pupils and bowel sounds.
- **Favoring SS (absent here):** lower-extremity–predominant **clonus and hyperreflexia**, myoclonus, tremor, mydriasis, hyperactive bowel sounds, and onset within hours of a serotonergic change.
The laboratory contrast is useful but not pathognomonic: marked CK elevation, transaminitis, leukocytosis, and low iron cluster with NMS [7].

**Q2 — Mechanism and why tempo differs.**
- **NMS:** acute central **dopamine D2 hypofunction** — in the nigrostriatal pathway (rigidity), hypothalamus (thermodysregulation), and mesocortical/limbic circuits (altered mentation). Because it reflects a *dopaminergic deficit state* evolving as receptor blockade and downstream signaling shift, onset is typically **gradual over 1–3 days** (up to ~2 weeks after initiation/titration) [4][5][6].
- **Serotonin syndrome:** **excess post-synaptic 5-HT2A (and 5-HT1A) agonism.** It behaves like a graded, dose-dependent *toxicity*, so it appears **rapidly — often < 6–24 h** of a serotonergic addition, dose increase, or overdose, and clears quickly once the agent is withdrawn [1][2]. The patient's slow 72-hour build is far more consistent with the dopaminergic mechanism.

**Q3 — Operationalizing the diagnosis.**
- **Hunter Serotonin Toxicity Criteria** (sensitivity ~84%, specificity ~97%; outperform Sternbach) require a serotonergic agent **plus** one of: spontaneous clonus; inducible clonus + agitation/diaphoresis; ocular clonus + agitation/diaphoresis; tremor + hyperreflexia; or hypertonia + T > 38 °C + ocular/inducible clonus [8]. **This patient meets none** — there is no clonus.
- **NMS** has no single validated bedside rule of comparable performance; DSM-5-TR frames it clinically (recent dopamine antagonist exposure or dopaminergic withdrawal + hyperthermia + rigidity + mental-status change + autonomic instability + supportive labs — CK elevation, leukocytosis). International consensus (Delphi) criteria weight these features similarly. **Limit in mixed exposure:** neither instrument was designed to adjudicate a patient on *both* drug classes, so criteria supplement — not replace — bedside reasoning about the neuromuscular phenotype and time course [3][4][5].

**Q4 — Malignant catatonia overlap.**
NMS is widely conceptualized as a **drug-induced form of malignant (lethal) catatonia**; the phenotypes can be clinically indistinguishable — rigidity, hyperthermia, autonomic instability, mutism, waxy features [9]. Practical consequences:
- Screen for **catatonic signs** (mutism, negativism, posturing, waxy flexibility, staring) — present here in part.
- A **lorazepam challenge** is both diagnostic and therapeutic for catatonia and is reasonable early; it is also first-line supportive care in NMS.
- For **refractory NMS or malignant catatonia, ECT** is an established rescue therapy [4][9].
- The overlap is *why* benzodiazepines sit early in the NMS pathway and why a poor benzodiazepine response should prompt escalation rather than reassurance.

**Q5 — Targeted pharmacotherapy by mechanism.**
- **NMS:**
  - **Dantrolene** — ryanodine-receptor antagonist reducing sarcoplasmic calcium release; attenuates rigidity and thermogenesis in severe/hyperthermic cases.
  - **Bromocriptine / amantadine** — restore dopaminergic tone centrally (bromocriptine = D2 agonist; amantadine = dopaminergic + NMDA-antagonist).
  - Evidence is **largely from case series/retrospective data**, not RCTs; discontinuation of the offending agent plus aggressive supportive/ICU care remains the backbone [4][5][6]. Reported incidence ~0.02–0.03% of antipsychotic-exposed patients with mortality ~5.6% in contemporary series [5].
- **SS:** **cyproheptadine** — a 5-HT2A antagonist — for moderate–severe toxicity, on top of benzodiazepines and supportive care [1][2].
- **Pitfall:** if serotonin toxicity is genuinely still on the differential, **giving a dopamine agonist (bromocriptine) is theoretically counterproductive**, and some dopamine agents have their own serotonergic caveats — another reason to commit to the phenotype before layering antidotes. Avoid antipyretics (hyperthermia is muscular, not set-point driven) and avoid physical restraints that increase isometric muscle activity and worsen hyperthermia/rhabdomyolysis [2][4].

**Q6 — Contributing agents and de-prescribing.**
- **Haloperidol** (recently up-titrated) — the primary driver.
- **Metoclopramide** — central D2 antagonist; an under-recognized NMS precipitant and a common "hidden" contributor. Stop it.
- **Fluoxetine** — the serotonergic agent that seeds diagnostic confusion; while it does not fit the phenotype, hold it during the acute event and note its long half-life for any future serotonergic sequencing.
- **Lithium** — can potentiate both neurotoxicity and serotonergic effects; check a level (toxicity can mimic/compound the picture) and hold pending renal recovery, since evolving AKI from rhabdomyolysis will raise levels.
- **Reconciliation plan:** stop all dopamine antagonists, hold serotonergic and lithium agents, obtain levels, and document the event prominently to govern rechallenge.

**Q7 — Recovery and rechallenge.**
NMS typically resolves over **days to ~2 weeks** with agent withdrawal and supportive care — slower than SS, which usually clears in 24–72 h [1][4][5]. For antipsychotic **rechallenge**: wait **at least 2 weeks** after full resolution, choose a **lower-potency or atypical agent** at a low starting dose, titrate slowly, and monitor temperature, CK, and autonomic signs; recurrence can occur at any point after resolution, so shared decision-making and close follow-up are essential [5]. Where feasible, address the psychiatric indication with the lowest-risk effective option and consider ECT if antipsychotics are high-risk.

### Ranked differential diagnosis (resident depth)
1. **Neuroleptic malignant syndrome** — best fit (D2 antagonist up-titration + metoclopramide, subacute onset, lead-pipe rigidity, hyporeflexia, CK ~10k, leukocytosis, low iron).
2. **Malignant catatonia** — may be indistinguishable from NMS; screen catatonic signs, trial lorazepam, consider ECT if refractory [9].
3. **Serotonin syndrome** — on the differential given fluoxetine, but the phenotype (no clonus/hyperreflexia, normal pupils, slow onset) argues against; Hunter criteria not met [8].
4. **Lithium neurotoxicity** — coarse tremor, confusion, ataxia, myoclonus; check level, especially with evolving AKI.
5. **Other drug-induced hyperthermia / mimics** — malignant hyperthermia (anesthetic exposure), anticholinergic toxicity (dry skin, absent bowel sounds), sympathomimetic toxicity, plus **CNS infection, nonconvulsive status epilepticus, thyroid storm, heat stroke, and sepsis** — exclude systematically [6][10].

### Workup & management summary
- **Workup:** CK, CMP (renal/hepatic), CBC, serum iron, coags/DIC panel, lithium level, blood/urine cultures and CNS infection work-up as indicated, ECG, continuous temperature and hemodynamic monitoring; consider EEG if catatonia/NCSE is in question.
- **Immediate management:** **stop all dopamine antagonists** (including metoclopramide); ICU-level supportive care — IV fluids, active cooling, correct electrolytes, protect renal function against rhabdomyolysis.
- **Pharmacotherapy:** benzodiazepine (lorazepam) early (agitation + possible catatonia); **dantrolene** and **bromocriptine/amantadine** for moderate–severe NMS; **ECT** for refractory NMS/malignant catatonia.
- **If the phenotype had been SS:** stop serotonergic agents, benzodiazepines, **cyproheptadine**, ICU with sedation/paralysis if severe.

### Safety note
If any component were an **intentional ingestion**, medical stabilization comes first, followed by psychiatric risk assessment and safety planning once the patient can participate — the teaching emphasis is recognition and escalation, not method detail.
