# 4-Week Rotation Plan


**In one line** — A week-by-week map for the four-week inpatient block: where to focus, what to read, and which skills to demonstrate. It's a scaffold, not a rule — your patients drive the order.

**Week 1 — Foundations & safety** — Orient to the unit and the team; get fluent with the acute workup and the bedside tools. Run suicide and violence risk assessment, agitation/de-escalation, decisional capacity, withdrawal (CIWA-Ar/COWS), delirium, and catatonia (Bush-Francis). Begin carrying your own patients. *Read:* the [Acute & Safety](?page=delirium.md) topics, the bedside tools, and the suicide/violence/agitation domains of [Evidence-Based Inpatient Psychiatry](?page=evidence_inpatient.md).

**Week 2 — Diagnosis & psychopharmacology** — Deepen the diagnostic core (mood, psychosis, anxiety/OCD, substance) and level up prescribing: measurement-based care, sequencing, augmentation, and treatment-resistance. *Read:* the [Core Topics](?page=t_mood.md), the [Psychopharmacology Primer](?page=psychopharm_primer.md) and **[Advanced Psychopharmacology](?page=adv_psychopharm.md)**, and the [Landmark Trials](?page=landmark_trials.md).

**Week 3 — Systems, med-legal & disposition** — Move from the patient to the system: civil commitment and involuntary holds, capacity and consent, defensible documentation, family meetings, and complex disposition. *Read:* **[Inpatient Systems & Med-Legal](?page=systems_medlegal.md)**, [Family & Discharge](?page=exp_family.md), and [Motivational Interviewing](?page=motivational_interviewing.md).

**Week 4 — Integration, supervision & scholarship** — Lead rounds, supervise and teach the student, and consolidate. Pick a case or topic to present. *Read:* **[Supervision, EPAs & Teaching](?page=supervision_teaching.md)**, the **[Psychiatry Canon](?page=canon_200.md)**, and the full [Evidence-Based Inpatient Psychiatry](?page=evidence_inpatient.md).

**Demonstrate across the block (EPAs)** —
- Independently assess and manage an acute psychiatric emergency (suicide, agitation, catatonia, delirium) with appropriate escalation.
- Build and defend a psychopharmacologic plan, including for a treatment-resistant patient.
- Determine and document decisional capacity, and navigate an involuntary hold appropriately.
- Lead a treatment-team round and a family meeting.
- Give structured feedback to, and entrust appropriate tasks to, a medical student.

# Supervision, EPAs & Teaching the Student

**In one line** — On adult inpatient you sit in the middle of the supervision chain: you earn graduated autonomy from your attending while becoming the first-line supervisor for the MS3 — and both directions run on the same skills of explicit expectations, active feedback, and knowing when to ask for help.

**The competency frame** — ACGME organizes residency around six core competencies (patient care, medical knowledge, practice-based learning, interpersonal/communication skills, professionalism, systems-based practice), tracked through psychiatry **Milestones** that describe behaviorally what progression from novice to ready-for-practice looks like. **Entrustable Professional Activities (EPAs)** are the unit of clinical work an attending decides you can be trusted to do — e.g., "perform and document an admission psychiatric evaluation," "manage agitation safely." Entrustment is graded: from *observe only*, to *direct supervision in the room*, to *indirect supervision* (attending available but not present), to *provide oversight to others*. Your job over a four-week block is to move along that gradient on specific activities — not vaguely "get better," but earn indirect supervision on the admission note, the family meeting, the agitation call.

**Using supervision actively** — Supervision is not a status report; it is a learning tool you drive. Come with an agenda and name your question explicitly: "I'm unsure whether this presentation is bipolar depression versus unipolar — can we reason through the discriminators?" beats "Bed 4 is the same." Tell your attending where you want autonomy and where you want eyes on you; calibrated self-disclosure of your learning edges is what lets them safely hand you more. Ask for the entrustment decision out loud: "Would you be comfortable with me running tomorrow's family meeting and you debriefing after?"

**Teaching the MS3** — On day one, orient them: physical geography of the unit, safety norms (where the exits and alarms are, never block the door, the agitation protocol), who's who, and what a good day looks like. Set explicit expectations — how to pre-round, when to present, what you want documented — and assign *real but appropriately scoped* responsibility (own 1–2 patients, draft the note, call collateral). When they present, use the **One-Minute Preceptor**: (1) get a commitment ("What do you think is going on?"), (2) probe for supporting reasoning ("What led you there?"), (3) teach one general principle, (4) reinforce what was done well, (5) correct errors specifically. Model the interview and the difficult conversation rather than only describing them — students learn the mental status exam and de-escalation by watching you do them.

**Feedback that lands** — Make it specific, behavior-based, timely, and actionable; "your suicide risk assessment didn't document protective factors or access to means — let's add those two next time" teaches, "good job" does not. Use **Ask–Tell–Ask**: ask for their self-assessment, tell them your observation and one concrete change, then ask them to plan how they'll apply it. Keep *feedback* (formative, in-the-moment, for growth) distinct from *evaluation* (summative, on the record) — and flag clearly when you're shifting registers. The same model works on the receiving end: solicit feedback on yourself the same way you give it.

**Running the team** — Lead rounds with a predictable structure and a clock; protect time for the sickest and don't let a stable patient consume ten minutes. Use **closed-loop communication** for anything safety-critical — orders, precaution changes, hand-offs read back and confirmed. Build **psychological safety**: the unit functions when nurses, the student, and you can voice a concern or a near-miss without fear, so explicitly invite dissent and thank people for catching things.

**Supervision for the harder parts** — Bring countertransference and difficult cases to supervision by name: the patient who makes you angry, anxious, or want to discharge early is clinical data, not a personal failing. Inpatient psychiatry carries real emotional load; watch yourself for burnout's signs — exhaustion, cynicism/depersonalization, a sense of ineffectiveness — and treat them as a reason to act, not push through. **Asking for help is a competency** (it lives under professionalism and practice-based learning), not a weakness; the unsafe resident is the one who doesn't escalate.

**Documentation & the medicolegal frame** — When you supervise the student, the attending remains the responsible physician and ultimately accountable; your oversight is real but operates under their license. Note co-signature requirements where they apply, document supervision and your own independent assessment rather than simply endorsing a student's note, and remember that "agree with above" over an exam you didn't perform is both a quality and a liability problem.

**What the resident does**
- Negotiate explicit entrustment with the attending: name which EPAs you want to advance and what level of supervision you're asking for.
- Drive each supervision session with a prepared agenda and a named clinical question.
- Orient the MS3 on day one to safety, expectations, and scope; assign real but bounded ownership.
- Teach at the bedside with the One-Minute Preceptor; give feedback via Ask–Tell–Ask.
- Run rounds on a structure and a clock; use closed-loop communication for all safety-critical items.
- Bring countertransference, stuck cases, and your own warning signs to supervision proactively.
- Document your independent assessment and supervision; honor co-signature and attending-of-record rules.

**High-yield pearls**
- "Get a commitment" before you correct — let the learner reason first, then teach to the gap.
- Feedback describes a *behavior* and a *next action*; if it could fit any learner, it's too vague.
- Separate feedback from evaluation out loud — surprise at the summative review is a supervision failure.
- Entrustment is earned by accurate self-disclosure: telling your attending what you *can't* yet do safely is what unlocks autonomy.
- Burnout is a signal to escalate, not a character flaw; the same is true for the student you supervise.
- The attending is the physician of record — your supervision adds a layer, it doesn't transfer the accountability.

**TL;DR (shown above the page text):**

> Use the interview as a circle with the patient's story at the center — covering all 11 domains in whatever order the conversation allows — and always gather the safety trio (suicide, violence, vulnerability) before closing.

**Key points (bulleted card):**

- Ask suicide questions directly: 'Have you had thoughts of killing yourself?' — direct questions do not implant suicidal thoughts, and indirect approaches miss real risk.
- MSE language should be behavioral and observable: write 'affect constricted, minimally reactive, mood-congruent' rather than 'patient appeared depressed' — describe what you saw, not your interpretation.
- Close every interview with a summary in the patient's language, one correction invitation, and one named next step — this closes the loop and builds trust.

**Can't-miss / red-flag line:**

> 'Denies SI/HI — no risk' is not a risk assessment; document what you asked, what the patient reported, and the reasoning that led to your risk level.

**Clinical-workflow narration (per-stage coaching text):**

- **ask** — Open with the patient story, then loop through mood, psychosis, anxiety/trauma, substance use, cognition, medical causes, and safety.
- **mse** — Observe from the first moment: engagement, psychomotor activity, speech, affect range, thought form, perception, cognition, insight, and judgment.
- **safety** — Before closing, ask directly about suicide, violence, grave disability, abuse, and access to means.
- **say** — I will ask some direct safety questions because they help us take good care of you here.
- **collateral** — Ask permission when possible; explain confidentiality limits and why collateral helps establish baseline and safety.
- **rounds** — Report what the patient said, what you observed, what you still need, and what safety questions were answered.
- **exam** — Interview questions test next best question, safety closure, and whether you can distinguish patient report from MSE observation.
- **actions** — Practice guardedness; Open collateral workflow

**Embedded check-for-understanding**

1. *Stem:* During a rounds presentation, a student reports: 'On MSE, mood was depressed and the patient was anxious.' The attending asks for more precise language. Which revision is best?
   - The patient appeared to have a depressed mood and showed signs of anxiety
   - Patient denied euphoria and grandiosity; affect within normal limits
   - Mood: 'really low, like a 2 out of 10' (patient quote); affect constricted, minimally reactive, mood-congruent; no psychomotor agitation **← keyed correct**
   - Patient reported moderate depression and anxiety consistent with major depressive disorder
   - *Rationale:* The MSE separates mood (the patient's subjective report, always quoted in the patient's words) from affect (the clinician's behavioral observation: range, reactivity, congruence). Option 3 shows both correctly. The other options either interpret rather than observe, skip the patient quote, or make a diagnostic statement where a description belongs.

**Working the circle.** Open in the centre with the patient's narrative, then let their words pull you to whichever domain they open; follow that thread, come back to the centre, and pick up the next one. Gather safety the moment the door opens to it — and always before you close. By the end, every section of the circle should be covered, even though the path through it was the patient's, not yours.

Cover all of these — in whatever order the conversation allows:

1. Opening narrative.
2. Current symptoms and timeline.
3. Safety: suicide, self-harm, violence, vulnerability.
4. Psychiatric history.
5. Substance use and withdrawal risk.
6. Medical/neurologic contributors.
7. Medications, adherence, adverse effects.
8. Trauma and developmental context, asked gently.
9. Family, supports, living situation, collateral.
10. Function, strengths, values, discharge barriers.
11. Close with summary and next step.

## High-Yield Opening Questions

- "What was happening before you came to the hospital?"
- "What feels most urgent to you today?"
- "When did this start to feel different from your usual baseline?"
- "What has helped even a little?"
- "Who knows you well enough to notice when you are not yourself?"

## Safety Questions

Ask directly and calmly.

Suicide:

- "Have you wished you would not wake up?"
- "Have you had thoughts of killing yourself?"
- "Have you thought about how you would do it?"
- "Have you taken any steps toward that?"
- "What has stopped you so far?"
- "Do you have access to firearms, stockpiled medications, or other lethal means?"

Violence:

- "Have you felt afraid you might hurt someone?"
- "Has anyone felt unsafe around you recently?"
- "Have you made threats or had thoughts of retaliation?"
- "Do you have access to weapons?"

Vulnerability:

- "Has anyone hurt, threatened, coerced, or exploited you?"
- "Do you feel safe returning to where you were living?"

## Substance And Withdrawal Screen

Ask about:

- Alcohol: amount, frequency, last drink, morning drinking, prior withdrawal, seizures, delirium tremens.
- Benzodiazepines: prescribed or non-prescribed, daily dose, last use.
- Opioids: last use, route, tolerance, withdrawal symptoms, overdose history.
- Stimulants: amount, route, sleep loss, paranoia, crash/depression.
- Cannabis: daily use, potency, psychosis/anxiety connection.

Escalate if there is possible alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal risk.

## Collateral

Ask permission explicitly:

> "Would it be okay if we spoke with someone who knows you well? It helps us
> understand what changed and how to support you after discharge."

If the patient says no:

- Reflect the reason.
- Ask what they worry would happen.
- Offer a limited call with the patient present.
- Escalate to the team if safety requires an exception.

## Trauma-Informed Moves

- Ask permission before sensitive topics.
- Explain why you are asking.
- Give control where possible.
- Avoid pressing for details that are not needed for immediate care.
- Thank the patient for telling you.

Example:

> "I ask everyone some safety and trauma questions because those experiences can
> affect sleep, trust, and discharge planning. You do not have to share details
> today."

## MSE Structure

| Domain | What To Observe Or Ask | Example Language |
|---|---|---|
| Appearance | hygiene, dress, apparent age, distress | "Appears stated age, hospital attire, mildly disheveled" |
| Behavior | cooperation, eye contact, psychomotor activity | "Guarded but cooperative; intermittent eye contact" |
| Speech | rate, rhythm, volume, latency | "Soft, slowed, increased latency" |
| Mood | patient quote | Mood: "empty" |
| Affect | range, reactivity, congruence | "Constricted, mood-congruent, minimally reactive" |
| Thought process | linear, tangential, circumstantial, disorganized | "Tangential but redirectable" |
| Thought content | SI, HI, delusions, obsessions, preoccupations | "Persecutory ideas without fixed delusional conviction" |
| Perception | hallucinations, illusions | "Reports auditory hallucinations; no visual hallucinations" |
| Cognition | orientation, attention, memory when relevant | "Oriented to person/place; impaired attention on months backward" |
| Insight | understanding of illness/situation | "Partial insight into depression; limited insight into alcohol contribution" |
| Judgment | decision-making in context | "Impaired around safety planning; accepts inpatient care" |

## MSE Words To Use Carefully

Avoid vague or stigmatizing words:

- "Manipulative" -> describe the behavior and context.
- "Noncompliant" -> "missed doses because..." or "declined because..."
- "Attention-seeking" -> "sought staff support repeatedly when distressed."
- "Poor historian" -> "timeline limited by disorganization/intoxication/anxiety."
- "Denies" alone -> include what you asked and how risk was reasoned.

## Mini Cognitive Screen

Use when delirium, dementia, intoxication, withdrawal, catatonia, or medical illness is possible.

- Orientation: person, place, date, situation.
- Attention: days of week backward or months backward.
- Fluctuation: "Has this changed during the day?"
- Arousal: alert, drowsy, hypervigilant, fluctuating.

Delirium is a clinical emergency until proven otherwise.

## Closing The Interview

End with:

- Summary in the patient's language.
- One correction invitation.
- One next step.

Example:

> "Let me make sure I understood. The last two weeks have been marked by very
> little sleep, more drinking, and thoughts of not wanting to live, especially
> at night. I may have missed something. What should I correct? The next step is
> that I will share this with the team and we will talk with you about safety
> and sleep today."

## What To Present After

Use this 30-second update:

1. One-line ID and reason for admission.
2. Current syndrome and timeline.
3. Safety/risk update.
4. MSE highlights.
5. Differential or formulation.
6. One question for the team.

Plain-English note: this guide keeps the student from bouncing between random
questions. It gives a sequence, exact language, and a way to turn the interview
into a presentation.
