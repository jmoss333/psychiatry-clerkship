### suicide_direct_question_001 — Ask About Suicide Directly

- Topic `Suicide risk` · Setting: Inpatient admission interview
- Learner goal: Use plain, direct language without softening or avoiding the question.
- Skill tags: `suicide`, `direct-questioning`, `safety`
- Linked pages: `pg_suicide.md`
- Evidence: `cssrs-columbia-lighthouse`, `va-dod-suicide-cpg-2024`
- Faculty review: {"status": "draft", "reviewer": "", "lastReviewed": ""}

**Prompt.** A patient admitted after worsening depression says, "I do not want to be a burden anymore." What do you say next?

**Rapid drill** (target 20 s).

- *Stance:* Ask directly, using the patient's words, while keeping your voice calm and matter-of-fact.
- *Starter:* Begin with "When you say..." and then ask the direct safety question.
- *Must include:*
  - Reflect the burden phrase briefly
  - Use the words "killing yourself" or "ending your life" plainly
  - Pause and leave room for an answer
- *Avoid:*
  - Euphemisms, reassurance before assessment, or arguing that life is worth living

**Response options (what the learner picks between):**

- **(a)** Are you saying you feel sad, or is this more about stress?  *(rated: **missed**)*
  - *feedback:* This stays vague. It may feel gentle, but it avoids the safety question the patient just opened.
- **(b)** When you say you do not want to be a burden, have you had thoughts of killing yourself?  *(rated: **best**)*
  - *feedback:* Best choice. It is direct, nonjudgmental, and connected to the patient's own words.
- **(c)** You should not think that way; your family cares about you.  *(rated: **harmful**)*
  - *feedback:* This can shame the patient and close the conversation. Start with direct assessment and validation.

### psychosis_validation_001 — Validate Psychosis Without Colluding

- Topic `Psychosis` · Setting: Follow-up interview on the unit
- Learner goal: Acknowledge fear without agreeing with the delusional belief.
- Skill tags: `psychosis`, `validation`, `alliance`
- Linked pages: `t_psychosis.md`
- Faculty review: {"status": "draft", "reviewer": "", "lastReviewed": ""}

**Prompt.** A patient whispers, "The cameras in the vents are watching me." What do you say next?

**Rapid drill** (target 20 s).

- *Stance:* Validate the fear, state your own perception, and invite safety-focused exploration.
- *Starter:* Try a sentence that starts with the feeling before it addresses the belief.
- *Must include:*
  - Name that the experience sounds frightening
  - Avoid agreeing that cameras are present
  - Offer to understand what would help the patient feel safer
- *Avoid:*
  - Debating reality, mocking the belief, or asking questions that deepen the delusional frame

**Response options (what the learner picks between):**

- **(a)** There are no cameras. That is not real.  *(rated: **missed**)*
  - *feedback:* This may be factually true, but it can sound dismissive and increase guardedness.
- **(b)** That sounds frightening. I do not see cameras, but I want to understand what you are experiencing and help you feel safer here.  *(rated: **best**)*
  - *feedback:* Best choice. It validates emotion, avoids collusion, and invites more assessment.
- **(c)** Who put the cameras there?  *(rated: **missed**)*
  - *feedback:* This can deepen the delusional frame. Ask about experience, distress, safety, and meaning instead.

### guardedness_privacy_001 — Respond to Guardedness

- Topic `Alliance and guardedness` · Setting: Early inpatient interview
- Learner goal: Lower the threat level, name choice, and explain confidentiality limits without pressuring disclosure.
- Skill tags: `guardedness`, `alliance`, `confidentiality`, `trauma-informed`
- Linked pages: `pg_interview.md`, `t_psychosis.md`, `agitation.md`
- Evidence: `project-beta-deescalation-2012`
- Faculty review: {"status": "draft", "reviewer": "", "lastReviewed": ""}

**Prompt.** A patient crosses their arms and says, "I know whatever I say will just be used against me." What do you say next?

**Rapid drill** (target 25 s).

- *Stance:* Lower pressure by naming choice, confidentiality limits, and your role in safety.
- *Starter:* Use one sentence to give the patient more control before asking for more information.
- *Must include:*
  - Say the patient can choose what to answer
  - Name the real limits to confidentiality (imminent risk to self/others, mandated reporting) — not routine team sharing
  - Invite what would make the conversation feel safer
- *Avoid:*
  - Arguing that no one is using information against them or threatening consequences as the opener

**Response options (what the learner picks between):**

- **(a)** No one is using anything against you. I just need you to answer the questions.  *(rated: **missed**)*
  - *feedback:* This argues with the fear and adds pressure. Guardedness usually softens when you name choice and explain the frame.
- **(b)** You get to choose what you answer. Our team shares information to care for you, so what you tell me isn't a secret I keep from them — that's not a breach of confidentiality. What I would have to share more widely is an immediate risk to your safety or someone else's, or something the law requires me to report. My goal right now is to understand what would help you feel safer here.  *(rated: **best**)*
  - *feedback:* Best choice. It respects autonomy, distinguishes routine team care-coordination from the real limits to confidentiality (imminent risk to self or others, mandated reporting, court order), and keeps the conversation oriented toward safety and care.
- **(c)** If you will not talk, the team will have to make decisions without you.  *(rated: **harmful**)*
  - *feedback:* This may be true in some situations, but as an opening response it sounds coercive and can confirm the patient's fear.
- **(d)** What are you worried might happen if you tell me more?  *(rated: **partial**)*
  - *feedback:* This can be helpful after you establish choice and confidentiality limits. Alone, it may feel like another demand.

### rupture_limit_setting_001 — Repair After a Limit

- Skill tags: `rupture-repair`, `borderline-personality`, `limits`

**Prompt.** After you explain that an early discharge is not planned today, the patient says, "You people never listen. I am done talking." What do you say next?

**Rapid drill** (target 25 s).

- *Stance:* Repair the rupture without walking back the clinical limit.
- *Starter:* Try: validate first, limit second, repair offer third.
- *Must include:*
  - Name that the patient feels unheard
  - Keep the discharge limit clear
  - Offer one concrete way to slow down and listen
- *Avoid:*
  - Making calmness a condition for being heard or responding with cold technical accuracy

**Response options (what the learner picks between):**

- **(a)** If you refuse to talk, that is your choice.  *(rated: **missed**)*
  - *feedback:* This is technically true but relationally cold. It escalates distance after a rupture.
- **(b)** I hear that this feels like we are not listening. I cannot promise discharge today, but I can slow down and make sure I understand what matters most to you.  *(rated: **best**)*
  - *feedback:* Best choice. It validates, keeps the limit, and offers repair.
- **(c)** You need to calm down before we continue.  *(rated: **harmful**)*
  - *feedback:* This often sounds controlling. Name the emotion and the limit without making calmness a precondition for being heard.

### bpd_rupture_repair_001 — Repair a BPD Rupture

- Topic `Borderline personality and rupture repair` · Setting: Post-rounds check-in
- Learner goal: Validate the feeling, own the interactional impact, and return to a consistent plan.
- Skill tags: `borderline-personality`, `rupture-repair`, `validation`, `documentation`
- Linked pages: `t_personality.md`, `pg_suicide.md`, `brief_psychotherapy.md`
- Faculty review: {"status": "draft", "reviewer": "", "lastReviewed": ""}

**Prompt.** A patient with chronic suicidality says, "You wrote that I was manipulative. I knew you were just like everyone else." What do you say next?

**Rapid drill** (target 30 s).

- *Stance:* Address impact before accuracy, then invite collaboration on language.
- *Starter:* Start with the effect of the words, not your intent.
- *Must include:*
  - Acknowledge that the note felt hurtful or invalidating
  - Avoid defending yourself as the first move
  - Invite a shared look at how to describe the crisis accurately
- *Avoid:*
  - Using formulation terms like splitting or manipulation at the patient during the rupture

**Response options (what the learner picks between):**

- **(a)** I did not mean it that way, and that is not what the note says.  *(rated: **missed**)*
  - *feedback:* This may be accurate, but it leads with self-defense. First address the rupture and the patient's experience.
- **(b)** I am sorry that what you read landed that way. I can see why it felt hurtful. I want to understand what felt most invalidating, and then we can look together at how to describe the crisis accurately.  *(rated: **best**)*
  - *feedback:* Best choice. It repairs impact without abandoning accuracy, and it invites collaboration on language and meaning.
- **(c)** This is exactly the kind of splitting we are trying to avoid.  *(rated: **harmful**)*
  - *feedback:* Using formulation language at the patient in a rupture is shaming and escalates distrust.
- **(d)** Let's focus on your discharge plan instead of the note.  *(rated: **missed**)*
  - *feedback:* This avoids the relational injury. Discharge planning will go better after the rupture is named and repaired.

### medication_ambivalence_001 — Medication Ambivalence

- Topic `Shared decision-making` · Setting: Medication discussion
- Learner goal: Use motivational interviewing instead of persuasion reflex.
- Skill tags: `motivational-interviewing`, `medication`, `shared-decision-making`
- Linked pages: `psychopharm_primer.md`, `motivational_interviewing.md`
- Faculty review: {"status": "draft", "reviewer": "", "lastReviewed": ""}

**Prompt.** A patient says, "I know the antipsychotic helped, but I hate the weight gain. I am not taking it after discharge." What do you say next?

**Rapid drill** (target 25 s).

- *Stance:* Reflect ambivalence before offering options.
- *Starter:* Try one reflective sentence followed by one open question.
- *Must include:*
  - Validate the weight-gain concern
  - Ask what the medicine helped with
  - Ask what would need to change for treatment to feel acceptable
- *Avoid:*
  - Threats, lectures, or closing the decision before the patient's values are clear

**Response options (what the learner picks between):**

- **(a)** If you stop it, you will probably be hospitalized again.  *(rated: **missed**)*
  - *feedback:* This may be intended as education, but it starts with threat. Explore values and tradeoffs first.
- **(b)** It makes sense that weight gain would make you hesitant. What did you notice the medicine helped with, and what would need to be different for it to feel worth taking?  *(rated: **best**)*
  - *feedback:* Best choice. It reflects ambivalence and elicits the patient's own reasons, concerns, and conditions.
- **(c)** There are no side-effect-free medications, so we have to accept some tradeoff.  *(rated: **missed**)*
  - *feedback:* This is too clinician-centered. The next step is collaborative exploration, not closing the decision.
- **(d)** Would it be okay if we compare a few options, including changing the dose, switching medicines, and making a monitoring plan for weight and labs?  *(rated: **partial**)*
  - *feedback:* This is useful once the patient feels heard. Start with reflection and evocation, then offer options with permission.

### mania_limit_sleep_001 — Offer a Sleep-Restoring Plan in Mania

- Topic `Mania and collaborative limits` · Setting: Evening check-in on the unit
- Learner goal: Use calm structure, offer choices, and connect sleep to the patient's goal without debating grandiosity.
- Skill tags: `mania`, `limits`, `sleep`, `de-escalation`
- Linked pages: `t_mood.md`, `agitation.md`, `psychopharm_primer.md`
- Evidence: `project-beta-deescalation-2012`
- Faculty review: {"status": "draft", "reviewer": "", "lastReviewed": ""}

**Prompt.** A manic patient pacing the hallway says, "I do not need sleep. I need my laptop so I can finish the company launch tonight." What do you say next?

**Rapid drill** (target 30 s).

- *Stance:* Validate urgency, state the safety limit, and offer low-stimulation sleep choices.
- *Starter:* Use a calm, concrete structure: urgency, limit, choices.
- *Must include:*
  - Name the patient's energy or urgency without endorsing the grandiose plan
  - State the laptop or stimulation limit plainly
  - Offer two or three concrete choices that point toward sleep
- *Avoid:*
  - Debating whether the launch is real, threatening security early, or calling the patient manic as the opener

**Response options (what the learner picks between):**

- **(a)** That does not make sense. You are manic and need medication.  *(rated: **harmful**)*
  - *feedback:* This is confrontational and likely to escalate. Avoid debating insight when the immediate goal is safety, sleep, and collaboration.
- **(b)** You have a lot of energy and the launch feels urgent. Tonight the safest plan is no laptop and a quieter space. We can choose together between a warm drink, a low-stimulation room, or medicine to help your body sleep.  *(rated: **best**)*
  - *feedback:* Best choice. It validates urgency without colluding, states the limit, and offers concrete choices that point toward sleep.
- **(c)** If you keep pacing, security will have to get involved.  *(rated: **missed**)*
  - *feedback:* Threatening consequences too early can escalate. Name the limit and offer choices before moving up the safety ladder, unless there is imminent danger.
- **(d)** Tell me more about the company launch.  *(rated: **partial**)*
  - *feedback:* Curiosity can build alliance, but here it risks extending activation. Pair brief listening with a sleep-focused structure.

**Prompt.** The patient has consented to a family meeting. The family arrives anxious and starts asking rapid-fire questions. What do you say first?

**Rapid drill** (target 30 s).

- *Stance:* Join the anxiety, set consent boundaries, and create a shared agenda before details.
- *Starter:* Open by orienting the room before answering the first question.
- *Must include:*
  - Acknowledge that there is a lot the family wants to understand
  - Name what the patient has consented to discuss
  - Set one shared goal for the meeting
- *Avoid:*
  - Launching into answers before structure or shutting the room down with confidentiality alone

**Response options (what the learner picks between):**

- **(a)** Let me answer each question in order.  *(rated: **missed**)*
  - *feedback:* This is responsive but unstructured. The meeting can become a long Q&A without goals or boundaries.
- **(b)** I can see there is a lot you want to understand. Before details, let's agree on the goal, what the patient has consented for us to discuss, and what we need to decide before discharge.  *(rated: **best**)*
  - *feedback:* Best choice. It joins emotionally, names consent, and sets a structure.
- **(c)** We cannot discuss much because of confidentiality.  *(rated: **missed**)*
  - *feedback:* Confidentiality matters, but this response shuts down collaboration. State the consent boundary and what can be discussed.

### collateral_questions_001 — Ask Better Collateral Questions

- Topic `Collateral` · Setting: Phone call with family
- Learner goal: Ask for baseline, timeline, safety, and functional change rather than global impressions.
- Skill tags: `collateral`, `family`, `diagnostic-reasoning`
- Linked pages: `exp_family.md`, `ddx.md`
- Faculty review: {"status": "draft", "reviewer": "", "lastReviewed": ""}

**Prompt.** You call a patient's sister for collateral. She says, "He has just not been himself." What do you ask next?

**Rapid drill** (target 25 s).

- *Stance:* Turn a vague collateral concern into baseline, timeline, function, and safety data.
- *Starter:* Use one bundled question that asks for baseline, change, timing, and risk.
- *Must include:*
  - Ask what the patient is like at baseline
  - Ask what changed and when it changed
  - Ask for specific safety or functional concerns
- *Avoid:*
  - Asking for the family's conclusion before gathering concrete observations

**Response options (what the learner picks between):**

- **(a)** Do you think he is safe to come home?  *(rated: **missed**)*
  - *feedback:* This asks for a conclusion before gathering facts. First define baseline, timeline, and concrete risks.
- **(b)** What is he like at baseline, what changed, when did it change, and what specific safety concerns have you noticed?  *(rated: **best**)*
  - *feedback:* Best choice. It turns a vague concern into time course, baseline, and safety data.
- **(c)** Has he been taking his medication?  *(rated: **partial**)*
  - *feedback:* Medication adherence is useful, but it is too narrow as the first follow-up to a broad change-from-baseline concern.

### family_conflict_discharge_001 — De-escalate Family Conflict Around Discharge

- Topic `Family and discharge planning` · Setting: Inpatient family meeting
- Learner goal: Validate fear, protect confidentiality, and shift the room from blame to concrete safety tasks.
- Skill tags: `family-meeting`, `discharge`, `safety-planning`, `conflict-de-escalation`
- Linked pages: `exp_family.md`, `pg_suicide.md`, `family_playbook.md`
- Evidence: `va-dod-suicide-cpg-2024`, `joint-commission-suicide-prevention`
- Faculty review: {"status": "draft", "reviewer": "", "lastReviewed": ""}

**Prompt.** A parent says, "If you discharge him, it will be your fault if something happens." The patient looks down and stops talking. What do you say next?

**Rapid drill** (target 30 s).

- *Stance:* Validate fear without promising zero risk, then move the room toward concrete safety planning.
- *Starter:* Start with fear, then shift from blame to the next safety task.
- *Must include:*
  - Name the parent's fear
  - Avoid promising certainty or zero risk
  - Invite the specific safety steps that must be in place before discharge
- *Avoid:*
  - Defensiveness, blame, or questions that shame the patient in front of family

**Response options (what the learner picks between):**

- **(a)** We would never discharge someone unless we were sure nothing bad could happen.  *(rated: **harmful**)*
  - *feedback:* This promises certainty the team cannot provide. It also sets up false reassurance instead of a realistic risk plan.
- **(b)** I hear how scared you are, and I do not want to minimize that. We cannot promise zero risk, but we can slow down, hear what worries you most, and name the specific safety steps that must be in place before discharge.  *(rated: **best**)*
  - *feedback:* Best choice. It validates fear, avoids a false guarantee, and turns conflict into a concrete safety-planning task.
- **(c)** The decision is ultimately up to the doctors, so let's move on.  *(rated: **missed**)*
  - *feedback:* This shuts down collateral and increases adversarial tension. The family may hold key safety and feasibility information.
- **(d)** Can you tell us exactly what you think he is hiding from us?  *(rated: **partial**)*
  - *feedback:* Specific concerns matter, but this phrasing can shame the patient and intensify triangulation. Invite concerns while preserving the patient's dignity.

### interview_motive_suspicion_001 — Respond When the Patient Questions Why You're Asking

- Topic `Guardedness directed at the interview itself` · Setting: First encounter, guarded or paranoid inpatient
- Learner goal: Meet suspicion about the interview itself with a smaller, lower-stakes opening rather than repeating or justifying the original question.
- Skill tags: `guardedness`, `psychosis`, `paranoia`, `alliance`, `de-escalation`
- Linked pages: `pg_interview.md`, `t_psychosis.md`, `violence.md`
- Evidence: `project-beta-deescalation-2012`
- Faculty review: {"status": "draft", "reviewer": "", "lastReviewed": ""}

**Prompt.** You introduce yourself and ask what brought them to the hospital. Without looking up, the patient says, "Why do you want to know that?" What do you say next?

**Rapid drill** (target 20 s).

- *Stance:* Meet suspicion with validation and a smaller question, not a bigger explanation.
- *Starter:* Answer their question in one sentence, then ask something smaller than your first question.
- *Must include:*
  - Name that the suspicion is reasonable — don't argue with it
  - State your role in one short sentence
  - Trade the original broad question for a smaller, lower-stakes one
- *Avoid:*
  - A long justification or promise about your intentions as the opener
  - Repeating the original question unchanged

**Response options (what the learner picks between):**

- **(a)** I'm just doing my job — I ask everyone this, it's nothing personal, I promise I'm not trying to catch you out or anything.  *(rated: **harmful**)*
  - *feedback:* Over-explaining and promising reassurance reads as anxious, not trustworthy, and can confirm there's something to defend against. The long justification also costs you the small question that would actually move things forward.
- **(b)** That's a fair question. I'm a medical student on the team here to understand how you're doing, at whatever pace works for you. We don't have to start with the whole story — is it okay if I just ask how last night went for you?  *(rated: **best**)*
  - *feedback:* Best choice. It treats the suspicion as reasonable, states your role plainly, hands back control over pace, and swaps the broad question for a small, low-stakes one a guarded patient can answer.
- **(c)** What brought you to the hospital?  *(rated: **missed**)*
  - *feedback:* Repeating the question ignores what the patient just told you: the question itself felt threatening. Without a smaller opening, most guarded patients stay silent or answer in one word.
- **(d)** I understand you might not want to talk, but I do need to know what's going on with you so we can help.  *(rated: **partial**)*
  - *feedback:* Better than arguing, but "I need to know" still centers your need over the patient's control, and the ask stays broad. Name the choice explicitly and narrow the question.

### rounds_naming_uncertainty_001 — Name Uncertainty on Rounds

- Topic `Oral presentation — naming what is not yet known` · Setting: Morning rounds, hospital day 1, diagnosis not yet settled
- Learner goal: State a leading formulation while explicitly naming what remains unknown and what would resolve it, instead of overclaiming certainty or trailing off.
- Skill tags: `oral-presentation`, `uncertainty`, `rounds`, `differential-reasoning`
- Linked pages: `doc_oral.md`, `ddx.md`, `pg_formulation.md`
- Faculty review: {"status": "draft", "reviewer": "", "lastReviewed": ""}

**Prompt.** You're presenting a patient on rounds. Your attending asks, "So what do you think is going on?" — but you're genuinely torn between two possibilities. What do you say?

**Rapid drill** (target 20 s).

- *Stance:* Give a leading hypothesis, name the real competing one, and say what would resolve it.
- *Starter:* In one breath: my leading thought is X, but I haven't ruled out Y, and Z will tell us which.
- *Must include:*
  - State a specific leading formulation, not a vague impression
  - Name the one competing possibility that's still genuinely live
  - Say what finding or result would resolve it, and when
- *Avoid:*
  - Stating either possibility as settled fact
  - Stopping at "I don't know" with nothing further

**Response options (what the learner picks between):**

- **(a)** I think it's bipolar disorder — she's manic.  *(rated: **harmful**)*
  - *feedback:* Overclaiming certainty can send the team down the wrong path, and trust erodes when the real picture emerges. If the differential is genuinely open, say so; the team would rather know.
- **(b)** My leading formulation is bipolar mania, given the decreased sleep and grandiosity — but I haven't ruled out a substance-induced picture, and the toxicology is still pending. I'm watching the trajectory over the next 24 hours and I'll flag it as soon as the tox screen is back.  *(rated: **best**)*
  - *feedback:* Best choice. It gives the team a working answer, names the real competing possibility, and says what specific information would resolve it and when. That's what naming uncertainty sounds like.
- **(c)** Um, I'm not really sure yet.  *(rated: **missed**)*
  - *feedback:* Honest, but it gives the team nothing to act on. Naming uncertainty well means saying what you think, what's still open, and what would settle it, not stopping at "I don't know."
- **(d)** It could be bipolar mania or it could be substance-induced — I'm not sure which.  *(rated: **partial**)*
  - *feedback:* Better than overclaiming or going blank, but it stops short of a working answer. Push one step further: name your leading hypothesis and what would change your mind.

## Diagnostic reasoning cases (5 cases)

### mania_psychosis_substance_001 — Mania, Psychosis, Or Substance-Induced?

- Setting: Resident-level admission formulation
- Learner goal: Hold competing diagnostic hypotheses, state what would update each one, and connect the formulation to safe inpatient priorities.

**Patient brief.** A 24-year-old is admitted after four nights of little sleep, pressured speech, grand plans, irritability, religious auditory experiences, heavy cannabis use, and one recent stimulant exposure.

**Facts available to the learner:**

- **Time course** — Symptoms escalated over one week; sleep decreased before the most disorganized behavior.
- **Syndrome** — Pressured speech, flight of ideas, grandiosity, irritability, increased goal-directed activity, and poor insight.
- **Substances** — Cannabis most days; one stimulant exposure. Tox data are supportive but not explanatory by themselves.
- **Collateral** — Family later describes a prior week of decreased sleep, spending, and conflict that resolved without hospitalization.

**Step 1 — Problem Representation** (`problem_representation`)

*Prompt.* Which problem representation best avoids both anchoring and diagnostic sprawl?

- **(a)** Young adult with acute manic syndrome, psychotic features, impaired judgment, and substance exposure, with bipolar, substance-induced, medical, and primary psychotic etiologies still being tested.  *(rated: **best**)*
  - *feedback:* Best. It names the syndrome, time course, risk, and uncertainty without converting the first impression into a final diagnosis.
- **(b)** Young adult with first-episode psychosis and secondary sleep disruption, with mania to consider if psychosis persists after sobriety.  *(rated: **partial**)*
  - *feedback:* This is plausible, but it reverses the chronology and underweights decreased need for sleep, increased energy, pressured speech, and grandiosity.
- **(c)** Likely substance-induced psychosis or mania because cannabis and stimulant exposure are objective data while mood history is collateral.  *(rated: **partial**)*
  - *feedback:* Objective data can still be misleading if interpreted without timing. Substance exposure is a contributor, not a complete formulation.
- **(d)** Bipolar I disorder with psychotic features, now confirmed by collateral, with substance use addressed as a discharge issue.  *(rated: **missed**)*
  - *feedback:* Collateral raises the probability of bipolar illness, but confirmation is too strong and substance use remains relevant during admission.

**Step 2 — Differential Discipline** (`differential`)

*Prompt.* How should the resident present the working differential on rounds?

- **(a)** Lead with manic syndrome with psychotic features; explicitly track substance-induced, medical/neurologic, and primary psychotic alternatives and the data that would move each up or down.  *(rated: **best**)*
  - *feedback:* Best. A resident-level differential is dynamic: it names the lead hypothesis and defines how uncertainty will be updated.
- **(b)** Use schizoaffective disorder as the neutral diagnosis because it includes both mood and psychotic symptoms.  *(rated: **missed**)*
  - *feedback:* Schizoaffective disorder is not a neutral placeholder. It requires longitudinal evidence about psychosis outside mood episodes.
- **(c)** Document bipolar disorder as primary and substance-induced disorder as secondary because collateral is more reliable than tox.  *(rated: **partial**)*
  - *feedback:* This may be where the formulation is heading, but the reasoning should avoid ranking evidence by source alone. Timing and recurrence matter.
- **(d)** Keep the differential broad and avoid naming a leading diagnosis until several days of observation are complete.  *(rated: **missed**)*
  - *feedback:* Broad uncertainty can become avoidance. The team still needs a leading syndrome and a provisional risk-informed plan today.

**Step 3 — Management Reasoning** (`management_reasoning`)

*Prompt.* Which resident-level next step best links formulation to inpatient work without overreaching?

- **(a)** Frame the plan around safety, sleep restoration, agitation risk, medical/substance rule-out, collateral, and supervised medication reasoning rather than a single diagnostic label.  *(rated: **best**)*
  - *feedback:* Best. It connects diagnostic reasoning to ward priorities and keeps prescribing decisions supervised and formulation-driven.
- **(b)** Delay treatment planning until the substance-induced versus bipolar distinction is resolved.  *(rated: **missed**)*
  - *feedback:* Inpatient care cannot wait for perfect certainty. Safety, sleep, observation, collateral, and medical workup can proceed under uncertainty.
- **(c)** Prioritize antipsychotic selection because psychosis is the symptom most likely to prolong hospitalization.  *(rated: **partial**)*
  - *feedback:* Medication selection may matter, but it should not eclipse sleep, mania, substances, medical contributors, safety, and alliance.
- **(d)** Use the prior episode to teach the family that this is definitively bipolar disorder and substance use is not causal.  *(rated: **harmful**)*
  - *feedback:* This overstates certainty and can damage alliance. Psychoeducation should acknowledge probability, contributors, and what the team is still learning.

**Bias checks the case forces:**

- Do not let a positive tox screen become a complete explanation.
- Do not let manic symptoms disappear behind the word psychosis.
- Define what data would change your working diagnosis.

### delirium_vs_psychosis_001 — Delirium Or New Psychosis?

- Setting: Resident-level consultation formulation
- Learner goal: Prioritize syndrome recognition, reversible drivers, medication risk, and consult communication when psychosis-like symptoms occur on a medical service.

**Patient brief.** A 72-year-old post-op patient reports insects on the wall. Nursing describes waxing and waning confusion, nighttime worsening, new urinary symptoms, opioid exposure, and diphenhydramine for sleep.

**Facts available to the learner:**

- **Attention** — Cannot sustain months backward; drifts off, then becomes frightened and vigilant.
- **Arousal** — Somnolent in the morning, agitated after midnight, and inconsistently oriented.
- **Drivers** — Post-op pain, sleep disruption, possible infection, anticholinergic exposure, and opioids.
- **Baseline** — Family reports mild forgetfulness but independent function before surgery.

**Step 1 — Syndrome Recognition** (`syndrome`)

*Prompt.* Which formulation is strongest for the consult note?

- **(a)** Acute fluctuating inattention and altered arousal consistent with delirium, likely multifactorial, with visual hallucinations as part of the syndrome.  *(rated: **best**)*
  - *feedback:* Best. It makes delirium the syndrome and treats hallucinations as a feature rather than the organizing diagnosis.
- **(b)** Medication-induced psychosis from anticholinergic and opioid exposure, with delirium precautions while awaiting improvement.  *(rated: **partial**)*
  - *feedback:* Medication effects are plausible drivers, but the syndrome is delirium. Naming only medication-induced psychosis may narrow the workup too early.
- **(c)** Dementia with behavioral disturbance precipitated by hospitalization, because family reports baseline forgetfulness.  *(rated: **partial**)*
  - *feedback:* Baseline cognition affects vulnerability, but acute fluctuation and inattention should not be reduced to dementia progression.
- **(d)** Late-life primary psychosis should lead because hallucinations are vivid and frightening.  *(rated: **missed**)*
  - *feedback:* Late-life psychosis exists, but acute post-op fluctuation and inattention put delirium first.

**Step 2 — Consult Recommendation** (`consult_recommendation`)

*Prompt.* Which recommendation best balances psychiatry input with medical ownership?

- **(a)** Recommend a delirium driver review, deliriogenic medication reduction where feasible, infection/metabolic/hypoxia assessment, sleep-wake support, mobilization, sensory aids, and supervised agitation planning.  *(rated: **best**)*
  - *feedback:* Best. It is practical, multidisciplinary, and avoids framing delirium as only a psychiatry medication problem.
- **(b)** Recommend an antipsychotic only if the patient is dangerous, but otherwise defer all workup to the primary team.  *(rated: **partial**)*
  - *feedback:* This respects medical ownership but is too passive. Psychiatry can help structure the reversible-driver review and nonpharmacologic plan.
- **(c)** Recommend benzodiazepines for sleep if hallucinations worsen overnight, because sleep deprivation perpetuates delirium.  *(rated: **harmful**)*
  - *feedback:* Benzodiazepines can worsen non-withdrawal delirium. Sleep support should emphasize safer environmental and medical steps unless local supervised indications apply.
- **(d)** Recommend transfer to psychiatry once medically stable because hallucinations are now the dominant symptom.  *(rated: **missed**)*
  - *feedback:* The dominant syndrome is still delirium on a medical floor. Transfer framing can delay medical treatment of reversible causes.

**Step 3 — Family Update** (`family_update`)

*Prompt.* What is the best way to explain the reasoning to family?

- **(a)** This looks like delirium: a sudden, fluctuating brain-state change often driven by medical stressors and medications; the team is looking for reversible causes while keeping the patient safe.  *(rated: **best**)*
  - *feedback:* Best. It validates the frightening symptoms, explains the syndrome, and avoids implying the patient has suddenly developed a primary psychiatric illness.
- **(b)** The hallucinations are not psychiatric because they are caused by surgery and medication.  *(rated: **partial**)*
  - *feedback:* The intent is reassuring, but the statement is too certain. Delirium is a neuropsychiatric syndrome with multiple contributors.
- **(c)** The patient may have dementia, but we will know after the infection workup returns.  *(rated: **missed**)*
  - *feedback:* Baseline cognition matters, but this explanation does not center acute delirium or the fluctuating attention problem.
- **(d)** The patient is psychotic right now, so the family should avoid reinforcing the hallucinations.  *(rated: **missed**)*
  - *feedback:* Do not use a primary-psychosis frame for delirium education. Family can help with reorientation, glasses/hearing aids, familiar voices, and baseline history.

**Bias checks the case forces:**

- Do not let vivid hallucinations outweigh attention and arousal.
- Do not make psychiatry the owner of a medical delirium syndrome.
- Name reversible drivers and what the team can do today.

### catatonia_vs_refusal_001 — Catatonia Or Refusal?

- Setting: Resident-level inpatient risk formulation
- Learner goal: Recognize catatonia while carrying dangerous mimics, medication risk, and escalation thresholds.

**Patient brief.** A patient with severe depression is mute, staring, barely eating, and holding an awkward arm posture. Staff wonder whether the patient is refusing care or becoming psychotic again.

**Step 1 — Language And Differential** (`language`)

  - *feedback:* Best. It names observed signs, uses structured assessment, and keeps dangerous mimics and complications in view.
- **(b)** Psychotic depression with severe psychomotor retardation; catatonia remains possible if BFCRS is positive.  *(rated: **partial**)*
  - *feedback:* This is not unreasonable, but it makes depression the organizing diagnosis before naming the motor syndrome that needs urgent assessment.
- **(c)** Behavioral refusal versus catatonia; use oral intake and cooperation with nursing care to distinguish them.  *(rated: **missed**)*
  - *feedback:* Catatonia cannot be separated from refusal by cooperation alone. Objective motor signs and structured assessment are needed.
- **(d)** Likely malignant catatonia because poor intake is present, even though autonomic signs are absent.  *(rated: **partial**)*
  - *feedback:* Malignant catatonia must be monitored for, but do not overcall it without fever, autonomic instability, rigidity, or concerning labs.

**Step 2 — First Move** (`first_move`)

*Prompt.* What is the safest resident next step?

- **(a)** Raise catatonia urgently, obtain BFCRS, trend vitals/intake/mobility, review medications and medical drivers, and discuss supervised benzodiazepine challenge and escalation thresholds.  *(rated: **best**)*
  - *feedback:* Best. It combines recognition, measurement, medical safety, medication risk, and supervised treatment reasoning.
- **(b)** Hold antipsychotic changes until BFCRS is complete, but otherwise observe because vitals are stable.  *(rated: **partial**)*
  - *feedback:* Avoiding reflexive antipsychotic escalation is good, but observation alone is too passive given intake and immobility risks.
- **(c)** Increase antipsychotic coverage for psychotic depression while monitoring for rigidity and fever.  *(rated: **harmful**)*
  - *feedback:* Antipsychotics can worsen catatonia or malignant catatonia/NMS. The resident should elevate catatonia before escalating that pathway.
- **(d)** Focus on capacity and refusal documentation because the patient is not eating.  *(rated: **missed**)*
  - *feedback:* Capacity may become relevant, but the immediate diagnostic problem is a potentially treatable motor syndrome with medical risk.

**Step 3 — Escalation Threshold** (`escalation`)

*Prompt.* Which reasoning best defines when the case becomes emergent?

- **(a)** Escalate urgently for fever, autonomic instability, worsening rigidity, rising CK, dehydration, immobility complications, or failure to improve with supervised first-line treatment.  *(rated: **best**)*
  - *feedback:* Best. It names the physiologic and treatment-response signals that should change urgency.
- **(b)** Escalate only if the patient becomes violent or actively suicidal.  *(rated: **missed**)*
  - *feedback:* Catatonia can become life-threatening through medical complications even without violence or expressed suicidality.
- **(c)** Escalate if the BFCRS score is high, but vitals and oral intake are secondary.  *(rated: **partial**)*
  - *feedback:* BFCRS helps quantify symptoms, but medical instability, intake, immobility, and autonomic signs drive urgency.
- **(d)** Escalate when the diagnosis is certain, because premature treatment can obscure the mental status exam.  *(rated: **harmful**)*
  - *feedback:* Waiting for certainty can be dangerous. Catatonia is a clinical syndrome where timely supervised treatment and monitoring matter.

- Do not treat poor cooperation as evidence against catatonia.
- Do not let stable vitals erase intake and immobility risk.

### trd_augmentation_choice_001 — Choosing Between Augmentation Strategies

- Setting: Resident-level treatment planning
- Learner goal: Confirm true treatment resistance, then choose between comparably-supported augmentation strategies using this specific patient's risk factors rather than defaulting to the option with the strongest general evidence.

**Patient brief.** A 34-year-old woman with recurrent major depressive disorder is admitted after an intentional, since-medically-cleared overdose. She has completed an 8-week trial of sertraline 200mg with confirmed adherence and dose, and her depression has not meaningfully improved.

**Facts available to the learner:**

- **Trial adequacy** — Sertraline 200mg daily for 8 weeks, adherence confirmed by pharmacy fill records and collateral; PHQ-9 22 at the start of this episode, 19 now.
- **Safety context** — Admitted after an intentional overdose on an over-the-counter medication, medically cleared. Ongoing passive and intermittent active suicidal ideation.
- **Medical** — Normal renal function on admission labs. No cardiac history.
- **Social** — Lives alone; family is involved but works full-time and cannot provide daily supervision.

**Step 1 — Confirming Treatment Resistance** (`confirm_resistance`)

*Prompt.* Before calling this treatment-resistant depression, what's the resident-level move?

- **(a)** Confirm the sertraline trial was adequate in dose and duration, verify adherence beyond self-report, and confirm the lack of response with a repeated standardized scale rather than clinical impression alone.  *(rated: **best**)*
  - *feedback:* Best. "Treatment-resistant" is a measurement claim before it is a diagnosis — dose, duration, adherence, and a repeated scale are what make it one.
- **(b)** Move straight to discussing augmentation options with the attending, since eight weeks at a full dose is clearly enough.  *(rated: **partial**)*
  - *feedback:* Eight weeks at 200mg is a reasonable adequate trial, but skipping the explicit adherence and repeated-scale check means the team is acting on impression, not confirmed data.
- **(c)** Assume the lack of response reflects poor adherence outside the hospital and focus the plan on adherence counseling.  *(rated: **missed**)*
  - *feedback:* Pharmacy-confirmed adherence in the fact pattern already argues against this. Defaulting to an adherence story without checking the data can delay a patient who genuinely needs a treatment change.
- **(d)** Switch to a different SSRI at the same dose and reassess in another eight weeks.  *(rated: **harmful**)*
  - *feedback:* This restarts the clock on a patient with active suicidal ideation and a recent overdose without addressing whether escalation, not lateral switching, is what the acuity calls for.

**Step 2 — Choosing an Augmentation Strategy** (`augmentation_choice`)

*Prompt.* The attending asks the team to recommend an augmentation strategy. Lithium, a second-generation antipsychotic, T3, and ketamine/esketamine are all reasonable per the evidence in general. What's the best resident-level recommendation for this specific patient?

- **(a)** Name that no single option is clearly superior in general, but weigh this patient's recent overdose against lithium's narrow therapeutic index and lethality in overdose, and favor an option with a wider safety margin given she will eventually be unsupervised at home.  *(rated: **best**)*
  - *feedback:* Best. Holding two comparably-supported options and still reasoning to a patient-specific answer is exactly the resident-level move — the recent overdose is data that should change the recommendation, not a detail to set aside.
- **(b)** Recommend lithium first because it has the strongest anti-suicidal evidence of the options available.  *(rated: **partial**)*
  - *feedback:* The anti-suicidal evidence for lithium is real, but reciting the strongest population-level evidence without weighing this patient's overdose history and eventual unsupervised access is incomplete reasoning, not a full recommendation.
- **(c)** Recommend esketamine because it is the newest option and works the fastest.  *(rated: **missed**)*
  - *feedback:* Speed of onset is relevant to a rapid-acting-agent discussion, but "newest" is not a reason on its own, and this skips the actual safety and access reasoning the case is asking for.
- **(d)** Defer the augmentation decision entirely to the outpatient team after discharge.  *(rated: **harmful**)*
  - *feedback:* A patient admitted after an overdose, with a confirmed adequate-trial failure and ongoing suicidal ideation, is exactly who should not leave the hospital without a next step already started or clearly planned — deferring the whole decision abandons the acute opportunity to act.

**Step 3 — Integrating ECT Into The Plan** (`integrate_ect`)

*Prompt.* Given her acute suicide risk, how should ECT be positioned in the team's recommendation?

- **(a)** Raise ECT as a reasonable first-line option given the acute high suicide risk — not a last resort — and discuss it with the patient and family alongside a pharmacologic augmentation plan.  *(rated: **best**)*
  - *feedback:* Best. High acute suicide risk is one of the settings where ECT is reasonably first-line, and naming that clearly gives the patient a real choice instead of letting her default into it only after other options are exhausted.
- **(b)** Mention ECT only as a distant backup if every medication option fails.  *(rated: **partial**)*
  - *feedback:* This treats ECT as a last resort, which undersells how directly her acuity today — not a future failure — argues for raising it now.
- **(c)** Leave ECT out of the discussion since the patient hasn't asked about it.  *(rated: **missed**)*
  - *feedback:* A patient can only choose an option she's told about. Not raising a reasonably first-line option because she didn't ask isn't neutral — it's withholding it.
- **(d)** Present ECT to the family as what happens when a patient hasn't tried hard enough with medication.  *(rated: **harmful**)*
  - *feedback:* This is both clinically inaccurate and stigmatizing. ECT's appropriateness here comes from her acuity, not from any failure on her part.

**Bias checks the case forces:**

- Do not let "strongest general evidence" substitute for patient-specific risk-benefit reasoning.
- A recent overdose changes the calculus for any option with a narrow therapeutic index or overdose lethality — say so explicitly, don't just note it.
- ECT is a first-line option in specific high-acuity contexts, not a failure state to mention only once other options run out.

  - What seems to increase fear or suspiciousness?
  - What helps the person feel safer without arguing about the belief?
  - What concerns do you have about medication, substances, sleep, or follow-up?
- **say**
  - You do not have to agree with a belief to validate that the fear is real.
  - Sleep, substances, stress, medical issues, and medication adherence can all change relapse risk.
  - The most useful family response is calm, concrete, and non-argumentative.
- **avoid**
  - Do not tell family to prove the delusion wrong.
  - Do not imply family caused the illness.
  - Do not overstate the diagnosis before longitudinal data support it.
- **handoff**
  - Family understanding of the working diagnosis.
  - Relapse warning signs family noticed.
  - Medication, sleep, substance, and follow-up concerns.
- **safety**
  - Ask about weapons, threats, command hallucinations, victimized beliefs, and ability to seek help when indicated.
  - Use supervisor guidance for what can be shared without consent.
  - Escalate any imminent safety concern.

**Completion checks:**

- Provisional diagnosis named accurately
- Family warning signs obtained
- Non-argumentative family response taught
- Safety concerns screened when relevant
- Follow-up/support need identified

### family_involvement_boundaries_001 — When Family Involvement May Harm

- Setting: Confidentiality, coercion, abuse, or high-conflict systems · Time: 5 min supervisor huddle
- Learner goal: Avoid the reflex that family involvement is always helpful; decide what contact is permitted, clinically useful, and safe.

**Opening.** Family involvement can be treatment, but not automatically. I want to clarify consent, safety, coercion, and what information would actually change care.

**Scripted content by stage**

- **prepare**
  - Ask the patient privately who they want involved and who they do not want involved.
  - Screen for coercion, abuse, retaliation risk, restraining orders, custody/legal issues, and interpreter needs.
  - Clarify with the supervisor what can be shared and what can only be received.
- **ask**
  - Who helps you feel safer, and who makes things worse?
  - What are you worried might happen if we contact this person?
  - Is there anyone we should not call or should only call with you present?
  - What information from family would change the safety or discharge plan?
- **say**
  - We can receive information without sharing private details back.
  - You can change your mind about who is involved unless a safety/legal exception applies.
  - Our goal is safety and care, not forcing a family conversation.
- **avoid**
  - Do not use family as interpreters.
  - Do not assume the loudest caller is the safest support.
  - Do not disclose diagnosis, risk, medications, or discharge plans without permission or a clear exception.
- **handoff**
  - Who may be contacted, who should not be contacted, and why.
  - What information can be received versus shared.
  - Safety risks related to family/system contact.
- **safety**
  - Escalate abuse, mandated reporting, Tarasoff/duty-to-protect, or imminent risk questions to the supervising clinician.
  - Use trained interpreters.
  - Document consent, refusal, safety exception, or local-policy rationale.

**Completion checks:**

- Patient asked privately about involvement
- Coercion/abuse/retaliation risk considered
- Interpreter needs addressed
- Receive vs share boundary clarified
- Supervisor/local policy consulted for exceptions

### caregiver_baseline_adaptations_001 — Caregiver Baseline and Adaptations

- Setting: Delirium, dementia, autism, intellectual disability, or communication differences · Time: 6 min
- Learner goal: Use caregiver knowledge to separate baseline from acute change and translate it into safer unit adaptations.

**Opening.** You know what this person is like when they are doing well. I want to understand baseline communication, what has changed, and what helps the hospital feel less overwhelming.

**Scripted content by stage**

- **prepare**
  - Clarify permission, surrogate status, or the safety/legal reason for caregiver contact with the supervising team.
  - Review what you already know about baseline cognition, communication, sensory needs, ADLs, medical triggers, and home supports.
  - Decide whether the immediate goal is diagnosis, safety, communication adaptation, discharge feasibility, or all of these.
- **ask**
  - What is this person like on a typical good day?
  - How do they usually communicate pain, fear, hunger, confusion, or overstimulation?
  - What is clearly new compared with baseline: attention, sleep, speech, movement, eating, toileting, aggression, wandering, or self-injury?
  - What helps them calm, orient, sleep, take medication, or cooperate with care?
  - What medical problems often show up as behavior change for them?
- **say**
  - I do not want to mistake baseline communication for illness, or illness for baseline.
  - Specific examples will help us adapt the unit and avoid unnecessary escalation.
  - Caregiver expertise informs the plan, but it does not replace the team's clinical responsibility.
- **avoid**
  - Do not ask caregivers to provide restraints, interpret confidential questions, or supervise unsafe behavior on the unit.
  - Do not label behavior as just dementia, autism, or personality before checking pain, delirium, withdrawal, trauma, and unmet needs.
  - Do not promise discharge based only on family willingness.
- **handoff**
  - Baseline cognition, communication, function, and sensory needs.
  - Acute changes and likely medical, psychiatric, environmental, or medication contributors.
  - Adaptations that staff can use today, plus unresolved safety or discharge barriers.
- **safety**
  - Escalate acute delirium, catatonia, inability to eat or drink, falls, elopement, abuse, exploitation, or caregiver burnout.
  - Use trained interpreters for the whole conversation, not only the high-risk questions.
  - Document caregiver source, permission/legal frame, and what changed the plan.

**Completion checks:**

- Permission/surrogate/safety frame clarified
- Baseline communication and function obtained
- Acute change separated from baseline
- Unit adaptations named for staff
- Team update links caregiver data to plan

### culture_interpreter_family_001 — Culture, Interpreter, and Family Contact

- Setting: Interpreter-mediated or culturally complex family contact · Time: 6 min
- Learner goal: Elicit the patient's explanatory model and family role while protecting privacy, language access, and safety.

**Opening.** I do not want to assume my framework fits your family. I want to understand what you call the problem, who should be involved, and what we should be careful not to share.

**Scripted content by stage**

- **prepare**
  - Use a trained interpreter for any clinical discussion; relatives bridge only in an emergency while one is arranged.
  - Ask the patient privately who they want involved and what cultural, spiritual, or family context matters.
  - Clarify whether the goal is collateral, psychoeducation, discharge planning, conflict repair, or safety planning.
- **ask**
  - What do you and your family call what is happening?
  - What do you think caused it, and what kind of help makes sense to you?
  - Who usually helps make health decisions, and what do you want shared with them?
  - Are there family, faith, immigration, language, stigma, or discrimination concerns that change what feels safe?
  - What would respectful care look like today?
- **say**
  - I will speak in short pieces so the interpreter can translate everything accurately.
  - Family perspective can help, and the patient's privacy still matters.
  - If we disagree about the explanation, we can still agree on the next safety step.
- **avoid**
  - Do not ask family to interpret suicide, violence, trauma, abuse, sexual, substance, or psychosis questions.
  - Do not treat culture as a diagnosis or assume the family speaks with one voice.
  - Do not disclose more than the patient permits unless a safety/legal exception applies.
- **handoff**
  - Language access used and whether communication was direct with the patient.
  - Patient and family explanatory model, decision roles, and privacy boundaries.
  - Cultural/system factors that change alliance, safety, diagnosis, or discharge planning.
- **safety**
  - Use supervisor guidance for confidentiality exceptions, mandated reporting, threats, coercion, trafficking, or abuse concerns.
  - Use professional interpreters throughout — safety planning and means-safety conversations included.
  - Document interpreter use, consent boundaries, and the clinical reason for family contact.

**Completion checks:**

- Professional interpreter used when needed
- Patient sharing preferences checked privately
- Explanatory model elicited
- Family decision role and limits clarified
- Cultural/system issue tied to plan

## Longitudinal case — *One Patient, Six Weeks*

**One Patient, Six Weeks** · setting: Adult inpatient psychiatry

> Fictional composite only. This simulation teaches observation, communication, formulation, and handoff. It is not a clinical decision tool and does not replace supervision, local policy, or the patient's actual record.

**Patient.** Jordan — A fictional adult admitted after escalating mood symptoms, disrupted sleep, strained family communication, and an uncertain discharge plan.

**Frame.** The story changes as new information arrives. Keep syndrome, diagnosis, contributors, treatment targets, safety, and systems context distinct.

### Week 1 — Admission: start with the person and the timeline

- Focus: ['alliance', 'timeline', 'MSE']

**Patient state.** Jordan arrives guarded and tired after several nights of very little sleep. The chart is brief, the reason for admission is contested, and Jordan asks whether the team has already decided what is wrong.

**Learner task.** Practice opening the interview, mapping the timeline, and describing the mental status exam before naming a diagnosis.

**Checklist:**

- {"prompt": "I can state what changed, when it changed, and who noticed it.", "example": "Jordan reports several nights of very little sleep before admission. Jordan describes feeling exhausted, while the family noticed escalating mood and communication changes."}
- {"prompt": "I can name one alliance move that gives Jordan more control.", "example": "I might begin, \"I have read the admission note, but I want to understand what this was like for you. Would you rather start with the sleep changes or with what brought you here?\""}
- {"prompt": "I can separate observed MSE findings from my interpretation.", "example": "I observed brief answers and limited eye contact. I interpret this as guardedness, although fatigue or mistrust could also contribute."}

**Reflection prompt.** What would help Jordan feel that the interview is collaborative rather than an interrogation?

**Handoff.** On rounds, present the timeline and MSE first, then state what remains uncertain.

### Week 2 — Differential: hold more than one explanation

- Focus: ['syndrome', 'differential', 'medical rule-out']

**Patient state.** Jordan reports racing thoughts, unusual confidence, and feeling watched during the worst nights. A family member describes a previous period of reduced sleep, but the timing of substance exposure and medical contributors is still unclear.

**Learner task.** Build a problem representation, keep primary, substance-related, and medical explanations visible, and identify the next collateral question.

**Checklist:**

- {"prompt": "I can describe the syndrome without prematurely closing on a diagnosis.", "example": "Jordan has a syndrome of reduced sleep, racing thoughts, unusual confidence, and intermittently feeling watched. Whether this reflects a primary mood disorder, substance effects, or a medical contributor remains uncertain."}
- {"prompt": "I can name the data that would increase or decrease confidence in each leading explanation.", "example": "A prior episode with the same symptoms in the absence of substance exposure would increase my confidence in a primary mood disorder. A tight timeline with an exposure or a new medical finding would shift that confidence."}
- {"prompt": "I can ask collateral questions that clarify baseline and longitudinal course.", "example": "I would ask, \"Before this episode, what were Jordan's usual sleep, energy, speech, and daily functioning, and when did each of those begin to change?\""}

**Reflection prompt.** Which new fact would most change your working formulation, and why?

**Handoff.** On rounds, label your leading diagnosis as a working hypothesis and state what would update it.

### Week 3 — Treatment: make room for ambivalence

- Focus: ['medication conversation', 'shared decision-making', 'monitoring']

**Patient state.** Jordan is sleeping more but is unsure whether the improvement is worth feeling slowed down. Jordan worries that accepting medication means accepting a diagnosis and asks what the team is not saying.

**Learner task.** Rehearse a medication conversation that explores goals, concerns, alternatives, monitoring, and the role of supervision.

**Checklist:**

- {"prompt": "I can ask what improvement would matter most to Jordan.", "example": "I might ask, \"If treatment were helping in a way that mattered to you, what would you notice first—better sleep, clearer thinking, more energy, or something else?\""}
- {"prompt": "I can explain uncertainty and monitoring without promising a specific outcome.", "example": "We have seen your sleep improve, but we cannot know yet which changes will last or which burdens will ease. The supervising team will keep checking benefits and side effects with you as the plan evolves."}
- {"prompt": "I can bring medication questions to the supervising team instead of giving an unsupervised recommendation.", "example": "I would tell my supervisor, \"Jordan reports improved sleep but troubling slowing and is unsure about continuing. Could we review the likely benefits, adverse effects, alternatives, and monitoring together before I answer?\""}

**Reflection prompt.** What part of Jordan's ambivalence deserves curiosity before education?

**Handoff.** On rounds, report Jordan's goals and concerns alongside observed response and monitoring needs.

### Week 4 — Family and systems: widen the frame

- Focus: ['collateral', 'family meeting', 'discharge barriers']

**Patient state.** Jordan wants to leave soon. A family member is worried and frustrated, but Jordan does not want every detail shared. The team needs collateral, a clear confidentiality frame, and a realistic conversation about supports.

**Learner task.** Prepare a family meeting that respects Jordan's voice, gathers useful collateral, and identifies what the system must solve before discharge.

**Checklist:**

- {"prompt": "I can explain the purpose and boundaries of family involvement.", "example": "I might say, \"A family meeting can help us understand what changed and plan support after discharge. I want to ask what you are comfortable discussing, and I will confirm the confidentiality boundaries with my supervisor before we meet.\""}
- {"prompt": "I can ask for observations rather than inviting labels or arguments.", "example": "I would ask, \"What specific changes did you notice in Jordan's sleep, speech, spending, or daily functioning, and when did they begin?\""}
- {"prompt": "I can turn family concerns into concrete questions for the team and discharge plan.", "example": "The family is worried about sleepless nights and medication support. I would ask the team who will monitor sleep, how medication access will be handled, and whom the family should contact if symptoms return."}

**Reflection prompt.** How can the family be included without making Jordan feel discussed rather than included?

**Handoff.** On rounds, distinguish patient report, collateral observation, team interpretation, and unresolved disagreement.

### Week 5 — Safety: respond directly without losing the relationship

- Focus: ['suicide inquiry', 'agitation', 'supervision']

**Patient state.** After a difficult phone call, Jordan says, "Maybe it would be easier if I just disappeared," then asks to leave the unit. The meaning, intent, and immediacy of the statement need careful assessment.

**Learner task.** Practice direct, calm safety language; identify what must be escalated immediately; and keep the patient connected while the supervising team evaluates next steps.

**Checklist:**

- {"prompt": "I can ask directly about suicidal thoughts, intent, plan, access, and immediate safety.", "example": "I would ask, \"When you say it might be easier to disappear, are you thinking about killing yourself? Do you have a plan, intent, access to what you would use, or concern that you might act right now?\""}
- {"prompt": "I can state what I heard and what I need to discuss with my supervisor now.", "example": "I might say, \"I am concerned by what you just told me. I need to bring my supervisor in now so we can help keep you safe, and I will stay with you while we do that.\""}
- {"prompt": "I can avoid treating a screening result as a complete risk formulation.", "example": "Jordan's screening response is one data point. I also need to integrate the exact statement, intent, plan, access, behavior, acute stressor, protective factors, and remaining uncertainty with my supervisor."}

**Reflection prompt.** What would you say first so that Jordan hears concern rather than punishment?

**Handoff.** On rounds, describe the patient's words, context, observed behavior, protective factors, uncertainty, and supervision plan.

### Week 6 — Integration: tell the evolving story

- Focus: ['formulation', 'handoff', 'professional identity']

**Patient state.** Jordan is preparing for transition. Symptoms, goals, family concerns, medication questions, and follow-up needs have changed over time. The final task is not to tell a perfect story; it is to make the reasoning and remaining uncertainty visible.

**Learner task.** Give a concise longitudinal presentation, identify the current treatment targets, and plan a warm handoff that keeps Jordan's voice in the center.

**Checklist:**

- {"prompt": "I can explain how the working formulation changed as new data arrived.", "example": "I initially described a mood syndrome while keeping primary, substance-related, and medical causes open. Collateral and the observed course changed their relative likelihoods, and I would name which data drove each update rather than claim certainty."}
- {"prompt": "I can present current risks, strengths, needs, and unanswered questions without overclaiming.", "example": "Current concerns include recurrence after discharge and unresolved medication ambivalence; strengths include improved sleep and family involvement. We still need clarity about follow-up ownership and the response to early warning signs."}
- {"prompt": "I can name one relational skill I want to carry into the next rotation.", "example": "I want to carry forward the habit of naming uncertainty out loud while still offering the patient and team a clear next step."}

**Reflection prompt.** What did you learn about the difference between being right and being useful to a patient and team?

**Handoff.** For the final presentation, connect the timeline, formulation, treatment targets, family/system plan, safety plan, and follow-up ownership.
