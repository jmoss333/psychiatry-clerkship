## Proposed teaching enhancements — not implemented

1. **One detail changes the plan.** Extend the five existing overnight cases. A learner commits to a rationale, then receives one new fact (missed medication, new symptom, or competing patient priority). Reveal a reviewed explanation of what changes and what remains uncertain. Offer a clerkship explanation and an optional resident nuance.
2. **Defend your choice on rounds.** A short rehearsal with four prompts: target, tradeoff, monitoring, and reason to reconsider. Link directly to Psychiatric Pharmacy and the monitoring reading. Start with fixed fictional cases and no patient data entry.
3. **The adverse-effect detective.** Start with the patient's words, then choose a useful question or examination finding before opening the existing teaching section. Reuse Interaction Cards and the cases; avoid a diagnostic or prescribing calculator.
4. **Patient-priority comparison.** Two learners can justify different choices for the same fictional diagnosis by weighing alertness, weight concerns, sexual effects, and feasibility differently. Show reviewed tradeoffs and uncertainties, not a universal drug ranking.
5. **What success looks like over time.** A visual distinction between early observable changes, the intended treatment target, adverse effects, and follow-up responsibilities. Use evidence-supported qualitative stages without inventing numeric prescribing schedules.
6. **Teach it back.** A brief patient explanation rehearsal followed by a faculty-authored example. Pair it with the existing medication-ambivalence conversation case.

Recommended sequence: repair discovery first, then pilot One detail changes the plan using the existing five cases. Evaluate whether learners explain the reason for a change and can identify the responsible follow-up clinician; do not treat quiz completion as proof of clinical competence.
