## Before you open the form

The Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS) is a **structured interview**, not a
questionnaire you hand to a patient. Its value is not the number at the end. Its value is that it
forces two things that unstructured assessment reliably fails to do:

1. It separates **thoughts** from **behavior**, and scores them independently.
2. It makes the clinician ask about the specific element that distinguishes one level of severity
   from the next — instead of stopping at "any suicidal ideation? yes/no."

**This page teaches you how to conduct that interview.** It does not reproduce the instrument.
Use your institution's current official form for administration and documentation:

The official instrument, translations, and free training are at
[cssrs.columbia.edu](https://cssrs.columbia.edu/).

## 1 · The architecture: three axes, not one score

Most students arrive thinking suicide assessment produces a single risk number. The Columbia
protocol is built on the opposite premise — that risk has **separable dimensions that move
independently**, and that collapsing them loses the clinical signal.

| Axis | What it captures | Why it is separate |
|---|---|---|
| **Ideation severity** | An ordered ladder, from a wish not to be alive through to a formulated plan the patient intends to carry out | Each rung is defined by adding *one specific element* — not by intensity of distress |
| **Ideation intensity** | Frequency, duration, controllability, deterrents, and stated reasons for the ideation | A patient with daily uncontrollable thoughts and no deterrents is not the same as one with a fleeting monthly thought at the same severity rung |
| **Behavior** | Actual attempts, interrupted and aborted attempts, preparatory acts, and non-suicidal self-injury — each distinguished, each timed | Behavior is the strongest single predictor in the instrument, and it is scored on its own track |

**The teaching point students most often miss:** a patient can sit *low* on the severity ladder
and *high* on behavior. That patient is not low risk. The ladder is not the assessment; it is one
axis of it.

## 2 · Climbing the severity ladder — what each step actually asks

The ideation ladder moves through five levels. Learn them as **clinical constructs**, and ask
about them in your own words and the patient's register. Reading stems off a page is how the
interview goes flat.

**Rung 1 — The wish not to be alive.**
Not a wish to kill oneself. A wish to be dead, to not wake up, for it to stop. Passive. Students
routinely under-ask this because it feels less alarming; it is the entry point to the whole
conversation and the one most patients will answer honestly.

**Rung 2 — Active thoughts of killing oneself, with no method in mind.**
The shift here is from *wanting not to exist* to *the thought of ending one's own life*. Still
non-specific: no how.

**Rung 3 — Active thoughts with a method considered, but no intent to act.**
The patient has thought about *how*. Crucially, they have not decided to do it. Asking about
method at this stage is not planting an idea — it is establishing where on the ladder they are.

**Rung 4 — Active thoughts with some intent to act, no worked-out plan.**
This is the clinically decisive transition. **Intent — not method, not distress — is the hinge.**
A patient who has considered methods but is clear they will not act is in a different situation
from one who is unsure they can keep themselves safe.

**Rung 5 — A specific plan the patient intends to carry out.**
Method, and some degree of detail, and intent, together.

> **Ask about each rung explicitly.** The most common student error is inferring the higher rungs
> from the patient's affect. Distress is not intent. Calm is not safety.

## 3 · The behavior questions — the part that actually predicts

Behavior is asked separately, and it carries more predictive weight than any single ideation
rung. Four distinctions matter and are easy to blur:

- **Actual attempt** — a self-injurious act taken with *any* intent to die, however ambivalent.
  Note: no injury needs to have occurred. Intent, not tissue damage, defines it.
- **Interrupted attempt** — the patient was stopped by an outside circumstance or person.
- **Aborted / self-interrupted attempt** — the patient stopped themselves.
- **Preparatory behavior** — assembling means, writing a note, giving away possessions,
  researching methods.

**Non-suicidal self-injury is tracked separately and must not be folded in.** Conflating NSSI
with attempts both misstates the risk picture and communicates to the patient that you have not
understood them.

**Timing is part of the finding, not a footnote.** Lifetime history and recent history are
distinct data. Recent behavior — and, in particular, an attempt within the current episode —
changes disposition in a way that a remote attempt does not.

## 4 · How to ask so you get a true answer

- **Normalize before you ask, then ask directly.** "When people are carrying this much, thoughts
  about not wanting to be here are common. Have you had any?" Then ask the specific question.
  Do not soften it into unanswerability.
- **Use the patient's language back at them.** If they say "checking out," use "checking out."
- **Do not signal the answer you want.** "You're not thinking of hurting yourself, are you?" is a
  request for reassurance, not a question. Asking about suicide does not increase risk.
- **Do not visibly relax at a "no."** Patients read it, and it teaches them what gets the
  interview over with.
- **Ask about access to means every time, and ask concretely** — firearms in the home or
  accessible elsewhere, stored medication, and who else has access. This is the question most
  often skipped and most often actionable.
- **Ambivalence is the norm.** "Part of me" answers are data, not evasion. Follow them.
- **Collateral is part of the assessment**, not a courtesy — especially where the account is
  inconsistent, the patient is guarded, or cognition is impaired.

## 5 · What the instrument does *not* do

This is the most important paragraph on the page.

**The C-SSRS structures and documents an assessment. It does not predict.** No instrument in
psychiatry usefully predicts suicide in an individual patient. A meta-analysis of 50 years of
prediction research found that risk factors — individually and in combination — perform close to
chance at the individual level (Franklin et al., *Psychol Bull* 2017).

Two practical consequences you are accountable for:

1. **A low score never discharges your clinical judgment.** If the structured assessment is
   reassuring and your clinical impression is not, the impression governs, and you document why.
2. **What protects the patient — and you — is documented reasoning, not a documented score.**
   Record what you asked, what the patient said, what you concluded, what you did about it, and
   what would change your mind. A number in a flowsheet with no reasoning attached is the
   weakest possible record.

**Risk stratification into "low / moderate / high" is not a disposition.** It is shorthand for a
formulation. Write the formulation.

what the patient said, in the patient's words, before you write the note. If you are unsure
whether something meets a threshold, that uncertainty is itself the reason to escalate.

## 7 · Practice

- Ask about the wish not to be alive on **every** patient you interview this week, not only the
  ones admitted for suicidality. You will be surprised how often it is positive, and you will get
  fluent at asking.
- After each assessment, write one sentence naming which severity rung the patient occupies and
  which behavior category applies — then check it against what your supervisor concluded.
- Rehearse the means-access question out loud until it stops sounding like an accusation.
