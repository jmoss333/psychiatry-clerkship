## 0. The gap this closes

The library currently teaches **how to assess** at high quality and **how to intervene psychotherapeutically** almost not at all. A student can score a CIWA, structure a suicide inquiry, and run a capacity assessment — and then has no scripted, evidence-anchored answer to *"what do I actually do in the twenty minutes I have with this patient?"*

That is also Kaitlin's point, restated in evidence terms.

## 1. The organising claim

**Inpatient psychotherapy works, modestly, and modality barely matters.**

| Finding | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Adding psychotherapy to inpatient care for MDD | **SMD 0.24, NNT 7.4, zero heterogeneity** across CBT, BA, psychodynamic and IPT | Schefft 2019, 14 RCTs, 1,080 patients |
| Psychotherapy in institutional settings, sustained at 9–15 months | g = 0.42 (0.29–0.56) | Cuijpers 2021, 32 RCTs |
| Specific contribution beyond ward treatment | **d = 0.43, with no significant effect of therapy orientation — diagnosis moderated outcome more than modality** | Cohen-Chazani 2022, 37 samples, 4,443 patients |

**The design implication (Kleiman 2026, *JAMA Psychiatry* viewpoint):** stop treating the unit as short-term outpatient care. Outpatient protocols assume stable attendance, cumulative learning and continuity — none of which hold at a four-day length of stay. Build **stand-alone, open-enrolment modules targeting transdiagnostic crisis mechanisms** (emotion dysregulation, cognitive inflexibility, behavioural inactivity, life-threatening behaviours), deliverable by non-specialist staff.

> **This is the thesis the therapy curriculum should be built on**, and it also explains why the modality-by-modality architecture of v1 was the wrong shape.

## 2. Block A — *What can I do in the time I have?* (highest-yield single artifact)

Ship this as a page and as a printable card. It is the answer to the question students actually ask.

| Time | Intervention | What you say or do | Evidence anchor |
|---|---|---|---|
| **5 min** | Validation + agenda-setting | Reflect the emotion, name the experience, agree one goal for *this* conversation | Alliance r = .278; alliance is a therapist variable |
| **5 min** | MI readiness ruler | *"On a scale of 0 to 10, how important is it to you to ___? … Why not a lower number?"* | MI raises attendance in non-treatment-seeking patients, OR ≈ 5 |
| **5 min** | One distress-tolerance skill | Teach a single TIPP element — temperature, intense movement, paced breathing with a longer exhale, paired muscle relaxation | DBT skills training is the active ingredient (Linehan 2015 dismantling) |
| **5 min** | Behavioural-activation micro-dose | Name one pleasant *or* mastery activity for today and schedule it. Getting out of bed counts. | BA vs TAU SMD −0.78 (Cochrane) |
| **10 min** | Safety-plan review | Refine an existing plan; personalise warning signs first | Whole-plan quality AHR 0.79; **warning-sign quality HR 0.48** |
| **10 min** | Chain analysis | Vulnerability → prompting event → thought → emotion → urge → behaviour → consequence | MGH inpatient model; DBT |
| **10 min** | Structured psychoeducation | Diagnosis, medication rationale, relapse warning signs, when to seek help — from standardised material, not improvised | Cochrane: readmission **NNT 5**, relapse NNT 9, non-adherence NNT 11 |

## 3. Block B — the alliance module, rewritten

**Teach three facts and one correction.**

1. **The alliance is a therapist variable.** Therapist variability in alliance predicts outcome; patient variability does not. Confirmed across 152 studies. → *Alliance is a competency you are being trained in, not a property of the patient you were assigned.*
2. **Client-perceived empathy predicts outcome (r = .28). Empathic reflection as a technique does not.** Across 43 samples, the presence or absence of empathic reflections showed essentially no relationship to effectiveness. Machine-learning analysis of ~3,000 sessions found open questions and reflections related to symptom reduction while rated empathy per se did not.
3. **Withhold affirmation from maladaptive content.** Affirmation of maladaptive statements differentiated patients who did *not* achieve clinically significant change.

**The correction:** the library previously taught "read the author list and funding with the same care as the methods," on the strength of an allegiance paper. That paper found **no clear evidence** for allegiance affecting outcome. Replace it with the honest version: *orientation explains about 1% of outcome variance; CBT, person-centred and psychodynamic therapies produce similar effects in routine care.* The equivalence paradox is the teaching point.

**Setting-specific facts worth stocking:**

- **Inpatients report lower alliance than outpatients**, degraded further by involuntary status, coercion and short stays — *and alliance still predicts change in suicidal ideation (r = .30).* Structured patient-centred nursing encounters measurably raise alliance scores.
- **In psychosis, symptom severity depresses provider-rated but not client-rated alliance.** You will systematically underestimate the alliance with your sickest patients, and the patient's rating is often the better outcome predictor.
- **Video is non-inferior to in-person** (patient SMD −0.09); **telephone-only is significantly weaker** than either.
- **Split alliances matter in family work** — unbalanced alliances predict poorer outcome (r = .32).

## 4. Block C — ruptures: a teachable sequence

**Recognise.** Two subtypes: **withdrawal** (disengagement, minimal answers, agreeable compliance) and **confrontation** (complaint, anger, disagreement). Confrontation markers predict dropout at **d = .74**. Trainees miss the withdrawal ruptures — the quiet ones.

**Repair in this order.**

1. **Immediate** — acceptance, validation, curiosity, re-engagement. Foundational: repairs directly *and* builds the safety that step 2 requires.
2. **Renegotiate** the task or goal. Used less; less consistently successful alone.
3. **Expressive metacommunication** — name the rupture, explore what it is about. Powerful in successful cases; **can make things worse without the safety from step 1.** Delivers best later in a session and at low therapist dominance; early in a session, warmth buffers it.

**Model language for step 3** (for the skills lab, not the chart):
> *"I want to check something. When I brought up discharge planning a few minutes ago, something shifted — you got quieter. I don't want to steamroll that. What happened for you right then?"*

**Say the limitation out loud.** Successful repair is associated with better outcome (r = .29, d = .62), but **rupture-resolution training is non-significant for patient outcome (d = 0.22, p = .28)**, and in personality disorders the training effect is weaker still and expressive strategies more likely to backfire. Teach it as a well-evidenced process with an unproven training pathway.

## 5. Block D — the suicidal patient, honestly

**The one-line hierarchy students should be able to recite:**

> Screening finds people and does nothing on its own. The bundle — a personalised plan, means counselling, and sustained contact — is what moves attempts. No score tells you about this individual. And hospitalisation is protective in a narrower band than you think.

| Claim | Number | Caveat that must travel with it |
|---|---|---|
| Safety planning + follow-up | ~45% fewer suicidal behaviours; OR 2.06 for attending outpatient care | Cohort, not RCT; **no effect on ideation**; no completed RCT of the full six-step plan; VA/DoD rate the evidence low |
| Safety-planning-type interventions, pooled | RR 0.57, **NNT 16** | Behaviour, not ideation |
| Plan quality | AHR 0.79 whole-plan; **HR 0.48 for warning-sign quality** | 52.5% of at-risk inpatients had no plan; means documentation averaged 1.29 of 3 |
| Brief intervention in acute care, pooled | OR 0.69 attempts; OR 2.74 linkage | No effect on depression |
| BCBT-Inpatient, ≤4 sessions | **60% fewer attempts, NNT 7**; 71% fewer readmissions | Benefit attenuated in patients with SUD |
| Risk prediction models | AUC ≥ 0.80 in most — and **PPV ≤ 0.01** | ~90% of UK patients who died by suicide were rated no/low risk at last contact |
| Hospitalisation | 7.5% absolute reduction in 12-month attempts **only** for an attempt in the past day | Estimated to reduce risk in 28% and **increase it in 24%** |
| Caring contacts | Positive in several trials; France's national programme aHR 0.62 | Largest trial (N = 102,709) **null**; ≥12 months needed; a single postcard is not the intervention |
| Safety planning in adolescents | **Null** on ideation, behaviour, attempts and re-presentation | Family-integrated adaptations are the direction of travel |

**Lethal means — the three-tier framing** is now drafted into `SPEC_Safety_Planning_Practice_v1_DRAFT.md` §3 and should be taught verbatim from there. Short version: *population evidence strong, individual-counselling evidence thin but favourable, firearm-legislation reviews find a small reduction if any and cannot support individual causal claims — so the case rests on mechanism, cost and guideline endorsement, not on a demonstrated mortality reduction from counselling.*

## 6. Block E — the family meeting

**What to do with one meeting on day three.**

Open with **warmth**, not information. Low warmth is the protective EE domain (OR 0.35); criticism is the toxic one (OR 2.22); high EE overall predicts relapse at **OR 4.87**.

Then, in order of evidence:

1. **Structured psychoeducation** — diagnosis, medication rationale, early warning signs, what to do and whom to call. Cochrane: readmission NNT 5.
2. **A named plan for continuation.** ≤2 sessions is ineffective; purely inpatient-confined programmes have not reduced readmission; the strongest inpatient-initiated study bridged 4–5 months into the outpatient period.
3. **One problem-solving or communication skill**, if the family is high-EE or the patient is at high relapse risk — because the hospitalisation benefit is *not* maintained when psychoeducation is the sole component, and in the classic dismantling trial education alone did not reduce relapse in high-EE families.

**Teach the disagreement.** Four good syntheses of the same literature reach different conclusions depending on the outcome chosen — relapse, hospitalisation, expressed emotion, carer burden. Information-only psychoeducation ranks *first* for relapse (OR 0.18, 10% vs 37%) and is *insufficient* for hospitalisation. A 50-RCT review found no component reliably separated effective from ineffective interventions. This is the curriculum's best worked example of why *"does it work?"* is an incomplete question.

**And teach the implementation number.** 8% of patients received any recommended psychological therapy in the year after discharge in one UK study; 4% had received family psychoeducation in a Norwegian sample. Barriers: confidentiality, medical-model dominance on acute wards, no protected time, short stays. Even *unstructured* family contact with inpatient staff was associated with attending outpatient care at 7 days (OR 2.79) and 30 days (OR 3.07) — so the low-effort version is not nothing.

## 7. Block F — engagement, ambivalence and the addiction consult

**Motivational interviewing, stated accurately.** Cochrane 2023 (93 studies, 22,776 participants): small-to-moderate benefit versus no intervention (SMD 0.48), weakening over time, **no clear benefit versus treatment as usual at most timepoints.** Where it earns its place: attendance in non-treatment-seeking patients (OR ≈ 5, no heterogeneity), medication adherence (RR 1.17), and a durable signal from a very brief acute-setting dose (7.3 fewer substance days per month at two years from two sessions).

**Use OARS in every encounter** — open questions, affirmations, reflections, summaries — and remember Block B: the affirmation is withheld from maladaptive content.

**The behavioural intervention the library is missing.** For stimulant use disorder there is **no FDA-approved pharmacotherapy**, and **contingency management is the standard of care — a strong recommendation at high certainty, NNT 6–10 for abstinence.** ⚑ **Add this.** It is the strongest behavioural-treatment recommendation in addiction medicine, and it is a psychotherapy.

**Structural fact worth one line.** An addiction consult service roughly **doubles** inpatient medication initiation and post-discharge linkage in randomised trials. The therapeutic work is embedded in a service model; teach the model, not only the technique.

## 8. Block G — contraindications and pitfalls

**Clinical:**

- **Acute psychosis with disorganisation** — cognitively demanding work (cognitive restructuring, formal problem-solving) is poorly tolerated. Use supportive contact, one simple behavioural strategy, and psychoeducation.
- **Intoxication or active withdrawal** — defer MI and structured work until the patient is alert and oriented.
- **Involuntary admission** — the alliance is uniquely challenged by coercion, loss of autonomy and the physical environment. MI spirit (autonomy support, collaboration) matters most exactly here.
- **Trauma** — no detailed processing during acute crisis. Stabilisation, grounding, safety.
- **Personality disorder** — expressive rupture work is more likely to backfire; and **routine outcome-monitoring feedback was adverse** in a day-treatment/inpatient RCT.
- **Co-occurring SUD** — moderates effects; BCBT-Inpatient's readmission benefit was attenuated, and bipolar patients did not benefit from SPI+ in the landmark cohort.

**Trainee-specific:**

- Starting a multi-session manualised protocol that outlasts the admission.
- Improvised psychoeducation — 98% of surveyed trainees reported using unstructured conversation, only 25% used supplemental materials.
- Not documenting the psychotherapeutic work or communicating it to the outpatient team.
- Mandating a safety plan without ensuring its quality. **A poor plan can supply false reassurance.**
- No-suicide contracts. Abandoned; crisis response planning is superior.

## 9. Block H — rounds questions (short-answer format, matching the existing 100-question page)

1. *A student says CBT for psychosis should be reserved for patients with shorter illness duration. What does the evidence say?* — No covariate tested (age, gender, ethnicity, duration, phase, severity, dose, therapist training, manualisation, individual vs group) reliably moderated efficacy; the synthesis concludes it should be offered equally.
2. *Does safety planning reduce suicidal ideation?* — No. It reduces suicidal **behaviour** (RR 0.57, NNT 16). Saying otherwise misstates the finding.
3. *Which single step of the safety plan carries the strongest quality-outcome association?* — Step 1, warning signs (HR 0.48).
4. *Your attending asks whether more reflections would strengthen the alliance. Answer.* — Empathic reflections in isolation show essentially no relationship to effectiveness; client-perceived empathy does. Calibration, not frequency.
5. *A resident says MBT has meta-analytic support for self-harm. Correct them.* — Pre-post effects are large; against **active controls** MBT(-A) was not superior except for adult BPD symptoms, and the meta-analysis states that prioritising it for self-harm is not supported.
6. *Which rupture subtype do trainees miss, and which predicts dropout?* — Trainees miss **withdrawal**; **confrontation** predicts dropout (d = .74).
7. *In what order do you attempt repair, and what happens if you invert it?* — Immediate (validate) → renegotiate → expressive. Metacommunication without the safety from step 1 can exacerbate the rupture.
8. *Does psychiatric hospitalisation reduce suicide attempts?* — Only demonstrably for an attempt in the past day (7.5% absolute reduction). Estimated to increase risk in about a quarter of patients.
9. *What is the minimum effective dose of family intervention?* — More than two sessions, continued beyond three months. Two or fewer is ineffective.
10. *Family psychoeducation ranks first for relapse prevention. Why is that not the whole answer?* — Because the hospitalisation benefit is not maintained when psychoeducation is the sole component, education alone did not reduce relapse in high-EE families, and no component has been shown to separate effective from ineffective interventions.
11. *What proportion of patients actually receive a recommended psychological therapy after discharge?* — Roughly 8% in one UK study; 4% for family psychoeducation in a Norwegian sample.
12. *What is the standard of care for stimulant use disorder?* — Contingency management, a strong recommendation at high certainty, NNT 6–10. There is no approved pharmacotherapy.
13. *What did the DBT dismantling trial establish, and why does it matter on a ward?* — Skills training is the active ingredient for reducing NSSI and depression, which is what justifies skills-only, open-enrolment groups where individual DBT is impractical.
14. *A patient with psychosis seems disengaged and your alliance rating is low. What is the measurement wrinkle?* — Symptom severity depresses provider-rated but not client-rated alliance; you may be underestimating it, and the patient's rating often predicts outcome better.
15. *What does a suicide risk score tell you about this patient?* — Almost nothing at the individual level: PPV ≤ 0.01, and ~90% of those who died had been rated no or low risk. Use assessment to build a formulation of modifiable drivers.
