# Evidence-Based Inpatient Psychiatry

An evidence reference for the inpatient stay, admission through discharge — for each domain: the strongest evidence, the landmark studies, the current controversies, and practical recommendations. This complements the how-to teaching pages and bedside tools with the "what does the evidence actually say" view.

---

## 1. Suicide Assessment

### Strongest Evidence

The **Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS)** is the most widely endorsed tool. A 2025 meta-analysis found prior suicidal behavior predicted future attempts (pooled OR 3.14, 95% CI 1.86–5.31), and in an inpatient cohort of 1,055 adults, the C-SSRS demonstrated AUC = 0.757 for post-discharge suicide-related behavior. The Joint Commission (NPSG 15.01.01) and the VA/DoD 2024 Clinical Practice Guideline endorse the C-SSRS for screening. However, a systematic review using GRADE found that **no instrument fulfilled requirements for sufficient diagnostic accuracy** (sensitivity >80% and specificity >50%).

The **SAD PERSONS scale should not be used**: multiple studies demonstrate it performs no better than chance (AUC 0.55–0.59), with sensitivity as low as 1% for repeat self-harm. The VA/DoD 2024 guideline explicitly excludes it.

**Environmental safety** has the strongest single-intervention evidence. The VHA Mental Health Environment of Care Checklist (MHEOCC) produced an **82% reduction in inpatient suicide rate** (from 2.64 to 0.87 per 100,000 admissions, P < .001), sustained over 7 years. Hanging accounts for 71% of inpatient suicide deaths, and ligature point removal was associated with significant reductions in suicide by hanging in a UK national study.

### Landmark Studies

The **Diefenbach et al. (2024) RCT** in JAMA Psychiatry (n=200) is the largest inpatient suicide prevention trial: up to 4 sessions of Brief Cognitive Behavioral Therapy produced a **60% reduction in post-discharge suicide attempts** (OR 0.40, 95% CI 0.20–0.80; NNT = 7) and a 71% reduction in readmissions among patients without substance use disorders.

The Doupnik et al. (2020) meta-analysis of 14 studies found brief inpatient/acute-care suicide prevention interventions reduced subsequent attempts (pooled OR 0.69) and increased linkage to follow-up (OR 2.74). The following figure summarizes these outcomes:

### Current Controversies

A major paradigm shift is underway: **prediction versus prevention**. Hawton et al. (2022, Lancet Psychiatry) argue for shifting from risk prediction to therapeutic assessment, formulation, and risk management. UK/NICE and Australian/New Zealand guidelines now advise against using risk scales for prediction and treatment allocation, while US and European guidelines still endorse structured tools as adjuncts. Nearly 90% of UK mental health patients who died by suicide were rated as no/low risk at their last clinical contact.

### Practical Recommendations

- Use the C-SSRS to **structure** comprehensive assessments and trigger interventions, not to predict individual suicide

- Implement environmental safety checklists (MHEOCC) with systematic ligature point removal

- Deliver brief CBT for suicide prevention (up to 4 sessions) during hospitalization

- Complete a Safety Planning Intervention before discharge

- Ensure follow-up within 7 days of discharge — associated with reduced suicide risk (RR 0.44 in youth)

- Send caring contacts for 12 months post-discharge

## 2. Violence Risk Assessment

### Strongest Evidence

For **imminent (24-hour) risk**, the **Brøset Violence Checklist (BVC)** and **Dynamic Appraisal of Situational Aggression (DASA)** have the best evidence. A meta-analysis of 31 studies (282,238 assessments) found pooled effect sizes of Hedges' g = 1.35 (95% CI 1.15–1.56) for the BVC and g = 1.04 (95% CI 0.69–1.39) for the DASA. Both are rated as having acceptable utility and feasibility.

For **medium/long-term risk**, the **HCR-20v3** is the most globally used structured professional judgment tool, with pooled AUC of 0.69 (95% CI 0.65–0.72) for violent recidivism in forensic populations. An international study found HCR-20v3 performance deteriorated after 6 months, suggesting **reassessment every 6 months** is needed to maintain predictive validity.

### Current Controversies

A narrative review found that 67% of HCR-20 studies focused on predictive validity rather than whether its use actually reduced violence — only one study tested whether using the HCR-20 affected violence rates. The critical question remains whether risk assessment tools improve outcomes or merely classify patients.

### Practical Recommendations

- Use the BVC or DASA for daily shift-level violence risk screening

- Use the HCR-20v3 for medium-term risk assessment with reassessment every 6 months

- Combine structured tools with clinical judgment rather than relying on either alone

## 3. Agitation Management

### Strongest Evidence

**Verbal de-escalation is first-line treatment** per APA and AAEP Project BETA guidelines, effective for a majority of patients within 5 minutes. A cluster randomized study across all Slovenian psychiatric hospitals found de-escalation training reduced aggressive events by **73%** (IRR 0.268) and physical restraints by **70%** (IRR 0.304). However, Cochrane reviews found **no RCTs** of de-escalation techniques for psychosis-induced aggression.

When pharmacotherapy is needed, a landmark **individual participant data network meta-analysis** (Lancet Psychiatry, 2026; 2,705 participants, 13 RCTs) provides the most comprehensive comparison:

### Landmark Studies

The **TREC trials** established haloperidol + promethazine as superior to haloperidol alone (NNT = 6 for tranquility at 20 min), with all 10 dystonia cases occurring in the haloperidol-alone group — the trial was stopped early. The TREC-India trial found IM olanzapine and IM haloperidol + promethazine were similarly effective at 15 minutes, but olanzapine required significantly more additional drugs (43% vs. 21%).

**Inhaled loxapine** achieves peak plasma concentration in ~2 minutes and was significantly faster than IM aripiprazole in the PLACID trial (median 50 vs. 60 min to response; P = 0.0005), but carries a black box warning for bronchospasm and requires REMS enrollment.

### Practical Recommendations

- Always attempt verbal de-escalation first

- For mild-moderate agitation: offer oral medications (olanzapine 10 mg, lorazepam 2 mg, or haloperidol 5 mg + lorazepam 2 mg)

- For severe agitation requiring IM: haloperidol 5 mg + promethazine 25–50 mg IM, or olanzapine 10 mg IM (never combine IM olanzapine with IM benzodiazepines)

- Midazolam 5 mg IM is the fastest-acting agent but carries respiratory depression risk

- Avoid haloperidol monotherapy — it is the least effective option with the highest EPS risk

## 4. Rapid Tranquilization

### Key Evidence Summary

The 2026 network meta-analysis confirmed that **treatment effect differences between agents are smaller in severe agitation** than moderate agitation (ratio of ORs 0.38, 95% CI 0.17–0.80), meaning agent selection matters more for moderately agitated patients. Confidence in all estimates was low to very low.

For **methamphetamine-related agitation**, IV midazolam 5 mg + droperidol 5 mg achieved adequate sedation at 10 min in 85% vs. 47% (droperidol alone) and 50% (olanzapine alone).

### Current Controversies

- **Ketamine** for agitation is emerging but limited to ACEP Level C consensus recommendations; a network meta-analysis ranked it highest for effectiveness (SUCRA 93%) but with very low confidence

- **IV vs. IM route**: IV combinations may be faster but carry greater hemodynamic risks; most guidelines recommend avoiding IV treatments

- **Droperidol availability** varies by country, limiting generalizability of evidence favoring it

## 5. Seclusion and Restraint

Seclusion/restraint (S/R) may only be used as **last-resort interventions** per APA and Joint Commission standards. PTSD incidence after S/R ranges from 25–47%. Mechanical restraints have been associated with asphyxiation, cardiac arrest, and pulmonary embolism.

The **Six Core Strategies (6CS)** for S/R reduction have the strongest implementation evidence: a multisite study of 43 facilities found the stabilized implementation group reduced seclusion hours by 19% and proportion restrained by 30%. CMS public reporting reduced restraint duration by 49% and seclusion by 54%. Pennsylvania State hospitals achieved near-cessation of S/R through continuous 6CS adherence.

## 6. Delirium

### Strongest Evidence

The **Confusion Assessment Method (CAM)** remains the gold standard for delirium detection, with sensitivity 94–100%, specificity 90–95%, and interrater reliability κ = 0.92. A 2023 meta-analysis confirmed the CAM has the highest specificity (0.98) among bedside tools. Only 12–35% of delirium cases are recognized through routine clinical documentation.

**Multicomponent non-pharmacological prevention** is the cornerstone of management. A meta-analysis of 14 HELP-based studies showed a **53% reduction in incident delirium** (OR 0.47, 95% CI 0.38–0.58). The SCCM PADIS guidelines conditionally recommend these interventions.

**Antipsychotics do not improve delirium outcomes.** The MIND-USA trial (n=566) found no difference in days alive without delirium or coma with haloperidol or ziprasidone vs. placebo. The AID-ICU trial (n=1,000) similarly found no significant difference in the primary outcome. Long-term MIND-USA follow-up showed no benefit at 3 or 12 months. The PADIS guidelines recommend **against** routine antipsychotic use for delirium.

**Dexmedetomidine** has the strongest pharmacological evidence: a meta-analysis of 77 RCTs found it reduced delirium risk (RR 0.67, 95% CI 0.55–0.81) compared to other sedatives, though with increased bradycardia and hypotension.

### Practical Recommendations

- Screen all at-risk patients using the CAM, 3D-CAM, or 4AT

- Implement multicomponent non-pharmacological bundles (reorientation, mobilization, sleep hygiene, sensory optimization)

- Reserve antipsychotics for severe hyperactive delirium with behavioral disturbance refractory to non-pharmacological measures

- Use benzodiazepines only for alcohol/benzodiazepine withdrawal delirium

- Prefer dexmedetomidine over benzodiazepines for ICU sedation

## 7. Catatonia

### Strongest Evidence

Catatonia prevalence in acute psychiatric inpatient settings is **9–20%**, with mood disorders (not schizophrenia) being the most common underlying condition. The **Bush-Francis Catatonia Rating Scale (BFCRS)** is the most widely used and recommended instrument, with a screening cutoff of ≥2 items. Notably, the BFCRS identifies substantially more cases than DSM-5 criteria alone (18.3% vs. 6.6% prevalence in one study).

**Lorazepam** is effective in up to 90% of acute catatonia cases. A 2026 meta-analysis found an overall remission rate of 55% and response rate of 77%, with a mean BFCRS reduction of 11.88 points (Cohen's d = −3.15). The lorazepam challenge test (2 mg IV) is both diagnostic and therapeutic.

**ECT** is the definitive treatment, with response rates of **80–100%**, including up to 60% of benzodiazepine non-responders. The FDA reclassified ECT devices in 2018 as indicated for catatonia. Effects can be expected after 4–6 sessions; bitemporal placement is generally preferred.

### Differentiating Catatonia from NMS

Many experts consider NMS a type of malignant catatonia precipitated by dopamine-blocking agents. Key differentiating features favoring NMS include fever (OR 7.3), diaphoresis (OR 10.0), and lead-pipe rigidity (OR 9.6); features favoring catatonia include stupor, stereotypy, waxy flexibility, and negativism.

### Current Controversies

The evidence base consists primarily of case series and open studies — **no high-quality RCTs exist** for either benzodiazepines or ECT in catatonia. The Cochrane review found insufficient trial-derived evidence. DSM-5-TR states catatonic disorder due to another medical condition cannot be separately diagnosed during delirium, but research shows approximately one-third of ICU patients may have both conditions simultaneously.

### Practical Recommendations

- Screen with the BFCRS (≥2 items positive) in any patient with unexplained motor abnormalities, mutism, or negativism

- Perform a lorazepam challenge test (2 mg IV; 1 mg in elderly)

- If positive, continue scheduled lorazepam (minimum TID; typical maintenance 8–24 mg/day)

- If benzodiazepines insufficient or malignant features present, initiate ECT urgently

- **Avoid high-potency first-generation antipsychotics** — they can worsen catatonia and precipitate NMS

- Consider NMDA antagonists (amantadine, memantine) when delirium co-occurs, as benzodiazepines may worsen delirium

## 8. Capacity Evaluation

### Strongest Evidence

Appelbaum's **four abilities model** — understanding, appreciation, reasoning, and expressing a choice — is the foundational framework, endorsed by the APA Resource Document on Decisional Capacity (2019). Capacity is always **decision-specific** and not determined solely by diagnosis.

The **MacArthur Competence Assessment Tool for Treatment (MacCAT-T)** is the most widely used structured instrument, with excellent interrater reliability (ICC 0.92–0.98 across validations) and strong construct validity. A critical finding is that clinical assessment alone frequently **overestimates** capacity: in one study, 53.5% of schizophrenia patients were classified as impaired by MacCAT-T vs. only 18.4% by clinical assessment.

### Practical Recommendations

- Assess all four Appelbaum criteria for every capacity determination

- Use the MacCAT-T for difficult cases or those likely to involve legal proceedings

- MMSE scores <19 are highly likely to be associated with incompetence; scores ≥23–26 strongly suggest competence

- Document the specific decision, the information disclosed, and the patient's responses to each of the four criteria

- Remember that capacity can fluctuate — reassess when clinical status changes

## 9. Involuntary Treatment

### Legal Framework

Three landmark U.S. Supreme Court cases define the legal landscape: **Washington v. Harper** (1990) allows administrative review for involuntary medication of dangerous/gravely disabled prisoners; **Sell v. United States** (2003) requires judicial authorization for competency restoration with four specific criteria; and **Riggins v. Nevada** (1992) extends protections to pretrial detainees. A retrospective review found that 79% of federal defendants involuntarily treated under Sell were successfully restored to competency.

### Outcomes Evidence

A systematic review of 41 studies found involuntary patients had **equal or longer** length of stay, equal or greater readmission risk, and lower social functioning compared to voluntary patients. A pooled analysis of 2,790 involuntary patients across 11 countries showed significant reductions in suicidality (13.9% → 5.0% at 1 month) and hostility (46.1% → 14.5%). However, a systematic review of 10 studies found no consistent trend toward improved long-term medication adherence after involuntary hospitalization.

### Current Controversies

The UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (CRPD) Committee holds that all persons have legal capacity at all times and that involuntary detention/treatment violates the Convention — a position **not universally accepted** within the UN system and strongly criticized by some academics. The WHO's 2023 guidance fundamentally rejects coercion, representing a decisive shift from its 2006 position. International involuntary hospitalization rates vary nearly **20-fold** (14.5 to 282 per 100,000), driven by legislative, cultural, and systemic factors.

**Advance statements** are the only intervention category showing a statistically significant reduction in compulsory admissions in meta-analysis. The following figure from de Jong et al. (2016) illustrates this finding:

### Practical Recommendations

- Use the least restrictive alternative consistent with patient safety

- Document the specific legal criteria met for involuntary treatment

- Involve patients in decision-making to the greatest extent possible — perceived fairness partially mediates the relationship between involuntary status and perceived coercion

- Discuss psychiatric advance directives with all patients, particularly those with recurrent involuntary admissions

## 10. Family Meetings

### Strongest Evidence

**Family psychoeducation (FPE)** has the most robust evidence base, supported by over 100 RCTs. A Lancet Psychiatry network meta-analysis (2022) comparing 11 family intervention models found FPE was the most efficacious for relapse prevention in schizophrenia at 12 months: OR 0.18 (95% CI 0.12–0.27), with only 10% relapsing vs. 37% with treatment as usual. Even the simplest form of family psychoeducation was ranked among the most efficacious, suggesting that informing families about illness, symptoms, and treatment options has a protective role.

An RCT of 83 hospitalized patients with major depression found that at 15 months, treatment response rates were **49% (multi-family group therapy), 24% (single-family therapy), and 9% (TAU)**.

**Family involvement in discharge planning** is significantly associated with outpatient follow-up: any family involvement with inpatient staff was associated with follow-up within 7 days (OR 2.79) and 30 days (OR 3.07).

### Current Controversies

The **Open Dialogue** approach has generated substantial interest but **no completed RCT exists**; the ODDESSI trial is ongoing. Non-randomized Finnish studies reported impressive outcomes (82% with no/mild psychotic symptoms at 2 years, only 35% needing neuroleptics), but these cannot be attributed to the intervention without controlled data.

Implementation remains poor despite strong guideline recommendations. Key barriers include confidentiality concerns, organizational culture, and clinician uncertainty about family involvement.

### Practical Recommendations

- Offer family psychoeducation to all patients with psychotic disorders and their families — even brief, simple formats are effective

- Involve families in discharge planning — it significantly increases follow-up attendance

- Address confidentiality proactively with patients and families

- For mood disorders, consider multi-family group therapy during hospitalization

## 11. Discharge Planning

### Strongest Evidence

The 2022 Cochrane review (33 RCTs, 12,242 participants) found individualized discharge planning reduced readmissions (RR 0.89, 95% CI 0.81–0.97) and shortened length of stay (MD −0.73 days), though evidence for psychiatric populations specifically was limited. Effective components include pre- and post-discharge psychoeducation, structured needs assessments, medication reconciliation, transition managers, and inpatient-outpatient provider communication.

**Scheduling an outpatient appointment** at discharge is significantly associated with attending follow-up, regardless of pre-admission engagement level. Patients with no prior outpatient care were three times more likely to follow up within 7 days when an appointment was scheduled.

### Post-Discharge Suicide Risk

The post-discharge period carries the **highest risk for suicide**: pooled rates of 2,950 per 100,000 person-years in the first week and approximately 100 times the global suicide rate during the first 3 months. Follow-up within 7 days is a HEDIS quality indicator and is associated with reduced suicide risk and greater medication adherence.

### Practical Recommendations

- Schedule a specific outpatient appointment before discharge — do not simply provide a phone number

- Complete a Safety Planning Intervention with all patients at suicide risk

- Ensure medication reconciliation and patient/family education about medications

- Communicate directly with outpatient providers (warm handoff)

- Target follow-up within 7 days for all psychiatric discharges

## 12. Readmission Reduction

### Strongest Evidence

Thirty-day psychiatric readmission rates range from 7–17% across studies. The strongest and most consistent predictor is **previous psychiatric hospitalizations**. Other predictors include homelessness, schizophrenia/bipolar/personality disorder diagnoses, medical comorbidity, and co-occurring substance use disorders.

Unmet social needs stack **dose-dependently**: 30-day readmission climbs from ~11.5% for patients with no documented social risk to ~63.5% for those with five domains of need (adjusted OR up to ~12.55; Bensken et al., *JGIM* 2021). This reframes the social history as a readmission-risk instrument, not a formality — and ties directly to §11's finding that pre-discharge 7-day follow-up is associated with lower suicide risk (HR ~0.82).

**Critical Time Intervention (CTI)** has the strongest RCT evidence for readmission reduction. The landmark Herman et al. trial (n=150, formerly homeless with SMI) found CTI significantly reduced rehospitalization (OR 0.11, 95% CI 0.01–0.96) and homelessness (OR 0.22). Brief CTI reduced 30-day readmission from 47% to 28% (P < .001).

However, meta-analyses of transitional interventions overall show **modest, often non-significant** effects on readmission (pooled OR 0.76, 95% CI 0.55–1.05). The **ENRICH trial** (n=590), the definitive RCT of peer support for psychiatric discharge, found **no significant effect** on readmission (RR 0.97, 95% CI 0.82–1.14).

### Practical Recommendations

- Implement CTI or Brief CTI for high-risk patients (homeless, recurrent admissions)

- Address social determinants — housing instability is a major readmission driver

- Use the READMIT index to identify high-risk patients for targeted intervention

- Combine case management with CBT and psychoeducation — this combination shows the most consistent improvements

## 13. Follow-Up Interventions and Collaborative Care

### Post-Discharge Follow-Up

The **Safety Planning Intervention with structured telephone follow-up (SPI+)** was associated with ~50% fewer suicidal behaviors over 6 months and more than double the odds of engaging in outpatient care. The VA/DoD 2024 guideline recommends periodic caring communications for 12 months post-discharge, with moderate-quality evidence showing a 43% reduction in suicide attempts at 1 year.

Telehealth follow-up shows promise: the ATTEND pilot reduced wait times by 18.6 days and showed lower readmission rates (5% vs. 19%). Telehealth partial hospitalization was as effective as in-person treatment with higher completion rates (72.9% vs. 62.3%).

### Collaborative Care

The Collaborative Care Model (CoCM) has one of the strongest evidence bases of any complex intervention in psychiatry, supported by **over 90 RCTs**. The 2012 Cochrane review (79 RCTs, 24,308 participants) demonstrated significantly greater improvement in depression outcomes (SMD −0.34, 95% CI −0.41 to −0.27). A 2025 individual participant data meta-analysis in JAMA Psychiatry (35 datasets, N = 20,046) identified the **therapeutic treatment strategy component** — particularly manual-based psychotherapy and family involvement — as the most effective driver.

The **IMPACT trial** (n=1,801) is the landmark CoCM study: 45% of intervention patients had ≥50% reduction in depressive symptoms vs. 19% of usual care (OR 3.45, P < .001), with benefits persisting one year after resources were withdrawn.

### Measurement-Based Care

MBC can improve remission rates by up to 75% compared to usual care. The Joint Commission requires MBC in all accredited behavioral health programs since 2018. However, **less than 20% of practitioners** engage in MBC, and the VA/DoD 2022 guideline rated the overall evidence as weak.

### Current Controversies

The **DIAMOND initiative** highlights the implementation gap: despite increased collaborative care services and higher patient satisfaction, depression remission rates were **not significantly different** from comparison groups in real-world implementation across 75 clinics. The most important implementation factors were an engaged psychiatrist (r = 0.62) and face-to-face warm handoffs (r = 0.54).

### Practical Recommendations

- Implement SPI+ (safety planning + structured telephone follow-up within 72 hours) for all patients at suicide risk

- Send caring contacts (text, mail) for 12 months post-discharge

- Use collaborative care models for depression management in primary care settings

- Implement measurement-based care using validated tools (PHQ-9, GAD-7) at every encounter

- Leverage telehealth to bridge the gap between discharge and first outpatient appointment
