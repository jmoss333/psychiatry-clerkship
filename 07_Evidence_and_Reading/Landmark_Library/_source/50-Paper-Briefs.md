# PSYCHOPHARMACOLOGY

## 1. CATIE — Lieberman 2005

**Citation:** Lieberman JA, Stroup TS, McEvoy JP, et al. Effectiveness of antipsychotic drugs in patients with chronic schizophrenia. *N Engl J Med.* 2005;353(12):1209–1223.
**File:** `01-CATIE-Lieberman-2005.m4a`

### The Brief
Before CATIE, the field widely believed second-generation antipsychotics were meaningfully more effective and safer than the older drugs — a belief built mostly on industry trials in highly selected populations. NIMH funded CATIE to test that belief in people who actually show up to a community clinic. Lieberman and colleagues randomized 1,493 patients with chronic schizophrenia to olanzapine, quetiapine, risperidone, ziprasidone, or the mid-potency first-generation drug perphenazine, double-blind, for up to 18 months. The primary outcome was all-cause discontinuation — a brutal but honest composite of efficacy, tolerability, and patient preference. Seventy-four percent of patients stopped their assigned drug before the trial ended. Olanzapine had the longest time to discontinuation, but delivered the worst weight and metabolic burden. The other three SGAs were statistically indistinguishable from perphenazine. **Monday morning:** stop choosing antipsychotics by generation. Choose by metabolic risk, EPS vulnerability, sedation needs, adherence history, and prior response. Mid-potency first-generation drugs remain legitimate first-line options. **Caveat:** CATIE enrolled chronic, previously-treated patients; first-episode results look different, and clozapine only entered in Phase 2E.

## 2. STAR*D — Rush 2006

**Citation:** Rush AJ, Trivedi MH, Wisniewski SR, et al. Acute and longer-term outcomes in depressed outpatients requiring one or several treatment steps. *Am J Psychiatry.* 2006;163(11):1905–1917.
**File:** `02-STARD-Rush-2006.m4a`

### The Brief
STAR*D is the trial that made the field honest about depression outcomes. Rush and colleagues enrolled 4,041 real-world outpatients with non-psychotic major depression — people with the comorbidities and social chaos that industry trials systematically exclude — and ran them through up to four sequential pharmacotherapy steps under measurement-based care. Step 1 was open-label citalopram. Non-remitters could be randomized in Step 2 to a switch (sertraline, bupropion-SR, venlafaxine-XR) or augmentation (bupropion-SR or buspirone added to citalopram). Steps 3 and 4 escalated further. Remission rates fell sharply with each step: roughly 33%, 25%, 18%, and 13%. Cumulative remission across four steps reached about 67%, but relapse rates climbed — 40% at one year after a Step 1 remission versus 71% after a Step 4 remission. **Monday morning:** use a validated scale (QIDS or PHQ-9) at every visit, push the first SSRI to an adequate dose and duration before declaring failure, and give patients a realistic expectation — roughly one in three will remit fully on drug number one. Don't call anyone "treatment-resistant" until at least two adequate trials. **Caveat:** no placebo arm. The pharmacologic signal is entangled with spontaneous improvement and placebo response.

## 3. STEP-BD — Sachs 2007

**Citation:** Sachs GS, Nierenberg AA, Calabrese JR, et al. Effectiveness of adjunctive antidepressant treatment for bipolar depression. *N Engl J Med.* 2007;356(17):1711–1722.
**File:** `03-STEP-BD-Sachs-2007.m4a`

### The Brief
For decades psychiatrists reflexively added an antidepressant when a bipolar patient became depressed, on the theory that it couldn't hurt. STEP-BD tested that assumption. Sachs and colleagues, within the NIMH Systematic Treatment Enhancement Program for Bipolar Disorder, randomized 366 patients with bipolar depression who were already on a mood stabilizer to adjunctive paroxetine, bupropion, or matching placebo for up to 26 weeks. The primary outcome was "durable recovery" — eight consecutive weeks of euthymia. The result was clean and disappointing: 23.5% on adjunctive antidepressant versus 27.3% on placebo achieved durable recovery. No benefit. And — importantly — no increased rate of treatment-emergent affective switch in the antidepressant arm, countering the other dogma that antidepressants reliably induce mania in bipolar patients. **Monday morning:** in bipolar depression, your first move should be to optimize the mood stabilizer (lithium, lamotrigine, quetiapine, lurasidone, cariprazine) rather than add an SSRI or SNRI. If you do add one, know you are not delivering much benefit on average. **Caveat:** this was a negative pragmatic trial in patients already on stabilizers; it doesn't rule out antidepressant benefit for specific subtypes, nor does it comment on bipolar I versus II or rapid cycling.

## 4. Kane Clozapine — Kane 1988

**Citation:** Kane J, Honigfeld G, Singer J, Meltzer H. Clozapine for the treatment-resistant schizophrenic. A double-blind comparison with chlorpromazine. *Arch Gen Psychiatry.* 1988;45(9):789–796.
**File:** `04-Kane-Clozapine-1988.m4a`

### The Brief
Kane 1988 is the single trial that put clozapine back on the map after it was pulled from the US market in the 1970s over agranulocytosis deaths. Kane, Honigfeld, Singer, and Meltzer randomized 268 patients with schizophrenia who had failed at least three prior antipsychotic trials (plus a prospective haloperidol lead-in failure) to clozapine or chlorpromazine with benztropine, double-blind, for six weeks. The response rate was striking: 30% on clozapine versus 4% on chlorpromazine met the *a priori* response criterion (≥20% BPRS reduction plus a CGI of "mildly ill" or better). For a population the field had written off, a one-in-three response was revolutionary. This trial is the evidentiary foundation for every modern guideline that says clozapine is the treatment of choice for treatment-resistant schizophrenia — a standard that still applies almost 40 years later. **Monday morning:** if a patient has failed two adequate antipsychotic trials, clozapine is the next step, not the last resort. Monitoring logistics should not be the reason you delay. **Caveat:** short six-week trial, older comparator, and real-world clozapine uptake remains catastrophically low compared with what the evidence supports.

## 5. Cipriani Antidepressants Network Meta-Analysis — Cipriani 2018

**Citation:** Cipriani A, Furukawa TA, Salanti G, et al. Comparative efficacy and acceptability of 21 antidepressant drugs for the acute treatment of adults with major depressive disorder: a systematic review and network meta-analysis. *Lancet.* 2018;391(10128):1357–1366.
**File:** `05-Cipriani-AD-NMA-2018.m4a`

### The Brief
Cipriani 2018 is the definitive network meta-analysis of the antidepressant evidence base — 522 double-blind RCTs, 116,477 participants, 21 drugs — answering two blunt questions: do they work, and are any meaningfully better than the others. The answer to the first question is yes: every one of the 21 agents beat placebo on response (odds ratio 1.37–2.13). The answer to the second is less satisfying. Amitriptyline, mirtazapine, escitalopram, venlafaxine, and paroxetine came out on top for efficacy. Agomelatine, citalopram, escitalopram, fluoxetine, sertraline, and vortioxetine were best tolerated. Amitriptyline won efficacy but lost tolerability. The league tables got the headlines, but the real lesson is that between-drug differences in efficacy are modest compared to the difference any drug makes over placebo, and tolerability drives real-world effectiveness more than head-to-head efficacy rankings. **Monday morning:** reach for escitalopram, sertraline, or mirtazapine as reasonable first-line SSRIs/SNRIs; reserve amitriptyline for patients willing to accept the anticholinergic burden; use the league tables as tiebreakers, not as commandments. **Caveat:** acute-phase data only, 8-week outcomes, publication bias imperfectly corrected, and the CINeMA confidence ratings were "moderate" to "very low" for most comparisons.

## 6. BALANCE — Geddes 2010

**Citation:** BALANCE investigators and collaborators; Geddes JR, Goodwin GM, Rendell J, et al. Lithium plus valproate combination therapy versus monotherapy for relapse prevention in bipolar I disorder (BALANCE): an open-label randomised trial. *Lancet.* 2010;375(9712):385–395.
**File:** `06-BALANCE-Geddes-2010.m4a`

### The Brief
Before BALANCE, psychiatrists routinely used lithium plus valproate combinations for bipolar I maintenance on the assumption that two mood stabilizers must be better than one — despite no head-to-head trial supporting the combo. BALANCE put the three competing strategies against each other. Geddes and colleagues randomized 330 patients with bipolar I disorder to open-label lithium monotherapy, valproate monotherapy, or the combination, and followed them for up to two years. The primary outcome was initiation of new treatment for a mood episode. Combination therapy and lithium monotherapy both outperformed valproate monotherapy: hazard ratio 0.59 for combination versus valproate (95% CI 0.42–0.83) and 0.71 for lithium versus valproate (0.51–1.00). Lithium and the combination did not differ significantly from each other. The trial restored lithium's status as the gold standard for bipolar I maintenance and clarified that valproate monotherapy is the weakest of the three options. **Monday morning:** in bipolar I maintenance, lithium should be your default first-line choice. Valproate monotherapy is defensible in lithium-intolerant patients but is the weakest option on average. Combination therapy is reasonable for patients with breakthrough episodes on monotherapy. **Caveat:** open-label trial, bipolar I only, and tolerability differences complicate comparisons.

## 7. Cipriani Lithium & Suicide — Cipriani 2013

**Citation:** Cipriani A, Hawton K, Stockton S, Geddes JR. Lithium in the prevention of suicide in mood disorders: updated systematic review and meta-analysis. *BMJ.* 2013;346:f3646.
**File:** `07-Cipriani-Lithium-Suicide-2013.m4a`

### The Brief
Lithium's antisuicide effect is one of the cleanest signals in all of psychiatry, and Cipriani 2013 is the meta-analysis that crystallized it. Cipriani, Hawton, Stockton, and Geddes pooled 48 randomized controlled trials (6,674 patients) comparing lithium with placebo or other active drugs in mood disorders, looking at suicide and deliberate self-harm as primary outcomes. Against placebo, lithium reduced the risk of suicide by roughly 60% (odds ratio 0.13, 95% CI 0.03–0.66) and all-cause mortality by about 38% (OR 0.38, 0.15–0.95). Against other active drugs (including anticonvulsants), lithium also reduced suicide risk. The mechanism is still debated — some combination of impulse control, direct mood stabilization, and possibly specific effects on aggression — but the clinical signal is consistent across trials. **Monday morning:** in any patient with bipolar disorder or recurrent unipolar depression with a history of suicidal behavior or elevated suicide risk, lithium deserves explicit consideration regardless of whether the mood syndrome is textbook. Framing lithium as "the mood stabilizer with an anti-suicide effect" shifts the risk-benefit conversation meaningfully. **Caveat:** the number of suicide events in any individual trial is small, confidence intervals are wide, and lithium's narrow therapeutic window and long-term renal risks must be weighed against the benefit.

## 8. Leucht Antipsychotics Network Meta-Analysis — Leucht 2013

**Citation:** Leucht S, Cipriani A, Spineli L, et al. Comparative efficacy and tolerability of 15 antipsychotic drugs in schizophrenia: a multiple-treatments meta-analysis. *Lancet.* 2013;382(9896):951–962.
**French equivalent of Cipriani 2018 for antipsychotics.*
**File:** `08-Leucht-AP-NMA-2013.m4a`

### The Brief
Leucht 2013 is to antipsychotics what Cipriani 2018 is to antidepressants: a network meta-analysis that lets you compare 15 drugs directly and indirectly from 212 RCTs (43,049 patients) with acute schizophrenia. The efficacy ordering surprised almost no one who had read the literature carefully: clozapine sits well ahead of the pack (effect size on PANSS/BPRS around -0.88 versus placebo). Amisulpride, olanzapine, and risperidone form a second tier. Haloperidol, quetiapine, aripiprazole, sertindole, ziprasidone, chlorpromazine, asenapine, lurasidone, and iloperidone cluster below. The differences within the second and third tiers are modest; the difference between clozapine and everything else is real. Tolerability profiles diverge sharply — olanzapine is the worst for weight, haloperidol the worst for EPS, clozapine the worst for sedation and everything else except EPS. **Monday morning:** when acute efficacy is the dominant concern in treatment-resistant disease, clozapine is the right answer. Among non-clozapine options, differences are small enough that side-effect profile and prior response should dominate the choice. **Caveat:** acute-phase trials (6-week horizon), industry-sponsored trials dominate the evidence base, and the league tables are less informative than they look because within-tier differences are smaller than their confidence intervals.

## 9. Kellner Continuation ECT — Kellner 2006

**Citation:** Kellner CH, Knapp RG, Petrides G, et al. Continuation electroconvulsive therapy vs pharmacotherapy for relapse prevention in major depression: a multisite study from the Consortium for Research in Electroconvulsive Therapy (CORE). *Arch Gen Psychiatry.* 2006;63(12):1337–1344.
**File:** `09-Kellner-ECT-2006.m4a`

### The Brief
ECT has the highest acute response rate of any treatment in major depression — roughly 70–90% — but relapse after acute-course ECT is catastrophic without continuation treatment. The CORE continuation ECT trial tested the two realistic options for patients who had remitted after an acute ECT course. Kellner and the CORE investigators enrolled 531 depressed patients, delivered an open-label acute ECT course, and randomized the 184 remitters to either (a) continuation ECT on a fixed tapering schedule or (b) continuation pharmacotherapy with lithium plus nortriptyline. Follow-up was six months. Six-month relapse rates were statistically indistinguishable: 37.1% for continuation ECT and 31.6% for combination pharmacotherapy — both better than the ~84% relapse observed historically with no continuation treatment. **Monday morning:** if you send a patient for acute ECT, you must have a continuation plan before they finish — either scheduled continuation ECT or aggressive combination pharmacotherapy (lithium-plus-TCA is the evidence-based default; lithium plus venlafaxine is a reasonable modernization). Untreated, two-thirds will relapse within six months. **Caveat:** both arms still had substantial relapse rates; more aggressive maintenance strategies (continuation ECT plus pharmacotherapy together) are worth considering for very high-risk patients.

## 10. Moncrieff Serotonin Umbrella Review — Moncrieff 2022

**Citation:** Moncrieff J, Cooper RE, Stockmann T, Amendola S, Hengartner MP, Horowitz MA. The serotonin theory of depression: a systematic umbrella review of the evidence. *Mol Psychiatry.* 2022;28(8):3243–3256.
**File:** `10-Moncrieff-Discontinuation-2019.m4a` *(existing filename retained for continuity)*

### The Brief
The "chemical imbalance" story — that depression is caused by low serotonin and SSRIs correct it — was one of the most successful popularizations of psychiatric science, and one of the least defensible. Moncrieff and colleagues conducted an umbrella review, synthesizing 17 systematic reviews and meta-analyses across six research lines: CSF 5-HIAA, serotonin and metabolite levels in plasma/platelets, 5-HT1A receptor binding, serotonin transporter (SERT) imaging, tryptophan depletion studies, and SLC6A4 gene association and interaction studies. Across all six lines, the review found no consistent evidence that depressed patients differ from controls in central serotonin or serotonergic activity in a direction that would support the low-serotonin model. The paper does *not* argue that SSRIs don't work — a separate question with its own mixed evidence base — but it does argue that the explanatory story patients and clinicians have been telling each other for thirty years is not supported by the empirical record. **Monday morning:** stop telling patients they have a "chemical imbalance." The honest explanation is that we don't fully understand how SSRIs work, but that they reliably outperform placebo for moderate-to-severe depression and that the mechanism is probably more about neuroplasticity and downstream effects than a simple serotonin deficit. **Caveat:** an umbrella review inherits the methodological limits of its constituent reviews, and absence of consistent evidence is not evidence of absence.

# FAMILY AND SYSTEMS

## 11. Brown Expressed Emotion — Brown 1962

**Citation:** Brown GW, Monck EM, Carstairs GM, Wing JK. Influence of family life on the course of schizophrenic illness. *Br J Prev Soc Med.* 1962;16(2):55–68.
**File:** `11-Brown-EE-1962.m4a`

### The Brief
This is the paper that made the family environment measurable in schizophrenia research and seeded fifty years of family-intervention science. Brown and colleagues followed 128 men with schizophrenia discharged from hospital to different types of households — parental home, marital home, lodgings — and tracked relapse at one year. The striking finding was that patients returning to homes with high levels of expressed emotion (critical comments, hostility, emotional over-involvement) relapsed at roughly double the rate of those returning to low-EE environments. The methodology was agnostic to diagnosis and specific etiology; what mattered was the measurable emotional temperature of the household. This was the origin of the "high-EE" construct that would later be operationalized in the Camberwell Family Interview and reproduced in dozens of studies across cultures. **Monday morning:** when you're planning discharge from an inpatient unit, the receiving environment is a modifiable relapse risk factor. Ask about criticism, over-involvement, and household friction — don't assume "home" means "safe." **Caveat:** small sample, all-male, 1950s UK context. The original numbers have been refined many times; the conceptual contribution is the durable landmark, not the specific percentages.

## 12. Leff Family Intervention — Leff 1982

**Citation:** Leff J, Kuipers L, Berkowitz R, Eberlein-Vries R, Sturgeon D. A controlled trial of social intervention in the families of schizophrenic patients. *Br J Psychiatry.* 1982;141:121–134.
**File:** `12-Leff-Family-Intervention-1982.m4a`

### The Brief
Twenty years after Brown discovered that high expressed emotion predicted relapse, the question became: if we intervene in the family, does the relapse curve bend? Leff and colleagues answered it with the first randomized controlled trial of family intervention in schizophrenia. They recruited 24 patients living with high-EE relatives, on antipsychotic medication, and randomized them to routine outpatient care versus a structured package combining relatives' education, a relatives' group, and family sessions aimed at lowering criticism and over-involvement. Over nine months of follow-up, relapse was 50% in the control group versus 9% in the intervention group — a difference that was both statistically and clinically striking for a sample of 24. The mechanism appeared to be the intended one: EE measurably dropped in intervention families, and the patients whose family EE dropped were the ones who stayed well. **Monday morning:** family psychoeducation and EE-lowering work are not soft add-ons to schizophrenia care; they belong in every discharge plan where the patient is returning to a high-EE household. **Caveat:** small sample, single-site, requires trained facilitators, and the 9% figure is almost certainly too rosy for generic community implementation — but the direction of effect has been replicated many times (see Pharoah Cochrane, paper #20).

## 13. Falloon Family Management — Falloon 1982

**Citation:** Falloon IR, Boyd JL, McGill CW, et al. Family management in the prevention of exacerbations of schizophrenia: a controlled study. *N Engl J Med.* 1982;306(24):1437–1440.
**File:** `13-Falloon-Family-Therapy-1982.m4a`

### The Brief
Falloon's NEJM paper — published the same year as Leff's BJP trial — is the second pillar of the family-intervention evidence base in schizophrenia. Falloon and colleagues randomized 36 patients with schizophrenia who were living with families to either individual management (clinic-based medication management plus supportive individual therapy) or behavioral family management delivered in the home, emphasizing communication training, structured problem-solving, and education about the illness. Both groups received optimal medication. At nine months, the family-therapy group had a 6% exacerbation rate versus 44% in the individual group. Hospitalization rates followed the same pattern. The clinical significance is amplified by the venue: a NEJM paper in 1982 legitimized family therapy for schizophrenia in mainstream medical thinking and shifted the field from "family therapy as adjunct" to "family therapy as core." **Monday morning:** structured, behavioral, problem-solving family work is as evidence-based as any antipsychotic choice in community-dwelling patients with schizophrenia. Delivery in the home is part of the recipe, not an optional luxury. **Caveat:** small sample, specialized therapist training, and the outsized effect sizes of early family-intervention trials have softened as studies scaled up (see Pharoah Cochrane).

## 23. Wampold Common Factors — Wampold 2001

**Citation:** Wampold BE. *The Great Psychotherapy Debate: Models, Methods, and Findings.* Mahwah, NJ: Lawrence Erlbaum; 2001. (Key meta-analytic chapters published concurrently.)
**File:** `23-Wampold-Common-Factors-2001.m4a`

### The Brief
Wampold's book-length synthesis is the rigorous empirical case for the "dodo bird verdict" — the claim that all bona fide psychotherapies produce roughly equivalent outcomes, and that most of the variance in outcome is explained by factors common to all therapies (alliance, empathy, expectancy, allegiance, therapist effects) rather than specific techniques. ## 26. Rosenhan Pseudopatient — Rosenhan 1973

**Citation:** Rosenhan DL. On being sane in insane places. *Science.* 1973;179(4070):250–258.
**File:** `26-Rosenhan-Pseudopatient-1973.m4a`

### The Brief
Rosenhan's 1973 *Science* paper is the single most cited challenge to the reliability of psychiatric diagnosis in the 20th century. Ask yourself what observations would falsify it. Be especially skeptical when "normal" behavior on a unit is being reinterpreted as diagnostic confirmation. **Caveat:** Susannah Cahalan's 2019 investigation cast doubt on several of Rosenhan's claimed case details and some pseudopatients may not have existed; the paper should be read as a provocation, not a trial.

## 43. ACT Stein & Santos — Stein & Santos 1998

### The Brief

## 47. Volkow Addiction Brain Disease — Volkow 2016

**Citation:** Volkow ND, Koob GF, McLellan AT. Neurobiologic advances from the brain disease model of addiction. *N Engl J Med.* 2016;374(4):363–371.
**File:** `47-Volkow-Addiction-Brain-2016.m4a`

### The Brief
Volkow, Koob, and McLellan's 2016 NEJM review is the most authoritative synthesis of the brain disease model of addiction. The paper lays out three neurobiological systems that together explain the clinical phenomenology of addiction: (1) the reward system — mesolimbic dopamine pathway — which is hijacked by drugs that produce abnormally large dopamine releases and then downregulates, producing tolerance and anhedonia; (2) the stress and antireward system — extended amygdala, corticotropin-releasing factor, dynorphin — which becomes sensitized during withdrawal and drives the dysphoria and craving that precipitate relapse; and (3) the executive control system — prefrontal cortex — which is weakened, reducing the ability to inhibit drug-seeking even when the patient consciously wants to stop. Offer buprenorphine or methadone; these are the interventions with the strongest evidence for reducing overdose death. **Caveat:** the brain disease model has been criticized for potentially minimizing the social and motivational dimensions of addiction; the strongest defenses of the model, including Volkow's own writing, explicitly treat it as complementary to psychosocial frameworks, not a replacement.
