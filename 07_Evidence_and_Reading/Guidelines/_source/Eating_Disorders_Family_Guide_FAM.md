## Why This Matters

Eating disorders are the most lethal psychiatric illnesses. They are also among the most treatable, especially when caught early and when the family is part of the recovery plan.

If your loved one has an eating disorder, you are likely living in fear — fear of the next meal, the next weigh-in, the next number on the scale. You may have been told to "stay out of it" by older clinical models. That advice is outdated. Modern evidence — especially from Family-Based Treatment (FBT) — shows that families who are coached, calm, and *involved in refeeding* drive better outcomes than families who step back.

You are not the cause. You can be a powerful part of the cure.

---

## What an Eating Disorder Means for Your Family

Eating disorders are not about food. They are about how your loved one's brain has learned to regulate fear, control, shame, and pain — using food, weight, or their body as the lever. Once underway, starvation, binge cycles, or purging change the brain in ways that make the disorder self-reinforcing. Your loved one literally cannot think clearly until the body is renourished.

This is why "just eat" never works. The eating disorder is not a choice your loved one is making. It is, quite often, a voice in their head that is louder than yours — and that voice is *lying* to them about food, their body, and what they deserve.

A core skill for families is **externalizing** the illness: it is not your daughter who screamed at the dinner table. It is the eating disorder. Your daughter is still in there.

---

## The Two Family Patterns That Hurt Most

### Criticism

Criticism in eating disorders is uniquely toxic because the eating-disorder voice already weaponizes every body comment. *"You've gained weight" / "You're being dramatic about food" / "Look what you're doing to this family"* — even when said in pain — fuels the very illness you are trying to fight.

### Overinvolvement (and Walking on Eggshells)

The opposite trap is just as common. Hiding scales, never serving carbs, eating only "safe" foods to avoid a meltdown, monitoring every bite — this can slide into the family being *run by* the eating disorder rather than fighting it. The illness gets stronger. Everyone gets smaller.

The middle path is **calm, expectant, externalized firmness**: "I love you. The eating disorder doesn't get to decide what's on the table. We are eating this together."

---

## What NOT to Say — and What to Say Instead

| Don't Say | Try Instead |
|---|---|
| "You look so healthy now!" | "I'm glad you're here." (Avoid all body comments — positive or negative.) |
| "Just eat. It's not that hard." | "I know this meal is loud for you. I'm going to sit with you while you finish." |
| "You're being so dramatic." | "The eating disorder is yelling right now. We're going to keep going anyway." |
| "Are you sure you should eat that?" | (Say nothing. Comments on food choices feed the illness.) |
| "I wish I had your willpower." | (Avoid envy framing — it validates the disorder's logic.) |
| "Look what you're doing to your sister." | "This is a family illness. We're all going to recover together, and that includes me." |

## How to Support Without Rescuing

1. **Externalize the illness.** Speak about "the eating disorder" as separate from your loved one. "The ED is loud today" — not "you are being difficult."
2. **Sit with hard meals.** Your calm presence at the table — without lecturing — is therapy.
3. **Don't negotiate with the disorder.** Meal plans, weights, and clinical decisions are between your loved one and their treatment team. You are the support, not the dietitian.
4. **Keep the household running.** Eating disorders thrive on chaos. Predictable mornings, predictable dinners, predictable bedtimes are protective.
5. **Don't make body comments — about anyone.** Not your loved one, not yourself, not celebrities. Your loved one is listening.
6. **Hold your boundaries.** "We will not buy diet products. We will not skip family meals. We will not lock up the bathroom door."

## Self-Care for Caregivers

Eating-disorder caregiving has some of the highest burnout rates in mental health. Protect yourself.

- **Get your own clinician** familiar with eating disorder family work.
- **Eat your own meals normally and visibly.** Your relationship with food is being modeled in real time.
- **Find an FBT-informed support group** (F.E.A.S.T., NAMI, NEDA) — community matters.
- **Sleep.** Refeeding without sleep is a recipe for parental collapse.
- **Schedule respite.** Two hours a week, on the calendar, no apologies.
- **Track your own stress.** Use the *Family Stress Map* — caregiver depletion in ED families predicts treatment dropout.

## When to Get Emergency Help

Eating disorders can become medical emergencies quickly. Call 911 or go to the nearest emergency department if you notice:

- Fainting, chest pain, or irregular heartbeat
- Heart rate below 50 or blood pressure that drops when standing
- Vomiting blood, blood in stool, or coffee-ground vomit
- Confusion, severe weakness, or inability to stand
- Seizure
- Active suicidal intent or recent overdose (laxatives, diet pills, other)

Call your loved one's clinician — same day — if:

- Weight is dropping despite a meal plan
- Purging or restriction is intensifying
- New self-harm
- Withdrawal from treatment

**24/7 resources:**
- **988** (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline) — call or text
- **Crisis Text Line:** text HOME to 741741

**Eating disorder treatment referrals (not a 24/7 line):**
- **National Alliance for Eating Disorders Helpline:** 1-866-662-1235 (Mon–Fri, 9 am–7 pm ET) — free, clinician-staffed help finding specialized care

## Reflection Questions (bring to your next session)

1. What is one accommodation your family has made that has begun to feel like *the ED is in charge*?
2. When you talk *to* the eating disorder versus *to* your loved one, what changes?
3. Who in your family needs more support right now and isn't getting it?
4. What is one body comment, food rule, or diet behavior in the household you can drop this week?

---

## The Key Idea

> The eating disorder is not your loved one. It is an illness lying to them. Your job is not to argue with the illness — your job is to keep your loved one nourished, predictable, and surrounded by calm love until the illness loses its grip. Recovery is the most common outcome. Your steadiness is part of the medicine.
