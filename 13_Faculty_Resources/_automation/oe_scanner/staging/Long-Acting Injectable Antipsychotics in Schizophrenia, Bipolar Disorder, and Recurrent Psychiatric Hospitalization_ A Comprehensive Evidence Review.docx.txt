



Long-Acting Injectable Antipsychotics in Schizophrenia, Bipolar Disorder, and Recurrent Psychiatric Hospitalization: A Comprehensive Evidence Review


---

1. Scope and Framing


This review synthesizes evidence across RCTs, mirror-image studies, nationwide cohort studies, meta-analyses, and clinical practice guidelines on long-acting injectable antipsychotics (LAIs) for schizophrenia spectrum disorders and bipolar I disorder. Coverage includes efficacy/effectiveness data, mortality outcomes, patient selection, initiation logistics, equity concerns, and practical clinical tools. The evidence base spans >400,000 patients across multiple study designs and is current through mid-2026.

---

2. The Efficacy-Effectiveness Gap: RCTs vs. Mirror-Image Studies


The most important methodological insight in the LAI literature is the systematic divergence in effect sizes between study designs. The landmark Kishimoto et al. (2021) meta-analysis in The Lancet Psychiatry (137 studies, 397,319 patients) directly quantified this gap for the primary outcome of hospitalization or relapse:[1]



The ~10-fold difference in NNT (45 in RCTs vs. 4 in mirror-image studies) reflects fundamental design-related biases.[1] RCTs select adherent patients willing to be randomized and provide intensive monitoring that improves oral-arm adherence, thereby minimizing the very advantage LAIs are designed to provide.[2][3] Mirror-image studies may overestimate benefit through regression to the mean (LAIs are often initiated after a crisis) and expectation bias.[4][1] Cohort studies introduce a conservative bias because clinicians preferentially prescribe LAIs to more severely ill patients (Hedges' g = 0.15, p = 0.01 for illness severity).[5]

The Schneider-Thoma et al. (2022) Lancet network meta-analysis (127 RCTs, 18,152 participants) confirmed that within RCTs, LAIs were not more efficacious than their oral counterparts for relapse prevention, while noting that the risk difference in RCTs with broad inclusion criteria was only ~2%.[2] The Ostuzzi et al. (2022) World Psychiatry network meta-analysis (92 RCTs, 22,645 patients) concluded that olanzapine, aripiprazole, and paliperidone are the best choices for maintenance treatment, as both LAI and oral formulations of these drugs are among the best-performing treatments.[6]

The "true" effect size likely lies between these extremes. Within-individual designs from Scandinavian registries — which eliminate between-patient confounding by using each patient as their own control — provide perhaps the most informative estimates, suggesting a 20–50% reduction in rehospitalization with LAIs.[7][8][9]

3. Landmark Trials


PRELAPSE (Kane et al., 2020)


This cluster-randomized trial (489 patients, 39 US sites, 2-year follow-up) in early-phase schizophrenia (age 18–35, <5 years antipsychotic use) randomized clinics — not patients — to encourage aripiprazole once-monthly (AOM) vs. usual care. By randomizing at the clinic level, PRELAPSE partially overcame the individual-level selection bias inherent in traditional RCTs. AOM produced a significant 44% reduction in incidence rate of first hospitalization (NNT = 7).[10]

The following figure from the PRELAPSE trial shows Kaplan-Meier survival curves demonstrating the probability of remaining hospitalization-free over 2 years, with AOM consistently superior to clinician's choice.

Figure 2 Time Remaining Without Having a First Hospitalization

PRIDE (Alphs et al., 2015)


This 15-month RCT of 444 patients with schizophrenia and a history of incarceration found paliperidone palmitate monthly (PP1M) significantly delayed time to first treatment failure vs. oral antipsychotics (HR 1.43, 95% CI 1.09–1.88; p = 0.011). Treatment failure rates were 39.8% (PP1M) vs. 53.7% (oral), with arrest/incarceration being the most common reason for treatment failure (21.2% vs. 29.4%).[11]

EULAST (2023)


This pragmatic, open-label European RCT (511 patients, 50 sites, 15 countries) randomized early-phase schizophrenia patients 1:1:1:1 to LAI paliperidone, LAI aripiprazole, oral paliperidone, or oral aripiprazole. No significant difference in all-cause discontinuation was found between combined LAI and oral groups (HR 1.16, 95% CI 0.94–1.43, p = 0.18).[3] However, a critical secondary analysis found that among patients with comorbid substance use disorder (n = 143), LAIs reduced all-cause discontinuation by 36% (adjusted HR 0.641, 95% CI 0.438–0.938, p = 0.022), while no benefit was seen in those without SUD.[12]

PROACTIVE (Buckley et al., 2015)


This 30-month pragmatic RCT (305 patients, 8 US academic centers) comparing LAI risperidone vs. physician's choice oral SGAs found no significant difference in time to first relapse (42% LAI vs. 32% oral). The investigators noted that biweekly monitoring visits required for LAI administration may have contributed to low relapse rates in both arms.[13][14]

Subotnik et al. (2015)


In first-episode schizophrenia, LAI risperidone showed a 6-fold lower 12-month relapse rate (5% vs. 33%) compared to oral risperidone, providing some of the strongest RCT evidence for early LAI use.[15]

4. Relapse Prevention


Schizophrenia


The evidence for LAI-mediated relapse prevention is strongest in real-world settings. The Finnish FIN20 cohort (62,250 patients, up to 20-year follow-up) found the lowest rehospitalization risk with olanzapine LAI (adjusted HR 0.46), clozapine (HR 0.51), and paliperidone LAI (HR 0.51).[8][16] Among first-episode patients, the benefit was even more pronounced: flupentixol LAI (HR 0.24), olanzapine LAI (HR 0.26), and perphenazine LAI (HR 0.39).[8]

The Hamina et al. (2024) Finnish cohort (131,000+ patients) confirmed paliperidone 3-month LAI as the most effective formulation for relapse prevention (AHR 0.66 vs. oral olanzapine), followed by aripiprazole LAI (AHR 0.77) and olanzapine LAI (AHR 0.79). Critically, LAIs were more effective in the incident cohort (AHR 0.74) than the prevalent cohort (AHR 0.84), supporting early initiation.[9]

Bipolar I Disorder


The evidence base for LAIs in bipolar disorder is thinner but directionally consistent. Aripiprazole monohydrate (Abilify Maintena) is FDA-approved for bipolar I maintenance monotherapy based on a 52-week randomized withdrawal trial showing significantly delayed time to recurrence of any mood episode (HR 0.45; 95% CI 0.30–0.68; p < 0.0001), with relapse rates of 26.5% vs. 51.1%.[17][18] Risperidone LAI (Risperdal Consta) is approved as monotherapy or adjunctive therapy to lithium or valproate for bipolar I maintenance.[19] Both LAIs primarily prevent mania rather than depression.[20][21][22]

A Finnish nationwide cohort found LAIs reduced psychiatric rehospitalization by 30% vs. oral counterparts in bipolar disorder (HR 0.70).[23] An observational meta-analysis (17 studies) showed LAIs were superior to oral antipsychotics in preventing relapse/hospitalization in both cohort studies (RR 0.63, 95% CI 0.44–0.90) and mirror-image studies (RR 0.46, 95% CI 0.28–0.77).[24]

5. Hospitalization Reduction


Magnitude of Effect


A US meta-analysis of real-world studies (57 publications) found LAIs reduced odds of hospitalization by 38% (OR 0.62, 95% CI 0.54–0.71), hospitalization rate by 25% (IRR 0.75), and ER admissions by 14% (IRR 0.86, 95% CI 0.77–0.97).[25] The Wei et al. (2022) Hong Kong within-subject study found a 48% reduction in psychiatric hospitalizations (IRR 0.52) and 44% reduction in suicide attempts (IRR 0.56).[26]

The following figure from Wei et al. (2022) shows a comprehensive forest plot of subgroup analyses comparing LAIs vs. oral antipsychotics across multiple outcomes, demonstrating consistent benefits across sex, age, initiation timing, and substance use status.

Figure 2 Subgroup Analyses for the Use of Long-Acting Injectable Antipsychotics (LAIAs) vs Oral Antipsychotics (OAs) During the Full Treatment Period

Progressive Benefit Over Time


A 6-year mirror-image study of paliperidone palmitate 3-monthly (PP3M) showed mean psychiatric bed days declined from ~30 days/year pre-treatment to ~13 days/year post-treatment, with bed days reaching zero by year 3 of continuous treatment.[27]

The following figure from Clark et al. (2024) illustrates this progressive elimination of hospitalization over 3 years of continuous PP3M treatment.

Figure 1 (A) Mean number of bed days per year 3 years before and 3 years after PPLAI initiation for patients who continued PP3M, with standard error. (B) Mean number of bed days shown by year, 3 years before and 3 years after PPLAI initiation for patients who continued PP3M, with standard error.

Cost Implications


Pharmacy costs increase with LAIs (~$5,603/year), but medical costs decrease by a comparable amount (~$5,404/year), resulting in no significant net difference in total healthcare costs.[25] A Canadian study found total all-cause costs were $33,788 lower per patient in the 2 years post-LAI initiation vs. pre-initiation.[28] A Medicaid study found that after applying cumulative rebates, PP1M initiation resulted in significant total healthcare cost savings of $231 per patient per month.[29]

6. Mortality Outcomes


All-Cause Mortality


Real-world evidence consistently shows LAIs are associated with reduced all-cause mortality compared to oral antipsychotics. The Aymerich et al. (2025) meta-analysis (17 studies, n = 12,042) found LAIs reduced all-cause mortality by 21% (OR 0.79, 95% CI 0.66–0.95), with more pronounced benefit in first-episode psychosis.[30] The Correll et al. (2022) World Psychiatry meta-analysis (135 studies, N = 4,536,447) found SGA-LAIs had the largest protective effect against all-cause mortality among all antipsychotic regimens (RR 0.39, 95% CI 0.27–0.56).[31]



The following table from Huang et al. (2021) shows mortality outcomes comparing LAI and oral antipsychotic groups across all-cause, natural-cause, and suicide mortality, with consistent reductions favoring LAIs.

Table 2 All-Cause, Natural, and Suicide Mortality Risks in 4924 Patients With Schizophrenia Who Switched to LAIs Compared With Their OAP Using Counterparts

Cardiovascular Mortality


The FIN20 study found any antipsychotic use vs. nonuse reduced cardiovascular mortality by 38% (aHR 0.62, 95% CI 0.57–0.67); clozapine reduced it by 45%.[16] The Wei et al. (2025) study in older adults confirmed no significant increase in cardiovascular hospital admissions with LAIs (IRR 1.04, 0.86–1.25).[33]

Suicide Mortality


Evidence on LAIs and suicide is mixed and generally not statistically significant in most individual studies. The Aymerich et al. meta-analysis found no significant difference for suicidal mortality (OR 0.86, 95% CI 0.59–1.26).[30] However, early LAI use (within 2 years of diagnosis) may reduce suicide mortality by 47%.[32] SGA-LAIs appear preferable: FGA-LAIs were associated with increased suicide risk in one Taiwanese cohort (HR 1.52, p = 0.01).[34]

Timing Matters


A Taiwanese matched cohort found that switching to LAIs within the first 3 years of treatment was associated with markedly lower all-cause mortality (HR 0.49, 95% CI 0.27–0.87) and natural-cause mortality (HR 0.30, 95% CI 0.15–0.60). Late switching (after 3 years) showed no mortality benefit.[35]

Critical Caveat: Continuity Matters


A Taiwanese study of first-admission schizophrenia found that patients who received LAIs but discontinued early had worse outcomes than those who never received LAIs, while those who continued LAIs had the best outcomes. This highlights that LAI initiation without continuity may be counterproductive.[38]

8. Shared Decision-Making


Clinicians Overestimate Patient Resistance


A consistent finding across multiple studies is that clinicians overestimate patient resistance to LAIs.[39][40] The PRELAPSE trial demonstrated that with proper training, practitioners can communicate LAI advantages even in early illness stages and engage patients in shared decision-making, resulting in high acceptance rates. The investigators emphasized that "the use of LAIs does not deprive patients of autonomy or choice. The choice is made each time an injection is administered".[10]

Patient Perspectives


A 2025 systematic review of 39 qualitative studies (1,477 patients) identified three core themes: (1) perception and experience of medication, (2) social dynamics and influence, and (3) trust and communication with healthcare providers. Side effects and lack of information were frequently cited concerns. While LAIs were linked to symptom stability and functional improvements, many patients lacked adequate information about their effects.[41]

Barriers to Informed Decision-Making


A focused ethnographic study identified that discussions about LAIs were restricted to patients with nonadherence or repeated hospitalizations, with inadequate patient education and limited therapist knowledge about LAIs.[42] Canadian focus groups found that 9 of 34 patients had never heard of LAIs at all.[43] The multinational ADVANCE study found that patients initially treated in outpatient settings with strong support were more likely to accept LAIs, while those first treated during acute inpatient episodes often feared treatment and felt a lack of control.[44]

9. Equity and Coercion Concerns


Racial Disparities: A Two-Layered Problem


The evidence reveals a dual disparity. First, Black patients are more likely to receive LAIs overall (AOR 1.38, 95% CI 1.31–1.46 in a national Medicare sample of 210,686 patients).[45] Second — and more clinically consequential — among LAI users, Black patients receive inferior first-generation LAIs at higher rates. In Medicare LAI users, Black patients had substantially lower rates of SGA-LAI use than White patients (adjusted 60.1% vs. 69.9%; AOR 0.63, 95% CI 0.60–0.66).[45] In Medicaid beneficiaries, Black patients had higher FGA-LAI utilization (46.8% vs. 38.9%; OR 1.47).[46] FGA-LAIs showed significantly earlier discontinuation than SGA-LAIs (HR 2.12), suggesting this disparity may contribute to poorer outcomes.[47]

The APA Resource Document on Anti-Racist Mental Health Policies identifies that Black patients are "more likely to be treated with first generation antipsychotics, more likely to be treated with higher medication doses, and more likely to receive long acting injectable formulations" as a consequence of racialized clinical descriptors.[48]

Perceived Coercion


A cross-sectional study found that mean total coercion levels were significantly higher for depot than oral formulations (4.39 vs. 2.80, p = 0.027), with 30% of depot patients feeling people tried to force them to take medication vs. 2% of oral patients (p < 0.001).[49] The US DECIDE survey found that clinicians with high LAI use were more likely to "use any means necessary to ensure that a patient is on an LAI" (44–51%) and had used guardianship to assist with treatment (69–70%).[50]

Assisted Outpatient Treatment (AOT)


A Cochrane review (3 RCTs, n = 749) found no significant differences between compulsory community treatment and voluntary care for readmission (RR 0.98, 95% CI 0.82–1.16), estimating it would take 142 orders to prevent one readmission.[51][52] However, the APA Resource Document notes that sustained periods of outpatient commitment (≥6 months), when linked to intensive services, may improve outcomes.[53]

The Ethical Paradox


The same formulation can represent both coercive overuse (when disproportionately imposed on minority patients without shared decision-making) and inequitable underuse (when minority patients are denied access to newer SGA-LAIs with better tolerability). Roberts and Geppert's ethical analysis argues that considerations of justice may actually indicate LAIs should be used more frequently and earlier for select patients — reframing the equity concern as one of under-access rather than over-imposition.[54]

10. Guideline Positions


A systematic review of 19 schizophrenia clinical practice guidelines found that 18 of 19 (94.7%) commented on LAIs, with the most common indications being nonadherence (77.8%), maintenance care (72.2%), and patient preference (66.7%). Only 5 of 18 (27.8%) recommended LAIs for first-episode schizophrenia.[55]



Sources:[55][56][57][58][59][60]

There is a growing disconnect between traditional guideline conservatism and accumulating evidence favoring early LAI use. Expert consensus increasingly frames LAIs as "not a last resort but a viable first-line/early-phase treatment option".[61]

11. Initiation Logistics and Oral Overlap Requirements




Sources:[62][63][17][64][65][66]

Key Practical Pearls


- Paliperidone palmitate is the only LAI with a true pharmacokinetic loading strategy that eliminates oral overlap entirely.[62][67]

- Risperdal Consta has the most burdensome oral overlap (21 days) due to its unique microsphere release kinetics with a 3-week lag before the main release phase begins.[65]

- Aristada Initio was specifically developed as a NanoCrystal dispersion to provide a 1-day alternative to the 21-day oral overlap.[68][69]

- Zyprexa Relprevv is the only LAI requiring a REMS program with mandatory 3-hour post-injection observation for post-injection delirium/sedation syndrome (PDSS), which occurs in <0.1% of injections but ~2% of patients over long-term treatment.[66]

- For patients naïve to paliperidone/risperidone, establish tolerability with oral paliperidone or oral risperidone before initiating the LAI.[63][65]

- CYP2D6 poor metabolizers on Abilify Maintena should start and maintain at 300 mg rather than 400 mg.[17]

14. Initiation Algorithm


Step 1: Candidate Identification

- Any patient with schizophrenia spectrum disorder or bipolar I disorder (manic-predominant polarity)

- Prioritize: nonadherence history, early-phase illness, comorbid SUD, justice involvement, frequent hospitalizations, patient preference

Step 2: Shared Decision-Making

- Normalize the conversation: "Many patients find that a monthly/bimonthly injection helps them stay well without having to remember daily pills"

- Address misconceptions proactively; do not restrict discussion to nonadherent patients

- Confirm tolerability with oral equivalent if patient is antipsychotic-naïve

Step 3: Select Formulation (based on clinical profile)

- Metabolic concerns / SUD comorbidity / bipolar I → Aripiprazole LAI (Abilify Maintena or Aristada)

- Need for rapid therapeutic levels / plan for extended intervals → Paliperidone palmitate (Sustenna/Erzofri → Trinza → Hafyera)

- Bipolar I adjunctive to mood stabilizer → Risperidone LAI (Risperdal Consta)

- Oral olanzapine responder, other options failed → Olanzapine pamoate (Zyprexa Relprevv, if REMS-capable setting)

Step 4: Initiate

- Verify tolerability with oral equivalent (if naïve)

- Use 1-day initiation protocols when available to minimize oral overlap burden

- Schedule follow-up injection before patient leaves clinic

- Document dosing window and missed-dose protocol in chart

Step 5: Transition to Extended Intervals (when stable)

- PP1M → PP3M after ≥4 months of adequate PP1M treatment[70]

- PP3M → PP6M after ≥1 cycle of PP3M[71]

- Aristada: consider Q6W (882 mg) or Q2M (1064 mg) dosing

15. Counseling Script


> "I'd like to talk with you about a different way to take your medication. Instead of pills every day, there's an option for a shot that works for [2 weeks / 1 month / 2 months / 3 months / 6 months]. It's the same type of medicine, just delivered differently.

>

> Many of my patients tell me they prefer it because they don't have to think about medication every day. It can help keep the medicine at a steady level in your body, which may help you stay well and out of the hospital.

>

> This is not a punishment or something we only offer when things aren't going well. It's actually something that works best when we start it early. You would come to the clinic for your appointment, get the injection, and that's it until next time.

>

> Some things to know: [For aripiprazole: the most common side effect is restlessness/akathisia, and it tends to have less weight gain. For paliperidone/risperidone: it can raise prolactin levels, which we'll monitor. For olanzapine: weight gain is a concern, and you'd need to stay at the clinic for 3 hours after each shot for monitoring.]

>

> You're always in control of this decision. If you try it and don't like it, we can switch back. The injection wears off over time — it's not permanent. What questions do you have?"

This script incorporates evidence-based principles: normalizing LAI use rather than framing it as a last resort, emphasizing patient autonomy and reversibility, providing balanced information about side effects, and using plain language.[61][10][41][44][43]

16. Evidence Gaps and Emerging Data


Several important uncertainties remain:

- No validated patient selection algorithm exists; current guidance relies on clinical risk factors rather than formal scoring tools.[37]

- Head-to-head LAI comparisons are scarce; most evidence compares LAIs to oral antipsychotics rather than to each other.

- Bipolar depression prevention has not been demonstrated with any currently available LAI; both aripiprazole and risperidone LAIs primarily prevent mania.[20][21][22]

- Paliperidone palmitate is not FDA-approved for bipolar disorder (only schizoaffective disorder), limiting its formal use in bipolar populations.[72]

- The intersection of race, involuntary treatment, and LAI prescribing — specifically whether Black patients are more likely to receive LAIs involuntarily vs. voluntarily — remains under-researched.[73]

- Aripiprazole 2-month ready-to-use (Ari 2MRTU 960 mg) has shown comparable efficacy to AOM 400 mg in bipolar I in a 32-week open-label trial, potentially expanding options.[74][75]

- Immortal time bias may affect some observational mortality estimates; a 2024 Canadian study raised concerns that after correcting for this bias, the protective association of antipsychotics with mortality was attenuated.[76]

---

17. Summary


The totality of evidence supports LAIs as a highly effective strategy for reducing relapse, hospitalization, and mortality in schizophrenia spectrum disorders, with emerging evidence in bipolar I disorder. The benefit is most pronounced in early-phase illness, nonadherent patients, those with comorbid substance use, and justice-involved populations. Real-world studies consistently show 20–50% reductions in rehospitalization and 20–60% reductions in all-cause mortality with LAIs vs. oral antipsychotics, with the caveat that RCTs show more modest effects due to selection of adherent patients. Guidelines are evolving from positioning LAIs as a last resort for nonadherence toward recognizing them as a viable early-phase treatment option. Clinicians should engage in shared decision-making, offer LAIs proactively rather than punitively, and remain vigilant about racial disparities in both LAI access and formulation quality.
