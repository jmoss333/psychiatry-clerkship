
The Psychiatry Resident Psychopharmacology Canon: An Evidence-Based Compendium


---

I. ANTIPSYCHOTICS


Landmark Effectiveness Trials


CATIE (2005) enrolled 1,460 patients with chronic schizophrenia across 57 US sites in a double-blind, NIMH-funded pragmatic trial comparing olanzapine, quetiapine, risperidone, ziprasidone, and perphenazine.[1] The primary finding — that 74% of patients discontinued before 18 months (median 4.6 months) — underscored the profound tolerability challenges across all agents.[1] Olanzapine had the longest time to discontinuation (9.2 months) but the greatest metabolic burden; perphenazine was comparably effective to most SGAs, challenging the assumed class superiority of second-generation agents.[1][2] In Phase 2, clozapine was superior to other SGAs for patients who failed Phase 1 due to lack of efficacy.[2]

CUtLASS (2006) randomized 227 UK patients to FGA vs. SGA classes and found no advantage for SGAs in quality of life, symptoms, or cost-effectiveness, with a trend actually favoring FGAs.[4] CUtLASS Band 2 confirmed clozapine's superiority over other SGAs in treatment-resistant populations.[5]

EUFEST (2008) studied 498 first-episode patients and found all SGAs had lower discontinuation rates than low-dose haloperidol, but no differences in PANSS total score change by mixed-model analysis.[6][7] Response rates ranged from 67% (amisulpride, olanzapine) to 37% (haloperidol).[6]

Network Meta-Analyses: The Efficacy Hierarchy


Three successive NMAs have established a consistent efficacy hierarchy. The Leucht et al. 2013 NMA (212 RCTs, 43,049 participants) first demonstrated that not all antipsychotics are equally effective, with clozapine (SMD 0.88) significantly more effective than all others.[8] The Huhn et al. 2019 update (402 RCTs, 53,463 participants) confirmed that clozapine, amisulpride, olanzapine, and risperidone were significantly more efficacious than multiple other agents, and that side-effect differences were more marked than efficacy differences.[3] The Schneider-Thoma et al. 2026 update (438 RCTs, 78,193 participants) incorporated xanomeline-trospium — the first non-dopaminergic antipsychotic — and reaffirmed the same top-tier agents.[9]

For maintenance treatment, the Schneider-Thoma et al. 2022 NMA (127 RCTs, 18,152 participants) found no clear superiority of specific antipsychotics for relapse prevention, suggesting choice should be guided mainly by tolerability.[10]

Key Safety Controversies


Metabolic effects remain the dominant tolerability concern. The 2004 ADA/APA Consensus Statement established monitoring recommendations, yet metabolic screening remains dismally low (baseline glucose ~23%, lipids ~8%).[11][12] The metabolic risk hierarchy is well-established: clozapine and olanzapine carry the highest risk; ziprasidone, lurasidone, and aripiprazole the lowest.[3] GLP-1 receptor agonists (e.g., semaglutide) are emerging as promising interventions for antipsychotic-induced metabolic effects.[13]

Mortality in elderly with dementia prompted the 2005 FDA black box warning (1.6–1.7× increased mortality risk), extended to all antipsychotics in 2008.[14][15] NNH estimates range from 26 (haloperidol) to 50 (quetiapine) over 180 days.[14]

II. ANTIDEPRESSANTS


STARD: The Foundational Pragmatic Trial


STARD enrolled 4,041 outpatients across 41 sites in the largest pragmatic antidepressant trial. Step 1 citalopram achieved ~33% remission; subsequent steps showed diminishing returns with no antidepressant clearly superior to another at any step. The originally reported cumulative remission rate of ~67% was challenged by a 2023 reanalysis using protocol-stipulated blinded ratings, which found the actual rate was [16][17]35.0% — approximately half the original figure.[18]

Cipriani 2018: The Definitive NMA


The landmark NMA (522 RCTs, 116,477 patients, 21 antidepressants) confirmed all 21 antidepressants were more effective than placebo (ORs 1.37–2.13).[18] For the best balance of efficacy and acceptability, escitalopram and sertraline ranked in the top half for both domains.[18] A "novelty effect" was identified: drugs appeared 1.18× more effective when they were the experimental agent.[18]

The Efficacy Debate


The Kirsch meta-analysis (2008) and Fournier patient-level meta-analysis (2010) argued that the drug-placebo difference (~1.8–2.0 HDRS points) was below clinical significance thresholds except in very severe depression.[19] Counter-evidence from Gibbons et al. (2012) and Hieronymus et al. (2019), using patient-level data, found antidepressants effective across the full severity spectrum.[19] A Bayesian meta-analysis (169 RCTs) found the variability ratio was ~1.0, suggesting a near-constant, uniform treatment effect rather than distinct responder/non-responder subgroups.[20]

Publication Bias: Turner et al. (2008)


This landmark NEJM study compared 74 FDA-registered trials with their published counterparts and found 94% of published trials appeared positive vs. only 51% per FDA analysis, with effect size inflation of 32% overall.[20]

FDA Black Box Warning and Suicidality


The 2004 pediatric and 2007 young adult (<25 years) black box warnings were based on a meta-analysis of 24 pediatric RCTs showing increased suicidal ideation/behavior (RR ~1.95), though no completed suicides occurred.[22] Unintended consequences included reduced mental health care utilization and subsequent increases in suicide deaths.[22]

Key Negative/Practice-Changing Trials


CO-MED (2011): Neither bupropion-escitalopram nor venlafaxine-mirtazapine combination outperformed escitalopram monotherapy as first-step treatment (remission ~38% across all arms).[23] VAST-D (2017): Aripiprazole augmentation (28.9% remission) was modestly superior to switching (22.3%) but not to bupropion augmentation (26.9%).[23] TADS (2004): Combination fluoxetine + CBT was superior to either alone in adolescents; CBT alone did not differ from placebo at 12 weeks.[24]

III. MOOD STABILIZERS


Lithium


The BALANCE trial (2010) is the pivotal maintenance study: 330 bipolar I patients randomized to lithium monotherapy, lithium + valproate, or valproate monotherapy for 24 months. Lithium monotherapy was significantly superior to valproate monotherapy (HR 0.71), and combination was superior to valproate alone (HR 0.59, NNT = 7).[25] Lithium's prophylactic evidence uniquely comes from non-enriched-design RCTs.[26]

Lithium and suicide prevention is supported by the Cipriani et al. 2013 meta-analysis (48 RCTs, 6,674 participants): lithium reduced completed suicides (OR 0.13) and all-cause mortality (OR 0.38) vs. placebo.[27] Within-individual Swedish data (51,535 patients) confirmed a 14% reduction in suicidal events during lithium treatment but not during valproate treatment.[28]

Lithium monitoring: The McKnight et al. 2012 systematic review established the toxicity profile — reduced urinary concentrating ability (~15%), hypothyroidism (6-fold increase), hyperparathyroidism (~10% of users).[29] Therapeutic range: 0.8–1.2 mEq/L acute, 0.8–1.0 mEq/L maintenance; toxicity ≥1.5 mEq/L.[30]

Lamotrigine


The Bowden (2003) and Calabrese (2003) 18-month trials established lamotrigine's polarity-specific profile: superior to placebo for depressive relapse prevention, while lithium was superior for manic relapse prevention.[31][32] The pooled analysis confirmed complementary polarity profiles (median survival: lamotrigine 197 days, lithium 184 days, placebo 86 days).[33]

Valproate


The Bowden et al. 2000 maintenance trial is a key negative trial: the primary ITT analysis failed to separate divalproex from placebo, though enrichment analysis showed benefit.[33] Valproate lacks FDA approval for maintenance despite widespread off-label use.[34] Teratogenicity is a critical concern: MCM risk ~9–10%, dose-dependent, with neural tube defects in 1.5–5.0%; folate supplementation does not prevent valproate-associated NTDs.[34]

IV. CLOZAPINE


The Superiority Debate


The Kane et al. 1988 pivotal study (SMD −0.88 vs. chlorpromazine in TRS) led to FDA approval, but this effect size has never been replicated.[34] A critical tension exists between RCT-based and real-world evidence:

- RCT meta-analyses (Samara 2016, Dong 2024, Schneider-Thoma 2025 IPD-MA) find no clear superiority of clozapine over olanzapine/risperidone in blinded trials.[34][35]

- Real-world cohort data (Masuda 2019, Tiihonen 2017, FIN20) consistently show clozapine associated with lowest hospitalization, lowest all-cause mortality (aHR 0.39), and lowest suicide mortality (aHR 0.21).[35][36]

Explanations for this discrepancy include sampling bias (most severely treatment-resistant patients excluded from blinded RCTs), subtherapeutic clozapine dosing, and industry sponsorship bias.[37]

InterSePT and Anti-Suicidal Properties


The InterSePT trial (956 patients) demonstrated clozapine's significantly longer delay to recurrent suicidal behavior vs. olanzapine (HR 0.76), leading to the FDA's second approved indication.[38] Clozapine is the only antipsychotic consistently associated with decreased suicide risk across multiple nationwide register studies.[39][40]

REMS Monitoring and Underutilization


ANC monitoring follows a tiered schedule: weekly for months 1–6, biweekly for months 7–12, monthly thereafter.[40] A 2025 global Delphi consensus recommends discontinuing routine ANC monitoring after 2 years and lowering cessation thresholds, as 85–90% of agranulocytosis cases occur in the first year.[42] Despite ~30% of schizophrenia patients having TRS, fewer than 10% receive clozapine in most US states, with an 18% decline in population-adjusted use from 2015–2020.[43]

Augmentation of Partial Clozapine Response


Only ~40–50% of TRS patients respond adequately to clozapine monotherapy.[44] The TRRIP Working Group recommends ensuring plasma levels ≥350 ng/mL before augmentation.[45] Medium-dose aripiprazole (9–<16.5 mg/day) combined with high-dose clozapine was associated with a 32% decreased hospitalization risk in a 2025 meta-analysis of two nationwide cohorts.[46] ECT augmentation of clozapine achieved 50% response vs. 0% in the Petrides et al. 2015 RCT.[22]

V. LONG-ACTING INJECTABLE ANTIPSYCHOTICS


The RCT vs. Real-World Paradox


The Kishimoto et al. 2021 meta-analysis (137 studies, 397,319 patients) found LAIs reduced relapse/hospitalization across all study designs, but the magnitude differed dramatically: RCTs RR 0.88, cohort studies RR 0.92, pre-post studies RR 0.44.[47] This discrepancy reflects selection of adherent patients and frequent monitoring improving oral adherence in RCTs.[47]

Landmark Trials


PRELAPSE (2020): The first large-scale US cluster-RCT in early-phase schizophrenia (489 participants, mean age 25) showed AOM reduced hospitalization by 44% (HR 0.56, NNT = 7).[48] PRIDE (2015): PP1M delayed treatment failure vs. oral antipsychotics in patients with incarceration history (HR 1.43, median 416 vs. 226 days).[49] PROACTIVE (2015): A notable negative trial — no difference in relapse between LAI risperidone and oral SGAs, likely due to biweekly monitoring improving adherence in both groups.[50]

Real-World Effectiveness and Mortality


The Hamina et al. 2024 nationwide Finnish study found paliperidone 3-month LAI had the lowest relapse risk (AHR 0.66 vs. oral olanzapine), followed by aripiprazole LAI (0.77).[51] LAIs are associated with 33% lower mortality than equivalent orals (aHR 0.67), with the greatest benefit when initiated early (≤3 years of illness).[52][51]

VI. TREATMENT-RESISTANT DEPRESSION


Definition and Staging


TRD is defined as inadequate response to ≥2 antidepressants despite adequate trial duration and adherence, affecting at least 30% of persons with depression.[53] The Maudsley Staging Method is the best-validated multidimensional staging model, incorporating treatment failure, severity, and duration (scores 3–15).[54]

Augmentation Strategies


Lithium augmentation is supported by 10 placebo-controlled trials (NNT = 5, OR 3.11).[55] Atypical antipsychotic augmentation has the most robust evidence: aripiprazole (OR 1.85) and quetiapine XR (OR 1.92) are the best-supported agents in network meta-analyses.[56] Five atypical antipsychotics now carry FDA approval for adjunctive MDD: aripiprazole, brexpiprazole, cariprazine, lumateperone, and quetiapine XR.[57]

Esketamine/Ketamine


Intranasal esketamine (FDA-approved 2019) demonstrated a MADRS difference of −4.0 to −6.8 points vs. placebo in the TRANSFORM trials.[19] The SUSTAIN-1 relapse prevention trial showed NNT = 4.[19] In a head-to-head NEJM trial, esketamine was 1.54 times as likely as quetiapine XR to achieve remission at week 8.[58] A 2025 phase 4 RCT demonstrated esketamine monotherapy efficacy (effect sizes 0.48–0.63), with significant effects within 24 hours.[59]

IV ketamine meta-analysis (26 RCTs, 1,166 patients) showed SMD −1.74 at 4 hours and −0.89 at 1 week, with significant anti-suicidal effects (SMD −0.69 at 24 hours).[60]

ECT vs. Ketamine: ELEKT-D


The ELEKT-D trial (n=403) found ketamine was noninferior to ECT for nonpsychotic TRD (response 55.4% vs. 41.2%), with substantially less memory decline.[55] ECT remains recommended as superior for psychotic depression, older adults, and inpatient settings.[55]

TMS


The THREE-D trial (n=414) established that 3-minute iTBS was noninferior to 37.5-minute standard rTMS (response 49% vs. 47%, remission 32% vs. 27%).[62] The Stanford Neuromodulation Therapy (SNT) protocol — 50 iTBS sessions over 5 days — achieved 57% remission vs. 0% sham in the initial RCT, with a replication RCT confirming 50% vs. 21% remission.[62]

Psilocybin


The Goodwin et al. 2022 NEJM phase 2 trial (n=233) showed psilocybin 25 mg produced MADRS change of −12.0 vs. −5.4 for 1 mg control at 3 weeks, with 37% response and 29% remission.[63] However, the EPISODE trial (2026, n=144) had a negative primary endpoint (response 17.0% vs. 10.6%, p=0.19), though key secondary endpoints were significant.[64] Meta-analysis of 6 RCTs found response RR 2.34 and remission RR 3.38 at 2–3 weeks.[65]

VII. TREATMENT-RESISTANT BIPOLAR DISORDER


No consensus definition exists, though the 2025 ISBD Task Force operationalized treatment-resistant bipolar I depression as inadequate response to two adequate trials of approved first-line treatments.[66] For refractory mania, guidelines recommend combination mood stabilizer + antipsychotic, then clozapine or ECT.[35]

Clozapine in bipolar disorder has never been tested in a placebo-controlled RCT but shows 72–77% response in open trials of refractory mania.[67][36] A 2025 multinational register study found clozapine was more effective than other oral antipsychotics in reducing psychiatric hospitalizations in bipolar disorder.[68]

ECT in bipolar depression achieves 73.9–77.1% response rates, with the only RCT (Schoeyen et al. 2015) showing ECT significantly superior to algorithm-based pharmacotherapy (response 73.9% vs. 35.0%).[68][69] Pramipexole augmentation showed 60–67% response vs. 9–20% placebo in small RCTs.[36][70]

VIII. TREATMENT-RESISTANT SCHIZOPHRENIA


The TRRIP consensus defines TRS as persistent at least moderate symptoms after ≥2 courses of different antipsychotics at adequate dose (≥6 weeks), duration, and adherence.[71] All major international guidelines recommend clozapine as the treatment of choice.[71] The Siskind et al. 2016 meta-analysis found clozapine NNT = 9 vs. other antipsychotics.[71] Target trough plasma level is ≥350 ng/mL.[72]

For clozapine-resistant schizophrenia (~50–60% of TRS patients), ECT augmentation (50% response vs. 0% in the Petrides RCT) and medium-dose aripiprazole augmentation have the best evidence.[45][46][22]

IX. ECT


Core Efficacy Data


The UK ECT Review Group meta-analysis (2003) established ECT's superiority over sham (SES −0.91) and pharmacotherapy (SES −0.80).[73] Overall response rates are 60–80% and remission rates 50–60%.[74] ECT is particularly effective in psychotic depression (OR 1.47 for remission) and older adults.[75]

Electrode Placement and Pulse Width


The Sackeim et al. (1993, 2000) studies established that RUL ECT efficacy is dose-dependent: low-dose RUL was essentially ineffective, while high-dose (6× threshold) RUL achieved response rates equivalent to bilateral ECT with fewer cognitive effects.[76][74] Ultrabrief pulse (0.3 ms) RUL ECT at 6× threshold achieved 73% remission with markedly fewer cognitive effects in the Sackeim 2008 study.[77]

Relapse Prevention


The Sackeim et al. 2001 JAMA study demonstrated that 84% relapse on placebo after ECT, establishing the necessity of continuation therapy; nortriptyline + lithium reduced relapse to 39%.[78] The CORE study (Kellner 2006) showed continuation ECT was equivalent to nortriptyline + lithium (~46% sustained remission).[79] The PRIDE Phase 2 study showed continuation ECT + venlafaxine + lithium achieved >80% sustained response in geriatric patients.[80]

Cognitive Effects


Bilateral ECT causes more severe and persisting retrograde amnesia than RUL ECT.[81] Autobiographical memory loss may be permanent (SMD 0.82 for bilateral, 0.32 for RUL).[81] Critically, ECT is not associated with increased dementia risk — a 2026 meta-analysis found significantly lower dementia risk in adults >50 years (HR 0.78).[82]

ECT in Catatonia


ECT improves 80–100% of catatonia, including up to 60% of benzodiazepine-refractory cases.[83]

X. PHARMACOGENOMICS


The Evidence Gap


The GUIDED trial (n=1,167) missed its primary endpoint (symptom improvement p=0.107) but showed significant secondary endpoints (remission 15.3% vs. 10.1%, p=0.007).[77] The PRIME Care trial (n=1,944) met its prescribing behavior co-primary endpoint but showed only "small nonpersistent effects on symptom remission" (OR 1.28, p=0.02 as repeated measure; week 24 specifically not significant).[84]

Meta-analyses show modest but statistically significant improvements (remission RR ~1.4), though effect sizes have attenuated in more recent, better-designed trials.[37] The APA Council of Research (2024 update) concluded that evidence does not support use of currently available combinatorial PGx tools for treatment selection in MDD.[35] CPIC guidelines for CYP2D6/CYP2C19 provide transparent, evidence-based dosing guidance for specific drug-gene pairs and represent the strongest evidence in this domain.[85]

XI. DEPRESCRIBING AND POLYPHARMACY


Antipsychotic Deprescribing


The RADAR trial (n=253, recurrent psychosis) found antipsychotic reduction doubled severe relapse risk (25% vs. 13%, OR 2.20), though two-thirds of those who reduced ≥50% did not experience severe relapse.[86] The HAMLETT trial (n=347, first-episode psychosis, 4-year follow-up) showed a critical temporal pattern: DRD was associated with higher short-term relapse but significantly better long-term functioning at 3–4 years.[86] The Wunderink 7-year follow-up similarly showed higher functional remission (55.9% vs. 21.7%) and recovery (52.9% vs. 17.4%) with early dose reduction.[35]

Hyperbolic tapering — reducing by a fixed proportion of the current dose based on the hyperbolic dose-D2 occupancy relationship — has been proposed but not yet tested in RCTs.[35]

Antidepressant Discontinuation


The ANTLER trial (n=478) found 56% relapse with discontinuation vs. 39% with continuation (HR 2.06), though 44% who discontinued did not relapse.[88] A 2026 network meta-analysis found that slow tapering alone did NOT significantly outperform abrupt discontinuation; the addition of psychological support was critical (NNT 4.3 for continuation + psychological support).[89]

The withdrawal vs. relapse distinction remains a fundamental methodological challenge. A 2025 JAMA Psychiatry meta-analysis found antidepressant discontinuation was not associated with greater depressive symptoms in the first 2 weeks, suggesting discontinuation symptoms are unlikely to be mistaken for mood relapse in the short term.[90]

Polypharmacy


Approximately 1 in 4 antipsychotic users receive antipsychotic polypharmacy.[91] The ASCP 2026 Task Force achieved consensus on 44 of 50 Delphi statements, including 100% agreement that utility of continuing any psychotropic should be reassessed at minimum annually.[56]

# | Finding | Why It Changed Practice
1 | SGAs are not categorically superior to FGAs (CATIE, CUtLASS) | Ended the assumption that SGAs should always be preferred; shifted focus to individual drug selection based on side-effect profiles
2 | Antipsychotic efficacy is not equal — a hierarchy exists (Leucht 2013, Huhn 2019 NMAs) | Challenged the "all antipsychotics are interchangeable" dogma; established clozapine, amisulpride, olanzapine, risperidone as top-tier
3 | Clozapine reduces suicide risk (InterSePT, FIN20, Taipale 2021) | Led to FDA's second indication; established clozapine as uniquely anti-suicidal among antipsychotics
4 | Clozapine has the lowest all-cause mortality (FIN20: aHR 0.39) | Reframed clozapine from "dangerous drug requiring monitoring" to "life-saving drug being underutilized"
5 | LAIs are superior to oral antipsychotics in real-world settings (Tiihonen 2017, Hamina 2024, PRELAPSE) | Shifted LAIs from "last resort for nonadherent patients" to consideration as early/first-line treatment
6 | STAR*D cumulative remission is ~35%, not 67% (Pigott 2023 reanalysis) | Recalibrated expectations for sequential antidepressant treatment; reinforced the magnitude of TRD
7 | All antidepressants beat placebo but differences between them are small (Cipriani 2018) | Confirmed antidepressant efficacy while showing selection should be guided by tolerability, not efficacy ranking
8 | Publication bias inflates antidepressant effect sizes by 32% (Turner 2008) | Transformed how clinicians and researchers interpret published trial data
9 | Lithium is superior to valproate for bipolar maintenance (BALANCE) | Challenged the trend of replacing lithium with valproate; reinforced lithium as first-line
10 | Lithium reduces suicide by ~87% (Cipriani 2013: OR 0.13) | Established lithium's unique anti-suicide property as a distinct clinical indication
11 | Lamotrigine prevents depressive but not manic relapses; lithium the reverse (Bowden/Calabrese 2003) | Enabled polarity-specific maintenance treatment selection
12 | Ketamine is noninferior to ECT for nonpsychotic TRD (ELEKT-D) | Provided the first rigorous head-to-head comparison; expanded treatment options for TRD
13 | Esketamine is superior to quetiapine for TRD (Reif 2023) | First active-comparator superiority trial for esketamine; positioned it as preferred augmentation
14 | 3-minute iTBS is noninferior to 37.5-minute rTMS (THREE-D) | Dramatically reduced treatment time, improving TMS accessibility and throughput
15 | Antipsychotic dose reduction improves long-term functioning in FEP (Wunderink 7-year, HAMLETT 4-year) | Challenged indefinite maintenance at full dose; opened discussion of guided dose reduction
16 | Slow tapering alone does not prevent relapse — psychological support is critical (2026 NMA) | Shifted deprescribing paradigm from purely pharmacological to integrated psychosocial approach
17 | Antipsychotics increase mortality 1.6–1.7× in elderly with dementia (Schneider 2005) | Led to FDA black box warning; fundamentally changed prescribing in dementia
18 | Combination antidepressants are not superior to monotherapy as first-step (CO-MED) | Prevented premature escalation to combination therapy; reinforced sequential approach
19 | Pharmacogenomic testing changes prescribing but has limited clinical outcome impact (GUIDED, PRIME Care) | Tempered enthusiasm for routine PGx testing; highlighted gap between analytical and clinical validity
20 | Hyperbolic tapering based on receptor pharmacology (Horowitz 2019/2021) | Provided a pharmacological rationale for gradual dose reduction; influenced emerging guidelines
