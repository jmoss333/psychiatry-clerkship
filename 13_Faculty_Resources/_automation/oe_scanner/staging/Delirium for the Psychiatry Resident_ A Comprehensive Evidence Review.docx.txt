
Delirium for the Psychiatry Resident: A Comprehensive Evidence Review


---

1. Scope and Framing


This review synthesizes the current evidence on delirium prevention, diagnosis, and management across hospital settings, organized for psychiatry residents who are frequently consulted for delirium on medical, surgical, and ICU services. It covers screening instruments, nonpharmacologic prevention bundles, pharmacologic treatment evidence, key differential diagnoses (catatonia, dementia, substance withdrawal), and setting-specific considerations (ICU vs. non-ICU). The review draws on NICE CG103, SCCM PADIS 2018/2025, Cochrane systematic reviews, and landmark RCTs through mid-2026. It concludes with a practical algorithm, medication table, nonpharmacologic bundle checklist, and curated reference list.

---

2. Delirium Screening Tools


Only 12–35% of delirium cases are recognized through routine clinical documentation without structured screening.[1] Tool selection should be guided by clinical setting, assessor training, and whether the goal is screening (high sensitivity) or confirmation (high specificity).[2]

Confusion Assessment Method (CAM)


The CAM remains the most widely used delirium instrument worldwide, used in >4,500 published studies.[3] It assesses four core features: acute onset/fluctuating course, inattention, disorganized thinking, and altered level of consciousness. With trained raters performing formal cognitive testing, sensitivity is 94–100% and specificity 90–95% (κ = 0.92).[3][4] However, sensitivity drops to ~40% when scored from routine clinical observations without structured cognitive testing.[5] This training dependency is the CAM's principal limitation.

CAM-ICU


Adapted for patients who cannot communicate verbally, the CAM-ICU is completable in ~2 minutes.[6] A 2023 Cochrane review (25 studies, 2,817 participants) found pooled sensitivity of 0.78 (95% CI 0.72–0.83) and specificity of 0.95 (95% CI 0.92–0.97).[7] The SCCM PADIS guidelines recommend CAM-ICU or ICDSC for routine ICU delirium screening.[2] Real-world nurse-documented assessments show only moderate agreement (κ = 0.42) with trained researcher assessments, underscoring the importance of training.[8]

The summary ROC plot from the Cochrane review illustrates the distribution of diagnostic accuracy across CAM-ICU studies, with the pooled estimate in the upper-left quadrant indicating generally high specificity and moderate-to-high sensitivity.

Figure 6 Summary ROC plot of CAM‐ICU for diagnosis of delirium.

4AT (4 'A's Test)


The 4AT assesses alertness, AMT-4 (orientation), attention (months backward), and acute change. Its key advantage is that no special training is required, and it is completable in <2 minutes.[5] A meta-analysis of 17 studies (3,702 observations) found pooled sensitivity of 0.88 and specificity of 0.88.[10] In a head-to-head comparison with the CAM in 785 acute medical patients ≥70 years, the 4AT had sensitivity of 76% vs. CAM sensitivity of only 40% when the CAM was used without formal cognitive testing.[5] NICE and multiple national guidelines now recommend the 4AT for routine screening.[5][10]

3D-CAM (3-Minute Diagnostic Interview)


The 3D-CAM operationalizes the CAM algorithm in a standardized 20-item assessment (median 3 minutes). A meta-analysis of 7 studies (1,350 participants) found pooled sensitivity of 0.92 and specificity of 0.95.[11] It detected 100% of mild delirium cases vs. 30% for the CAM-ICU in general medicine inpatients.[12] This tool offers the best combined sensitivity and specificity among brief instruments.

DRS-R-98 (Delirium Rating Scale–Revised-98)


The 16-item DRS-R-98 is best suited for longitudinal severity tracking and phenomenological research rather than rapid screening, requiring 20–30 minutes and psychiatric-trained clinicians.[3][13] Sensitivity ranges from 89–100% and specificity from 85–100% depending on cutoff.[9] It is the instrument of choice for C-L psychiatry when monitoring delirium severity over time.

3. Nonpharmacologic Prevention Bundles


Multicomponent nonpharmacologic interventions are the most consistently effective strategy for delirium prevention and are endorsed by all major guidelines.[3][9] At least 30–40% of delirium cases are considered preventable.[1]

Hospital Elder Life Program (HELP)


HELP is the foundational evidence-based model, targeting six delirium risk factors using an interdisciplinary team and trained volunteers.[1] A 2018 meta-analysis (44 articles, 14 in meta-analysis) demonstrated a 53% reduction in delirium incidence (OR 0.47, 95% CI 0.37–0.59), 42% reduction in falls, and cost savings of $1,600–$3,800 per patient.[9] The tailored, family-involved HELP (t-HELP) RCT (Wang et al., 2020; n = 281 surgical patients) showed postoperative delirium in 2.6% vs. 19.4% (RR 0.14, 95% CI 0.05–0.38; NNT = 5.9), with shorter length of stay (12.2 vs. 16.4 days).[9]

The cumulative incidence of delirium from the modified HELP cluster RCT (Chen et al., 2017) demonstrates significantly lower delirium rates in the intervention group, with divergence beginning in the early postoperative days.

ABCDEF Bundle (ICU Setting)


The ABCDEF bundle (Assess/prevent/manage pain; Both spontaneous awakening and breathing trials; Choice of analgesia and sedation; Delirium: assess/prevent/manage; Early mobility and exercise; Family engagement) operationalizes the SCCM PADIS guidelines.[2] The ICU Liberation Collaborative (Pun et al., 2019; 15,226 adults, 68 ICUs) showed complete bundle performance was associated with lower odds of delirium (AOR 0.60, 95% CI 0.49–0.72) and hospital death within 7 days (AHR 0.32, 95% CI 0.17–0.62), with a consistent dose-response relationship.[15] However, a recent pragmatic RCT (Sosnowski et al., 2026) found no significant difference in delirium incidence (37.9% vs. 36.4%) with only 50% bundle adherence, highlighting that the positive ABCDEF evidence is predominantly observational.[16]

Which Components Drive the Effect?


The 2021 Cochrane component network meta-analysis (Burton et al.; 14 multicomponent RCTs, 3,693 participants) identified three components with statistically significant individual effects:[9]

- Sleep hygiene: OR 0.25 (95% CrI 0.08–0.71)

- Reorientation & familiar objects: OR 0.32 (95% CrI 0.11–0.89)

- Cognitive stimulation: OR 0.45 (95% CrI 0.21–0.93)

Nutrition/hydration (OR 0.48), oxygenation (OR 0.35), and bowel/bladder care (OR 0.55) showed probable reductions but credible intervals included 1. Mobilization, sensory aids, and pain control had imprecise estimates as isolated interventions.[9] The evidence supports bundled delivery of ≥3 components rather than single-component interventions.

Sleep Promotion


The SCCM PADIS guidelines conditionally recommend sleep-promoting protocols in the ICU (earplugs, eye masks, noise/light reduction, clustering care), with pooled analysis showing reduced delirium prevalence (RR 0.62, 95% CI 0.42–0.91; very low quality).[2] A surgical ICU quality improvement study (Tonna et al., 2021; n = 646) implementing a multicomponent sleep-wake bundle found a significant reduction in proportion of ICU days with delirium (15% vs. 20%, adjusted decrease 4.9%).[18]

Early Mobility


A 2025 meta-analysis (59 RCTs, 8,462 ICU patients) found enhanced mobilization may reduce delirium duration (MD −1.34 days, 95% CI −1.85 to −0.83; low certainty) and ICU-acquired weakness (RR 0.79, 95% CI 0.66–0.95; moderate certainty).[19] A network meta-analysis found that multicomponent interventions involving early mobilization and family participation were the most effective combination (OR 0.12, 95% CI 0.02–0.83).[20]

4. Antipsychotics for Delirium: Evidence and Limitations


The Bottom Line


No antipsychotic has demonstrated clear efficacy for reducing delirium duration or improving delirium-free days in adequately powered, placebo-controlled RCTs. All major guidelines recommend against routine use. However, short-term use may be appropriate for severe agitation, hallucinations, or distress posing safety risks.[2][21]

Major ICU Treatment Trials


MIND-USA (2018): 566 ICU patients with delirium randomized to IV haloperidol (max 20 mg/day), IV ziprasidone (max 40 mg/day), or placebo across 16 centers. No significant difference in days alive without delirium or coma (median 8.5, 7.9, and 8.7 days; P = 0.26).[21] Long-term follow-up showed no benefit on cognitive, functional, or quality-of-life outcomes at 3 or 12 months.[22]

AID-ICU (2022): 1,000 ICU patients randomized to IV haloperidol (2.5 mg TID + 2.5 mg PRN, max 20 mg/day) or placebo. No significant difference in days alive and out of hospital at 90 days (35.8 vs. 32.9 days; P = 0.22).[23] However, 90-day mortality was numerically lower with haloperidol (36.3% vs. 43.3%), and a pre-planned Bayesian analysis found 99% probability of any mortality benefit and 94% probability of clinically important mortality benefit.[24]

EuRIDICE (2023): 132 ICU patients; stopped early for futility. Haloperidol did not increase delirium- and coma-free days, but haloperidol-treated patients were significantly less likely to receive rescue benzodiazepines (adjusted OR 0.41; P = 0.02).[25]

REDUCE (2018): The definitive prevention trial — 1,789 high-risk ICU patients randomized to prophylactic haloperidol 1 mg TID, 2 mg TID, or placebo. No difference in 28-day survival, delirium incidence (33.3% vs. 33.0%), or any secondary outcome.[26]

The REDUCE trial survival curves demonstrate overlapping Kaplan-Meier curves for haloperidol and placebo at both 28 and 90 days.

Figure 2 Survival Analysis at 28 and 90 Days

Palliative Care: Signal of Harm


The Agar et al. (2017) trial (247 palliative care patients) is a critical counterpoint: both risperidone and haloperidol arms had significantly worse delirium symptom scores than placebo, and haloperidol was associated with worse overall survival (HR 1.73; 95% CI 1.20–2.50).[3] This trial demonstrates that in palliative care, antipsychotics may cause harm.

Figure 2 Secondary Multivariable Mixed-Model Analysis of Delirium

Haloperidol vs. Atypical Antipsychotics


A 2025 meta-analysis (11 RCTs, 1,450 patients) found no significant difference in delirium severity (SMD −0.03) or mortality, but haloperidol had 2.7-fold higher odds of extrapyramidal symptoms (OR 2.72; 95% CI 1.26–5.87).[29]

QTc Safety


A secondary analysis of MIND-USA found that neither haloperidol nor ziprasidone caused clinically relevant QTc changes compared to placebo in patients with baseline QTc <550 ms.[30] A systematic review of IV haloperidol recommends ECG monitoring only when using doses >5 mg and telemetry only for high-risk patients receiving cumulative doses ≥100 mg or with QTc >500 ms.[31]

Inappropriate Continuation


Approximately one-third of older adults who start antipsychotics during hospitalization continue them after discharge. A 2025 trial emulation study found that antipsychotic discontinuation after hospitalization was associated with lower risks of rehospitalization and all-cause mortality compared with continuation.[32]

Dexmedetomidine


Dexmedetomidine is the only pharmacologic agent with consistent evidence for delirium reduction. A meta-analysis of 77 RCTs (n = 11,997) found dexmedetomidine reduced delirium risk compared to other sedatives (RR 0.67, 95% CI 0.55–0.81; moderate certainty), but increased bradycardia (RR 2.39) and hypotension (RR 1.32).[33] The 2025 PADIS update conditionally recommends dexmedetomidine over propofol for ICU sedation.[34] The SCCM PADIS guidelines also conditionally recommend dexmedetomidine for delirium in mechanically ventilated adults where agitation precludes weaning/extubation.[2]

Melatonin and Ramelteon


A 2026 dose-response meta-analysis (24 studies, 3,680 participants) found melatoninergic agonists reduce delirium incidence (RR 0.77, 95% CI 0.62–0.94), with superior effects in surgical ICU settings and an optimal cumulative dose of ~22 mg of melatonin.[3] A separate meta-analysis of sleep-wake regulating agents (ramelteon, suvorexant, lemborexant) found 40–46% relative risk reductions in delirium prevalence, though based on low-certainty evidence.[35] The 2025 PADIS update conditionally recommends melatonin for sleep disruption in the ICU.[34]

5. ICU vs. Non-ICU Delirium


Epidemiology


Delirium prevalence is substantially higher in the ICU (31–81%, higher with mechanical ventilation) compared to general wards (11–35%).[37][38] A 2025 meta-analysis (213 studies, 183,285 patients) found pooled ICU delirium prevalence of 35.7% and incidence of 28.8%.[38] Hypoactive delirium is the most common subtype across all settings, representing ~75% of cases.[1]

Key Differences


A direct comparison study (Canet et al., 2019; 100 ward vs. 100 ICU patients) revealed that ward delirium patients were older (median 84 vs. 65 years), more likely to have pre-existing dementia (38% vs. 2%), and more likely to have persistent delirium at discharge (66% vs. 17%).[39] ICU delirium patients were more likely to have undergone surgery (62% vs. 24%) and to recover.[39]



Outcomes


ICU delirium is associated with higher in-hospital mortality (RR 2.19, 95% CI 1.78–2.70) and long-term cognitive decline equivalent to mild Alzheimer's-type dementia, with delirium duration being the most potent risk factor.[40][41] In non-ICU settings, delirium independently predicts death (OR 2.0), institutionalization (OR 2.4), and incident dementia (OR 12.5).[1] A large cohort study (55,211 matched pairs) found delirium is an independent risk factor for incident dementia (subdistribution HR 3.00, 95% CI 2.91–3.10), with cumulative dementia incidence of 31% by 5 years after a delirium episode.[42]

6. Alcohol and Benzodiazepine Withdrawal Mimics


Alcohol Withdrawal Delirium (Delirium Tremens)


Delirium tremens typically begins 48–72 hours after the last drink and lasts 1–8 days (usually 2–3 days).[44][45] It occurs in 3–5% of patients hospitalized for alcohol withdrawal, with 1–4% mortality.[44] Key features include severe confusion, predominantly visual hallucinations, agitation, and prominent autonomic hyperactivity (tachycardia, hypertension, diaphoresis, fever) that distinguishes it from other delirium etiologies.[44][45] Predictors include CIWA-Ar scores >15 (especially with SBP >150 or pulse >100), recent withdrawal seizures, and prior withdrawal delirium.[44] The ASAM guideline emphasizes that a detailed neurological and medical examination should be conducted to rule out other common causes of delirium regardless of the apparent etiology.[46] Benzodiazepines are first-line treatment; symptom-triggered dosing reduces total benzodiazepine dose and duration.[45]

Benzodiazepine Withdrawal Delirium


Benzodiazepine withdrawal can produce a life-threatening delirium clinically similar to alcohol withdrawal, given the shared GABAergic mechanism.[9] A systematic review of 24 published cases found onset 12 hours to 13 days after dose changes, with disorientation, cognitive dysfunction, agitation, hallucinations, and persecutory delusions, typically hyperactive in nature.[47] Critically, delirium most often occurred due to tapering or abrupt discontinuation by inpatient healthcare teams, highlighting the importance of medication reconciliation on admission.[47] Benzodiazepine reinitiation led to complete symptom resolution in 22 of 24 cases.[47] Severe withdrawal can occur with as little as 15 mg diazepam equivalent daily for several months.[9]

Key Differentiating Features


Both alcohol and benzodiazepine withdrawal delirium feature prominent autonomic hyperactivity that is typically more pronounced than in other forms of delirium. The temporal relationship to substance cessation/reduction and the presence of autonomic signs are the primary distinguishing features. Clinicians should not rule out co-occurring disease or simultaneous withdrawal from other substances.[46]

7. Catatonia vs. Delirium


The Overlap Problem


Delirium and catatonia frequently co-occur despite the DSM-5-TR precluding a catatonia diagnosis in the presence of delirium. In a prospective study of 136 critically ill patients, 43% had delirium alone, 3% had catatonia alone, and 31% met criteria for both.[48] Among 120 medical inpatients with a prior delirium diagnosis, 26% were positive for both conditions.[49] The ICD-11, unlike DSM-5-TR, allows catatonia to overlap with and coincide with delirium.[47] The APA Resource Document on Catatonia (2025) explicitly states that "catatonia can overlap with and coincide with delirium".[47]

Key Distinguishing Features




Diagnostic Approach


The Bush-Francis Catatonia Rating Scale (BFCRS) is the most widely used instrument; a score ≥2 on the 14 screening items is considered positive.[47][50] In critically ill patients, a higher threshold of ≥4 BFCRS screening items was both 91% sensitive and 91% specific for DSM-5 catatonia.[48] The lorazepam challenge test (2 mg IV; ≥50% reduction on BFCRS = positive) is the typical confirmatory step, with overall response rate of 77% in a systematic review.[50] However, lorazepam response differs markedly between pure catatonia (60% positive) and catatonia-delirium overlap (11% positive), complicating both diagnosis and treatment.[49] An EEG revealing diffuse slowing makes concurrent delirium with catatonia more likely.[51]

8. Dementia vs. Delirium


Key Distinctions




The cornerstone of differentiation is determining the patient's baseline mental status and the acuity of any changes.[3] If uncertain, mental status changes should be treated as delirium until proven otherwise.[52]

Delirium Superimposed on Dementia (DSD)


DSD is extremely common: pooled prevalence is 48.9% among hospitalized older adults with dementia.[53] Dementia is the leading risk factor for delirium, consistently increasing risk by 2–5 times; two-thirds of delirium cases occur in patients with dementia.[52][54] Conversely, delirium is an independent risk factor for incident dementia (subdistribution HR 3.00).[42] Patients with DSD have longer hospitalization, worse cognitive and functional outcomes, and higher mortality compared to patients with dementia alone.[53] Inattention, altered levels of arousal, and motor dysfunction may help distinguish DSD from dementia alone.[52]

9. Resident Delirium Algorithm


Step 1: Screen


- All settings: Use 4AT (no training required, <2 min) for initial screening

- ICU: Use CAM-ICU or ICDSC per SCCM PADIS guidelines

- If positive: Confirm with 3D-CAM or full CAM with cognitive testing

- Track severity: DRS-R-98 for longitudinal monitoring

Step 2: Identify and Treat Underlying Causes (DELIRIUM mnemonic)


- Drugs (anticholinergics, benzodiazepines, opioids, steroids — review and reconcile)

- Electrolytes (Na⁺, Ca²⁺, glucose, renal/hepatic function)

- Lack of sleep (implement sleep hygiene protocol)

- Infection (UA, CXR, blood cultures, COVID/flu)

- Retention (urinary retention, constipation — remove catheters)

- Immobility (mobilize early, remove restraints)

- Underhydration/Undernutrition (IV fluids, oral intake, nutrition consult)

- Metabolic (thyroid, B₁₂, ammonia, hypoxia)

Step 3: Rule Out Mimics


- Alcohol/BZD withdrawal: Temporal relationship to cessation? Autonomic hyperactivity? → CIWA-Ar, benzodiazepine treatment

- Catatonia: Motor signs (posturing, waxy flexibility, mutism)? → BFCRS screen (≥2 positive); if positive → lorazepam challenge (2 mg IV); EEG if diagnostic uncertainty

- Dementia: Acute change from baseline? Inattention? Fluctuating consciousness? → If uncertain, treat as delirium until proven otherwise

- Seizures/NCSE: Consider EEG if unexplained altered mental status

Step 4: Implement Nonpharmacologic Bundle (ALL patients)


- See Nonpharmacologic Bundle Checklist below

Step 5: Pharmacologic Management (ONLY if needed)


- Indication: Severe agitation, hallucinations, or distress posing safety risk AND refractory to nonpharmacologic measures

- First-line: Haloperidol 0.5–1 mg PO/IM/IV q4–6h PRN (max 5 mg/day in elderly)

- Alternative: Quetiapine 12.5–25 mg PO q12h (preferred if EPS risk, Parkinson's, Lewy body)

- ICU with agitation precluding extubation: Dexmedetomidine infusion (0.2–0.7 mcg/kg/hr)

- Alcohol/BZD withdrawal: Benzodiazepines (symptom-triggered dosing)

- Catatonia: Lorazepam 1–2 mg IV/IM q4–8h; escalate to ECT if refractory

- Palliative care: Avoid antipsychotics (evidence of harm); prioritize comfort measures

- Always: Plan for discontinuation; do not continue antipsychotics at discharge without clear indication

Step 6: Monitor and Reassess


- Screen q8–12h with 4AT or CAM-ICU

- Track severity with DRS-R-98 if persistent

- Monitor QTc if using antipsychotics (ECG at baseline; repeat if dose >5 mg haloperidol or QTc >500 ms)

- Reassess for new precipitants daily

- Red flags for escalation: new focal neurologic signs, fever >38.5°C, seizures, NMS features (rigidity + fever + autonomic instability)

11. Nonpharmacologic Bundle Checklist


Based on NICE CG103, HELP, and ABCDEF bundle evidence:[2][9]

✅ Reorientation & Cognitive Stimulation (OR 0.32 and 0.45 respectively)[9]

- Introduce yourself and explain the situation at each encounter

- Provide clock, calendar, and familiar objects from home

- Engage in cognitively stimulating activities (conversation, reminiscence, word games)

- Minimize room/bed changes; maintain consistent care team

✅ Sleep Hygiene (OR 0.25)[9]

- Reduce nighttime noise and light (earplugs, eye masks)

- Cluster care activities to avoid nighttime interruptions

- Avoid nighttime medications when possible

- Promote daytime wakefulness (open blinds, encourage activity)

- Consider melatonin 3–5 mg PO qHS

✅ Early Mobility

- Mobilize within 24 hours of admission (or as soon as medically safe)

- Physical therapy consult for high-risk patients

- Minimize physical restraints and tethers (catheters, lines)

- Encourage sitting in chair for meals

✅ Sensory Optimization

- Ensure glasses and hearing aids are available and in use

- Adequate room lighting during daytime

- Minimize unnecessary noise

✅ Hydration, Nutrition & Bowel Care

- Monitor fluid intake; encourage oral fluids

- Assess for dehydration daily (mucous membranes, urine output)

- Nutritional assessment; assisted feeding if needed; ensure dentures

- Daily bowel assessment; prevent and treat constipation

✅ Pain Management

- Assess pain regularly (verbal or behavioral scale)

- Scheduled non-opioid analgesia (acetaminophen) when appropriate

- Minimize opioids; avoid meperidine

✅ Medication Review

- Review and reconcile all medications on admission

- Minimize anticholinergics, benzodiazepines, and sedative-hypnotics

- Identify and address benzodiazepine/alcohol dependence (do not abruptly discontinue)

✅ Infection & Hypoxia Surveillance

- Monitor for signs of infection; remove unnecessary catheters

- Monitor oxygen saturation; treat hypoxia

✅ Family Engagement

- Encourage family presence and participation in care

- Educate family about delirium (what it is, how to help)

- Involve family in reorientation and cognitive stimulation

14. Key Takeaways for the Psychiatry Resident


1. Screen systematically: Use the 4AT for rapid screening (no training needed) and the 3D-CAM or full CAM for confirmation. In the ICU, use CAM-ICU.

2. Prevention works; drugs don't: Multicomponent nonpharmacologic bundles reduce delirium by ~50%. No pharmacologic agent reliably prevents or treats delirium.

3. Always look for the cause: Delirium is a symptom, not a diagnosis. The consult should focus on identifying and treating precipitants (infection, medications, metabolic derangements, pain, constipation, urinary retention).

4. Think about withdrawal: Always reconcile home medications on admission. Abrupt benzodiazepine discontinuation by inpatient teams is a common and preventable cause of delirium.

5. Think about catatonia: Screen with BFCRS in any patient with motor abnormalities, mutism, or staring. Catatonia co-occurs with delirium in ~30% of critically ill patients. A lorazepam challenge can be both diagnostic and therapeutic — but response rates are lower in catatonia-delirium overlap.

6. Antipsychotics are for safety, not for cure: Use only for severe agitation or distress posing safety risks, at the lowest effective dose, for the shortest duration. Always plan for discontinuation. Never continue at discharge without a clear, documented indication.

7. Dementia is the #1 risk factor: Two-thirds of delirium occurs in patients with dementia. If uncertain whether the presentation is delirium or dementia, treat as delirium until proven otherwise.

8. Delirium has lasting consequences: It independently predicts incident dementia (HR 3.0), institutionalization, and death. Communicate this to patients and families.
