
Integrating Structured Family Meetings into Inpatient Psychiatry Residency Training: Competency Milestones, Curriculum Design, and Assessment Tools


---

1. Scope and Framing


This report provides a practical implementation framework for integrating family meeting skills into inpatient psychiatry residency training. It maps family-related competencies to the ACGME Milestones 2.0 framework, synthesizes the evidence on teaching methods (simulation, direct observation, graduated responsibility), presents validated and adaptable assessment instruments, and proposes a phased curriculum with specific milestones for each training year. The goal is to bridge the well-documented gap between the strong evidence base for family interventions and their persistently low implementation in routine inpatient practice.

---

2. The Training Gap: Why This Matters


2.1 The Implementation Paradox Revisited


Family psychoeducation is among the most evidence-based interventions in psychiatry — with an NNT of 7 for relapse prevention and >100 supporting RCTs — yet implementation rates range from 0% to 53% across settings.[1][2] A critical driver of this gap is inadequate training. A survey of psychiatry trainees at nine New York City hospitals found that while almost all reported providing psychoeducation to first-episode psychosis patients and families, 81% for patients and 84% for families reported that content and delivery were not uniform, with 98% relying on unstructured conversation and only ~25% using handouts.[3] A national survey confirmed that >80% of psychiatry, psychology, and social work programs offered family therapy didactics, but far fewer required them.[4]

2.2 Training Intensity Matters


Brief training is insufficient to change practice. A dissemination study found that two days of staff training produced no change in adoption of family interventions, whereas intensive training over several months resulted in implementation at the majority of study sites.[5] Carter (1989) found that residents who received modest or minimal family therapy training during residency were significantly less likely to use marital and family therapy in post-residency practice, while those who received adequate training preferred it second only to individual therapy.[6]

3. ACGME Milestones 2.0: Mapping Family Meeting Skills


3.1 The Competency Architecture


The ACGME Milestones 2.0 framework organizes psychiatry training across six core competency domains, with 22 subcompetencies rated on a 5-level developmental scale.[7][8] Family therapy/family intervention skills are not a standalone milestone or subcompetency — rather, they are embedded across multiple domains.[9] The Milestones 2.0 introduced "harmonized" milestones across four domains (ICS, PBLI, PROF, SBP), creating standardized expectations across specialties.[10]

3.3 Entrustable Professional Activities (EPAs)


Young et al. (2018) developed 10 end-of-training EPAs for psychiatry through a rigorous three-stage process including a national Delphi study of 31 experts.[13] While no EPA is exclusively dedicated to family meetings, several encompass family-related skills:

- EPA: Provide psychoeducation — includes educating patients and families about diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis

- EPA: Develop and implement treatment plans — includes coordinating with families as part of comprehensive treatment planning

- EPA: Manage transitions of care — includes discharge planning with family involvement

A mobile app-based EPA assessment tool piloted with psychiatry residents demonstrated feasibility (mean completion time 76 seconds per assessment), high-quality feedback (95% of comments behaviorally specific and actionable), and moderate correlation between entrustment scores and resident experience (r = 0.43, P < .001).[14]

4. Evidence-Based Teaching Methods


4.1 Simulation and Standardized Patients


A systematic review and meta-analysis of 163 studies (27 RCTs) on simulation in psychiatry found significant improvements in attitudes (SMD = 0.52), skills (SMD = 1.37), knowledge (SMD = 1.22), and behaviors (SMD = 1.07) compared to active controls, with effects sustained at 3-month follow-up.[15] A 2025 scoping review of 26 studies on family meeting training across healthcare settings found that role-play or simulation was the most common teaching method, with medical students and residents as the primary trainees.[16]

Specifically for psychiatry residents, Amsalem et al. (2021) demonstrated that a 5-hour simulation workshop (high-fidelity simulation with professional actor + lecture + role-play of 3 scenarios) significantly improved communication scores (mean 24.9 → 27.8, p<0.001) and self-confidence (3.4 → 4.0 on 5-point Likert, sustained at 2 months).[17]

4.2 Just-in-Time Coaching


Rock (2021) described "just-in-time" simulation — training immediately before a real clinical encounter — as a powerful scaffolding technique. Key steps include: (1) assessing the learner's understanding, (2) identifying challenging aspects, (3) providing a script as a cognitive aid, (4) emotional preparation through mental rehearsal, (5) coaching on approach/pace/tone, and (6) providing specific feedback afterward.[18]

4.3 Direct Observation with Structured Feedback


A procedural approach to teaching ICU family conferences used a 5-component module: instructional video, faculty observation of conferences, standardized observation with formative evaluation, online procedure log, and conference note template. Of respondents, 92% rated the module good/very good/excellent, though only 41% completed even one observed conference due to competing demands.[19] Direct observation remains underutilized in psychiatry training; a Canadian study identified barriers at resident, faculty, and administrative levels, with enablers including financial compensation, guidelines, and rotation-start discussions.[20]

4.4 Video Review


Video review of clinical encounters enables indirect observation, more accurate focus on specific moments, and promotes self-reflection. Supervisors describe using video to foster consensus and shift perspectives, though emotional risks (trainee anxiety, defensiveness) must be managed through trust-building.[20][21]

4.5 Graduated Responsibility Model


Campbell et al. (2021) compiled 10 tips for teaching trainees to conduct family meetings, emphasizing that the "see one, do one, teach one" axiom should not apply. Instead, they recommend: (1) structured preparation before every meeting, (2) graduated responsibility (observe → co-lead → lead with observation → lead independently), (3) explicit feedback using behavioral descriptors, and (4) debriefing after every meeting.[22]

5.2 Recommended Assessment Strategy


A multi-method approach is most robust, combining:

1. Direct observation with the Family Meeting Behavioral Skills Checklist (minimum 2 observed meetings per rotation)

2. Simulation-based OSCE annually using the ROSCE format with standardized family scenarios

3. EPA-based entrustment ratings via mobile app after each observed family meeting

4. Self-reflection through structured post-meeting debriefing forms

5. Procedure log tracking number and type of family meetings conducted

6. Proposed Curriculum: A Four-Year Developmental Framework


6.1 Overview




6.2 Year-by-Year Curriculum Detail


PGY-1: Foundations


Didactics (4–6 hours)

- Lecture 1: The evidence base — EE, landmark trials, NNT=7, the 50% rule

- Lecture 2: Family systems basics — genograms, family structure, communication patterns

- Lecture 3: Confidentiality and consent — HIPAA framework, listening vs. disclosing, practical scenarios

Clinical Requirements

- Observe ≥5 faculty-led family meetings on inpatient rotations

- Complete structured observation form for each meeting (what went well, what was challenging, what techniques were used)

- Co-lead ≥2 family meetings with senior resident or attending supervision

- Complete a genogram for ≥3 patients

Assessment

- Self-reflection logs reviewed by supervisor

- Informal faculty feedback after co-led meetings

- Knowledge assessment: brief quiz on EE, HIPAA, family intervention evidence

PGY-2: Core Skills


Didactics (6–8 hours)

- Workshop 1 (3 hrs): Simulation workshop — 3 standardized family scenarios with professional actors (high-EE family, family requesting information when patient declines involvement, discharge planning meeting)

- Lecture 4: Psychoeducation models — what to teach families about schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, and medications

- Lecture 5: Communication frameworks — MI with families, de-escalation, managing conflict

- Lecture 6: Brief family interventions feasible during short admissions

Clinical Requirements

- Lead ≥6 family meetings on inpatient rotation with faculty observation (minimum 2 directly observed with checklist)

- Participate in ≥1 multifamily group session (if available)

- Deliver psychoeducation to ≥3 families using standardized handout

- Document family meetings using structured note template

Assessment

- Annual OSCE with family meeting scenario (ROSCE format)

- Direct observation using Family Meeting Behavioral Skills Checklist (≥2 per rotation)

- EPA entrustment ratings via mobile app

- Self-confidence survey (pre/post simulation workshop)

PGY-3: Advanced Application


Didactics (4–6 hours)

- Seminar 1: Complex family dynamics — substance use, personality disorders, cultural considerations, high-conflict families

- Seminar 2: Family-focused therapy for bipolar disorder — Miklowitz model overview

- Seminar 3: Caregiver burden assessment and intervention — ZBI, NAMI resources, caregiver-focused interventions

Clinical Requirements

- Lead ≥8 family meetings independently across inpatient and outpatient settings

- Deliver ≥2 structured psychoeducation sessions (individual or group format)

- Complete ≥1 video-recorded family meeting with faculty review using FCOF

- Assess caregiver burden using ZBI in ≥2 families

Assessment

- Video review with FCOF scoring

- Procedure log documenting meeting types, complexity, and outcomes

- Supervisor evaluation of independent family meeting competence

- Written case formulation integrating family systems perspective

PGY-4: Mastery and Teaching


Didactics (2–4 hours)

- Seminar: Teaching family skills — how to supervise junior residents in family meetings

- Journal club: Critical appraisal of family intervention literature

Clinical Requirements

- Supervise ≥2 PGY-2 residents conducting family meetings (with feedback documentation)

- Lead or co-lead ≥1 QI project related to family involvement (e.g., tracking family meeting rates, developing unit protocols, implementing standardized psychoeducation)

- Present a family intervention case at grand rounds or case conference

Assessment

- Teaching evaluation by supervised residents

- QI project assessment (design, implementation, outcomes)

- Portfolio review: compilation of procedure logs, self-reflections, supervisor evaluations, and QI project across all 4 years

- Final entrustment decision: Can this resident independently conduct family meetings across a range of clinical scenarios?

7.2 The "Champion" Model


Successful implementation requires identified champions at multiple levels: a faculty champion who models family meeting skills and provides supervision, a residency training director who ensures curricular integration, and an administrative champion who supports protected time and resources.[5][32] The Berman & Heru (2005) GAP proposal emphasizes that the goal should be family-centered care competency — achievable within existing residency constraints — rather than sophisticated family therapy training, which requires more intensive investment.[9]

7.3 Standardized Cognitive Aids


Providing residents with structured tools reduces variability and increases confidence:

- Family Meeting Agenda Template (as detailed in the previous review)

- Psychoeducation Handout (diagnosis-specific, as provided previously)

- Family Meeting Note Template (structured documentation)

- HIPAA Decision Tree Pocket Card (when can/cannot disclose)

- Early Warning Signs Worksheet (completed collaboratively with family at discharge)

8. Teaching Pearls for Educators


1. Frame family meetings as a procedure. Just as residents learn to perform lumbar punctures with structured observation and graduated responsibility, family meetings should be taught with the same procedural rigor — preparation, observation, practice, feedback, independence.[19][22]

2. The 5-hour investment. A single 5-hour simulation workshop significantly improves communication scores and self-confidence, with effects sustained at 2 months. This is a high-yield, low-cost intervention that every program can implement.[17]

3. Debrief every meeting. The learning happens in the debrief, not the meeting itself. Even 5 minutes of structured feedback after a family meeting ("What went well? What would you do differently? What was the hardest moment?") accelerates skill development.[22]

4. Teach the "listening is not disclosing" framework early. Confidentiality anxiety is the #1 reason residents avoid family contact. Teaching this distinction in PGY-1 orientation transforms the barrier into a bridge.[34][35]

5. Use the NNT=7 to motivate. When residents understand that family psychoeducation has an NNT comparable to statins, it reframes family meetings from "soft skill" to "evidence-based procedure."[1]

6. Model it. Faculty who routinely conduct family meetings and invite residents to observe create a culture where family involvement is normative. Faculty who avoid family contact implicitly teach that it is optional.[32]

7. Start with the genogram. Teaching residents to complete a three-generation genogram for every admitted patient creates a natural entry point for family-systems thinking and identifies who should be invited to meetings.[9]

8. Assess caregiver burden explicitly. Teaching residents to administer the Zarit Burden Interview (even the 12-item short form) to at least one caregiver per rotation builds awareness that caregivers are hidden patients.[37]

9. Track it. What gets measured gets done. Programs that track family meeting rates as a quality indicator see higher implementation than those that treat it as optional.[2]

10. Connect inpatient to outpatient. The inpatient family meeting is the initiation of a longer-term intervention, not a standalone treatment. Teaching residents to make warm handoffs to outpatient family programs (NAMI FTF, outpatient family therapy) closes the loop.[38]

---

9. Evidence Gaps and Future Directions


No psychiatry-specific RCTs directly compare different methods of teaching family meeting skills to psychiatry residents in inpatient settings — most evidence is extrapolated from ICU, palliative care, and neonatology contexts.[16] Long-term skill retention is poorly studied; most evaluations use pre-post designs without longitudinal follow-up.[16] No standardized national assessment tool exists for family therapy competency in psychiatry residency.[1] The distinction between "family-centered care" and "family therapy" remains important for curriculum design — the ACGME mandates the former for all residents, while the latter requires more intensive training.[9]

The most promising direction is the development of psychiatry-specific family meeting OSCEs with standardized scenarios (high-EE family, confidentiality dilemma, discharge planning, patient declining involvement) that could be shared across programs and validated nationally. The EPA framework, combined with mobile app–based assessment, offers a scalable approach to longitudinal competency tracking.[13][14]
