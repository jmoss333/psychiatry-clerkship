Comprehensive Evidence Review: Diagnosis and Management of Catatonia in Inpatient Psychiatry and Medically Ill Patients


1. Scope and Framing


This review synthesizes current evidence on the recognition, diagnosis, and management of catatonia across psychiatric and medical settings. It covers the Bush-Francis Catatonia Rating Scale (BFCRS), the lorazepam challenge test, benzodiazepine dosing, ECT indications and timing, malignant catatonia, the catatonia-delirium interface, antipsychotic risks, and etiology-specific considerations (mood disorders, psychosis, autism spectrum disorder, medical illness, and substance-related states). The evidence base is drawn from the 2025 APA Resource Document on Catatonia, the 2023 NEJM review, the 2024 Nature Reviews Disease Primers, the 2019 Lancet Psychiatry review, Cochrane systematic reviews, and recent meta-analyses. A critical limitation throughout is the near-complete absence of randomized controlled trials — virtually all treatment evidence derives from case series, retrospective cohorts, and expert consensus.[1][2][3]

---

2. Epidemiology and Pathophysiology


Catatonia occurs in 5–18% of psychiatric inpatients and 3.3% of general medical inpatients, though it remains widely underrecognized.[4] A meta-analysis of 74 studies found a mean prevalence of 9% in acute psychiatric settings, rising to 23.9% among patients referred for ECT.[2] In the ICU, prospective studies suggest up to one-third of critically ill adults may meet criteria.[5][6] The majority of catatonia cases occur in the context of mood disorders (bipolar and depressive), not schizophrenia, contrary to historical assumptions.[1][7]

The pathophysiology centers on disrupted excitatory-inhibitory balance within cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical (CSTC) circuits, specifically decreased GABAergic inhibitory tone relative to glutamatergic excitatory tone in the frontal cortex and basal ganglia.[8][6] This model explains the therapeutic efficacy of GABA-A agonists (benzodiazepines) and NMDA antagonists (amantadine, memantine).[8] Dopamine blockade — particularly via the indirect basal ganglia pathway — can induce or worsen catatonia, providing the mechanistic basis for antipsychotic-related risks.[8] Increasing evidence also supports an inflammatory/immune-mediated component, with anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis as the prototypical example.[9][8]

3. Diagnostic Assessment: The Bush-Francis Catatonia Rating Scale


Structure and Scoring


The BFCRS is a 23-item clinician-administered scale (each item scored 0–3), with the first 14 items constituting the Bush-Francis Catatonia Screening Instrument (BFCSI).[10] A score of ≥2 on the 14-item screen is considered positive and prompts completion of the full 23-item scale.[8] The BFCSI includes all 12 DSM-5-TR diagnostic criteria for catatonia and all ICD-11 criteria.[8]

Psychometric Properties


The original 1996 validation demonstrated inter-rater reliability of 0.93 for the full scale and 0.95 for the screening instrument, with mean item agreement of 88.2% and 92.7%, respectively.[10] However, item response theory analysis has revealed many redundant items, low reliability at low severity, and an underlying factor structure explaining only ~37% of variance.[11] A knowledge assessment study found clinicians correctly scored only ~69% of BFCRS items, with no improvement by training level, highlighting the need for standardized training.[12]

DSM-5 vs. BFCRS Diagnostic Sensitivity


The DSM-5-TR requires ≥3 of 12 specified signs for diagnosis.[7] However, DSM-5 may underdiagnose catatonia: in one study, DSM-5 identified only 73% of treatment-validated cases compared to 91% by DSM-IV criteria.[11] In a South African cohort, the BFCSI identified all 44 catatonia cases (18.3% prevalence) while DSM-5 identified only 16 (6.6%), missing ~64% — largely because DSM-5 excludes staring, the most common sign (present in 75%).[13]

ICU-Specific Threshold


In critically ill patients where delirium-catatonia overlap is common, Wilson et al. (2017) found that a higher cutoff of ≥4 BFCSI items achieved 91% sensitivity and 91% specificity for DSM-5 catatonia.[5]

Rapid Screening: The Catatonia Quick Screen


The Catatonia Quick Screen (CQS) is a 4-item sequential screening tool assessing excitement, mutism, abnormal staring, and abnormal posture, achieving 97% sensitivity in the training cohort and 91% in validation.[14] Any single positive finding triggers further evaluation with a full diagnostic scale.

Protocol

Response Rates


A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis (53 studies, 1336 screened) found an overall remission rate of 55% and response rate of 77%, with a mean BFCRS reduction of −11.88 points (Cohen's d = −3.15, a very large effect).[15] The NEJM review reports lorazepam is effective in up to 90% of acute catatonia cases.[1] A dose comparison study (2 mg vs. 4 mg) found no between-group differences, suggesting 2 mg is the optimal challenge dose.[16] In pediatric patients (n=54, median age 16), the mean BFCRS decreased from 16.6 to 9.5 (Hedges's g = 1.20, p < 0.001).[17] The Cochrane review identified only one small RCT and concluded that while observational data strongly support use, trial-derived evidence remains insufficient.[3]

Important Caveats


A negative lorazepam challenge does not rule out catatonia — approximately 24% of catatonic patients may not respond.[18] Benzodiazepine response may be diminished in chronic schizophrenia-associated catatonia.[19] In comorbid catatonia-delirium, response rates appear lower (11% in one study vs. 60% in catatonia alone), though other data suggest some benefit.[20][21]

---

5. Lorazepam Maintenance Dosing


Acute Phase


Following a positive challenge, continue IV lorazepam for at least 24–48 hours before converting to oral.[8] IV lorazepam may be more potent than oral; a dose increase may be needed when switching routes.[8] Lorazepam should be administered at minimum three times daily (every 4–8 hours) given its 14 ± 5 hour half-life.[8]

Tapering


No controlled studies exist on benzodiazepine tapering after treatment response.[8] The APA Resource Document recommends: inpatient taper of 10–25% per day (slower in elderly); outpatient taper up to 25% per week; acute catatonia taper over 1–2 weeks; chronic catatonia taper over months.[8] PRN benzodiazepine should be prescribed during taper for symptom recurrence.[8]

6. Electroconvulsive Therapy


Efficacy


ECT response rates in catatonia are consistently reported at 59–100%.[1][24] In benzodiazepine-refractory catatonia specifically, response rates are 76–92%.[6] A retrospective study found 95% of patients (19/20) responded, with a mean of 4.2 sessions to response.[25] Motor inhibition symptoms (stupor, mutism) responded most robustly.[25] A meta-analysis (564 patients, 28 studies) found significant improvement (SMD = −3.14) but cautioned that published studies are of low quality.[26]

Timing of Initiation


- Standard pathway: If no response to scheduled lorazepam by 7 days, pursue ECT or pharmacologic augmentation[8]

- Malignant catatonia: ECT should be started urgently, without waiting for benzodiazepine failure[8]

- Life-threatening features: ECT can be first-line when severe autonomic instability, withdrawal leading to insufficient oral intake, or inanition is present[2]

- Response declines with chronicity: earlier treatment is associated with better outcomes[1][2]

Parameters


Bitemporal (BT) placement is generally preferred, though a naturalistic case series found 90% response and 70% remission with right unilateral (RUL) placement, with stable or improved cognition.[8][27] In pediatric patients, 6/10 who started with RUL responded to RUL alone, while patients with malignant catatonia preferentially required bilateral placement.[28] Thrice-weekly is the standard schedule; daily ECT may be used for severe symptoms.[8] Effects can be expected after 4–6 sessions over 1–2 weeks, but ≥12 sessions may be needed for full response.[8][1] In ICU malignant catatonia, 6–23 treatments were required.[29]

Maintenance ECT


Maintenance ECT following acute response can prevent relapse.[8] Many patients benefit from concomitant maintenance benzodiazepine treatment.[8]

7. Malignant Catatonia


Definition and Recognition


Malignant catatonia is characterized by catatonic symptoms plus autonomic instability (labile blood pressure, tachycardia, tachypnea), hyperthermia, and often hyperactivity progressing to stuporous exhaustion.[6][30] It is a medical emergency requiring ICU-level care.[29][30]

Mortality and Outcomes


Untreated malignant catatonia may be fatal in 10–20% of cases, sometimes within days.[31][32] The largest systematic case series analysis (117 cases) found that combined benzodiazepine + ECT treatment yielded the most favorable outcomes, while purely supportive therapy yielded the least favorable.[33] Earlier application of benzodiazepines was significantly associated with favorable outcome.[33] Patients with severe malignant catatonia have a 78% higher risk of dying than those with moderate severity.[33]

Management


- ICU setting with continuous monitoring of vital signs, fluid balance, and temperature[29][30]

- Lorazepam as first-line, with urgent ECT if no response or if malignant features are present at onset[8][33]

- Supportive care: VTE prophylaxis, pressure ulcer prevention, aspiration precautions, daily stretching, adequate hydration/nutrition (tube feeding/IV fluids as needed)[1][2]

- Correct vital sign abnormalities as a crucial part of management[8]

- Stop all antipsychotics immediately if NMS is suspected[34]

8. NMS–Catatonia Overlap


Many experts consider NMS to be a drug-induced form of malignant catatonia precipitated by dopamine receptor blocking agents.[8][9] A systematic review of 490 case reports identified discriminating features: diaphoresis (OR 10.0), rigor (OR 9.6), and fever (OR 7.3) favor NMS, while negativism (OR 0.26), posturing (OR 0.24), waxy flexibility (OR 0.22), and stereotypy (OR 0.12) favor catatonia.[35] However, the overlap is substantial, and a systematic review of 555 antipsychotic-induced events concluded the clinical overlap "suggests a clinical continuum".[36]

NMS treatment per the 2024 NEJM review includes: immediate cessation of the offending agent, IV fluids (target urine output ~200–300 mL/hr for rhabdomyolysis), cooling measures, dantrolene for core temperature ≥38–40°C with moderate-to-severe rigidity, and bromocriptine or amantadine as dopamine agonists.[34] ECT is reserved for refractory cases.[34] Residual catatonia can persist for months after NMS resolution; bromocriptine improved residual catatonic symptoms in 80% and resolved them in 50% of cases.[37]

---

9. Catatonia vs. Delirium


Distinguishing Features


Both conditions share mutism, stupor, immobility, staring, and withdrawal.[1][8] However, delirium is characterized by fluctuating decreased alertness and cognition with inattention as the cardinal feature, while catatonia features psychomotor abnormalities as the essential element with preserved (though often inaccessible) consciousness.[1][8] Signs considered nearly pathognomonic for catatonia include waxy flexibility, catalepsy, echophenomena, negativism, posturing, and stereotypies — these are not typical of delirium.[1][8]

Co-occurrence

10. Catatonia Across Etiologies


Mood Disorders


The majority of catatonia cases involve individuals with depressive or bipolar disorders.[7] Up to 20% of patients with acute mania exhibit classic catatonic signs.[2] Catatonia and psychotic features should raise suspicion for bipolar depression over unipolar depression.[39][40] Benzodiazepine response rates are highest in affective catatonia (~90%).[1] Valproate is a useful adjunct in bipolar-associated catatonia.[1]

Schizophrenia


Catatonic symptoms in schizophrenia tend to be persistent, enduring, and bizarre (mannerisms, negativism, stereotypies), as opposed to episodic symptoms in mood disorders.[19] Benzodiazepines may be less effective in chronic schizophrenia-associated catatonia.[19] Clozapine (<150 mg/day) is the preferred antipsychotic for chronic catatonia in schizophrenia.[1][8] A cohort study (JAMA Psychiatry, 2025; n=303) found 48.8% relapsed over median 29.8 months, with antipsychotic prescription at discharge associated with a 41.1% reduction in relapse risk (HR 0.59, 95% CI 0.38–0.92).[41]

Autism Spectrum Disorder


A meta-analysis found a pooled prevalence of 10.4% (95% CI 5.8–18.0%) of individuals with ASD meeting criteria for catatonia.[42] Diagnostic challenges are substantial due to overlapping features (mutism, stereotypies, echophenomena).[43][7] There is a median diagnostic delay of 330 days in NDD patients without psychosis, compared to ~16 days in neurotypical patients.[44] Treatment requires higher lorazepam doses (6–24 mg/day; mean maximal dose 17.4 mg/day in one study) and longer durations.[22][23] Alternative benzodiazepines (clonazepam, diazepam) and midazolam infusions may help in partial lorazepam responders.[45] Notably, 4/5 patients in one series had worsening with escalating antipsychotic doses prior to catatonia recognition.[45]

Medical Illness


Two-thirds of catatonia attributed to non-psychiatric medical illness is due to primary neurologic conditions.[8] Anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis is the most well-established autoimmune cause, with catatonia present in 69% of confirmed cases in one prospective study.[46] NMDARE may have an incomplete response to lorazepam and can worsen with high-potency antipsychotics.[8] Treatment is primarily immunotherapy, with ECT as effective adjunctive symptomatic treatment.[47][48] Other medical causes include metabolic disorders, focal cerebral lesions, infections, and medications.[1]

Substance-Related States


Intoxication: Cannabis and cocaine are the most commonly cited substances causing catatonia.[1] Withdrawal: Benzodiazepine, alcohol, opioid, and clozapine withdrawal can all precipitate catatonia.[1][49] A review identified 55 cases of withdrawal catatonia, with the majority from benzodiazepine (24 cases) and clozapine (20 cases) discontinuation.[49] Clozapine withdrawal catatonia is a distinct entity; re-initiation of clozapine is the treatment of choice.[8]

11. Antipsychotic Risks in Catatonia


Three Categories of Risk


1. Worsening of catatonic symptoms: In the largest systematic review (175 trials in 110 patients), antipsychotic use was beneficial in 60%, neutral in 29.1%, and harmful in 10.9% of trials[19][50]

2. Precipitation of NMS: Pre-existing catatonia confers an NMS risk of approximately 3.6%, compared to 0.07–1.8% in the general antipsychotic-treated population[9][51]

3. Antipsychotic-induced catatonia: SGAs were implicated in 63.8% of events overall; FGAs and polypharmacy were risk factors for malignant progression[36]

Risk Stratification by Agent


A WHO pharmacovigilance database analysis (>313,000 reports) found FGAs had a reporting odds ratio of 2.2 for catatonia syndromes compared to SGAs.[52] The highest individual risks were for molindone (ROR 6.0) and haloperidol (ROR 3.8).[52] NMS frequency with FGAs is approximately 0.2% versus 0.006% with SGAs; NMS mortality is 10–20% with FGAs versus 5.5% with SGAs.[53]

Safer Options When Antipsychotics Are Necessary


- Clozapine: Preferred for catatonia with psychosis, especially chronic schizophrenia; use at <150 mg/day[1][8]

- Aripiprazole: May have advantage due to partial dopamine agonism[4][8]

- Low-potency SGAs: Thought to carry least risk; should be given in combination with a benzodiazepine[8]

- Check serum iron before trialing any antipsychotic in catatonia (low iron is an NMS risk factor)[8]

- High-potency FGAs should be avoided[8]

- Never give antipsychotics during malignant catatonia or NMS[8]

---

12. Second-Line Pharmacotherapy


The NMDA antagonists amantadine and memantine are second-line options, typically used to augment benzodiazepines.[1][8] A narrative review identified 27 cases treated with amantadine and 20 with memantine, with rapid responses reported.[54] They may be particularly useful in comorbid catatonia-delirium (unlikely to worsen delirium) and in schizophrenia-associated catatonia (less responsive to benzodiazepines).[8] Amantadine is started at 50 mg twice daily and increased to 400 mg/day; memantine at 5 mg twice daily, increased to 10 mg twice daily.[8] Amantadine lowers the seizure threshold and should be used cautiously in epilepsy and anti-NMDAR encephalitis.[8] Valproate and carbamazepine may also be effective, particularly as adjuncts.[8]

13. Resident Algorithm for Catatonia Management


Step 1: Screen and Diagnose


- Use the Catatonia Quick Screen (CQS): excitement, mutism, abnormal staring, abnormal posture — any positive → full BFCRS

- Complete the 14-item BFCSI: score ≥2 → positive screen → complete full 23-item BFCRS

- In ICU patients: use threshold of ≥4 BFCSI items

- Document baseline BFCRS score

Step 2: Initial Workup


- Labs: CBC, CMP, CK, serum iron, TSH, urinalysis, urine drug screen, blood cultures if febrile

- Consider: anti-NMDAR antibodies, autoimmune encephalitis panel, LP if indicated

- Imaging: brain MRI if first episode or medical etiology suspected

- EEG: if seizures suspected or to rule out non-convulsive status epilepticus

- Review medication list: identify and hold dopamine antagonists; assess for recent benzodiazepine/clozapine discontinuation

Step 3: Lorazepam Challenge (see protocol below)


- Administer lorazepam 2 mg IV (1 mg if elderly, pediatric, or respiratory risk)

- Reassess BFCRS at 30 minutes

- If <50% improvement → repeat 2 mg IV → reassess at 30 minutes

- Document response

Step 4: Initiate Treatment Based on Response


Positive challenge (≥50% BFCRS reduction):

- Start scheduled lorazepam IV (e.g., 2 mg IV q6–8h)

- Titrate daily by 2 mg increments to sustained response

- Typical range: 8–24 mg/day (higher in ASD/NDD)

- Reassess BFCRS daily

- Convert to PO after 24–48 hours of IV stability (may need dose increase)

- Maximal response expected in 3–7 days

Negative or partial challenge:

- Start scheduled lorazepam regardless (negative challenge does not rule out catatonia)

- Add amantadine 50 mg BID → titrate to 200 mg BID, OR memantine 5 mg BID → 10 mg BID

- If no response by day 7 → ECT referral

Step 5: Assess for Malignant Features


- Temperature >38°C, autonomic instability, elevated CK, leukocytosis, low iron

- If present → urgent ECT referral, ICU transfer, aggressive supportive care

- Stop all antipsychotics immediately

Step 6: Supportive Care (All Patients)


- VTE prophylaxis (pharmacologic + mechanical)

- Pressure ulcer prevention (repositioning q2h, skin assessments)

- Daily passive range of motion/stretching

- Ensure hydration and nutrition (tube feeding/IV fluids if needed)

- Aspiration precautions

- Monitor vitals, CK, fluid balance

Step 7: Taper and Relapse Prevention


- Inpatient taper: 10–25% per day

- Outpatient taper: up to 25% per week

- Prescribe PRN benzodiazepine during taper

- Monitor for early signs of recurrence (slowed motor performance)

- Consider maintenance ECT or clozapine (<150 mg/day) for chronic/recurrent cases

15. ECT Referral Criteria


ECT should be pursued in the following situations:

1. No response to scheduled lorazepam after 7 days of adequate dosing (≥8 mg/day)[8]

2. Malignant catatonia — urgent referral without waiting for benzodiazepine trial (autonomic instability, hyperthermia, elevated CK, altered consciousness)[8]

3. Life-threatening features: inability to maintain oral intake, severe dehydration/malnutrition, inanition[2]

4. NMS not responding to dantrolene/bromocriptine[34]

5. Partial benzodiazepine response with persistent significant functional impairment after dose optimization and augmentation with NMDA antagonists[8]

6. Chronic/recurrent catatonia requiring maintenance treatment[8]

7. Catatonia in pregnancy (benzodiazepines carry teratogenic risk; ECT is relatively safe)[8]

ECT parameters: Bitemporal preferred (RUL acceptable for treatment-naïve patients); thrice weekly standard (daily for severe/malignant); expect response after 4–6 sessions; may need ≥12 sessions; continue concurrent benzodiazepines during ECT course.[8][1]

Summary


Catatonia is a common, underrecognized neuropsychiatric syndrome affecting 5–18% of psychiatric inpatients and 3.3% of medical inpatients, with the majority of cases occurring in mood disorders rather than schizophrenia. The BFCRS remains the gold-standard assessment tool, with the CQS offering rapid screening. The lorazepam challenge (2 mg IV) is both diagnostic and therapeutic, with response rates of 77% overall and up to 90% in acute cases. Scheduled lorazepam (8–24 mg/day) is first-line treatment, with ECT as second-line for benzodiazepine-refractory or malignant cases. Malignant catatonia is a medical emergency requiring urgent ECT and ICU care. Antipsychotics carry meaningful risks — particularly high-potency FGAs — and should be used cautiously, with clozapine preferred when antipsychotics are necessary. Catatonia and delirium frequently co-occur despite DSM-5-TR exclusion criteria, and clinicians should screen for both. Special populations (ASD/NDD) require higher doses, longer treatment durations, and heightened vigilance for diagnostic delay. The evidence base remains limited by the absence of RCTs, and treatment decisions rely heavily on expert consensus and observational data.
