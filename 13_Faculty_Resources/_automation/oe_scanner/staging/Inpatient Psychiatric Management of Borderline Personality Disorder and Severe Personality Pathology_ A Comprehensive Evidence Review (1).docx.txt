


Inpatient Psychiatric Management of Borderline Personality Disorder and Severe Personality Pathology: A Comprehensive Evidence Review


---

1. Scope and Framing


This review synthesizes the evidence base for managing patients with borderline personality disorder (BPD) and other severe personality pathology on inpatient psychiatric units. The focus is on practical inpatient management — when to admit, how to structure the admission, what to do (and not do) pharmacologically and psychotherapeutically, and how to discharge safely. The evidence draws from Cochrane systematic reviews, major guideline statements (NICE CG78, APA 2001/2010, Lancet 2021 seminar, JAMA 2023 review), landmark RCTs, and consensus frameworks. BPD constitutes approximately 20% of psychiatric hospital admissions and outpatient referrals, making competent management a core skill for every inpatient psychiatrist.[1]

---

2. Indications and Contraindications for Hospitalization


When to Admit


All major guidelines converge on a narrow set of indications for inpatient admission:[2][3]

- Confirmed suicidal intent with a plan and inability to contract for safety in the community

- Life-threatening self-injury or serious suicide attempt requiring medical stabilization

- Acute psychotic episodes (transient, stress-related paranoid ideation or severe dissociation) that impair reality testing

- Extreme agitation or anxiety unmanageable in outpatient settings

- Serious attacks on others or imminent danger to third parties

- Inability to access or tolerate outpatient treatment when day-hospital or residential alternatives are unavailable[4]

The critical clinical distinction is between suicide attempts, non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI), and high-risk behaviors — these must be differentiated before any admission decision.[2]

When Not to Admit


Expert consensus and guideline recommendations are equally clear about contraindications:[2][3][5]

- NSSI without suicidal intent — inpatient crisis intervention should not be initiated for non-life-threatening self-injury

- Chronic suicidality without acute escalation — recurrent suicidal ideation in the context of ongoing outpatient treatment is generally better managed with crisis planning and outpatient intensification

- Repeated serial hospitalizations — inconsistent treatment histories are associated with suicide attempts and completed suicides; frequent admissions can disrupt longer-term outpatient therapies[2]

- Behavioral escalation aimed at obtaining admission — admitting in response to escalating threats without genuine acute risk reinforces maladaptive contingencies

Outpatient crisis management is the preferred default whenever feasible. The Lancet 2021 seminar explicitly states: "Offer crisis intervention on an outpatient basis whenever possible".[2]

---

3. The Risk of Iatrogenic Harm


The Widely Held Concern


The belief that hospitalization causes regression, reinforces maladaptive behaviors, and creates institutional dependency in BPD patients is pervasive in psychiatric training. This concern has shaped decades of clinical practice, but the empirical evidence directly testing this hypothesis is surprisingly thin.[6]

What the Evidence Actually Shows


The most direct empirical examination is the Fowler et al. (2018) naturalistic longitudinal study comparing 245 BPD inpatients with 220 matched reference inpatients during extended treatment. Large effect-size improvements were observed in depression, anxiety, suicidal ideation, and functional disability (Cohen's d ≥ 1.0). Clinical deterioration was rare (≤1.1% on any outcome), with no difference from the non-BPD cohort. The authors concluded there was "no empirical support" for the assertion that extended inpatient care leads to deterioration in BPD.[6]

Chen et al. (2025) found that BPD-positive patients on an acute inpatient unit reported significantly greater improvements in anxiety, depression, and general psychiatric severity compared to BPD-negative patients during short-term hospitalization.[7]

Where Iatrogenic Harm Is Documented


The most tangible iatrogenic risk is medication escalation during hospitalization. A study of 569 BPD patients at a tertiary care unit found that the odds of being prescribed antidepressants, antipsychotics, mood stabilizers, hypnotics, or anxiolytics were all higher at discharge than at admission (incidence rate ratio 1.9 for total psychotropic prescriptions).[8] This is particularly concerning given that no medication class has been consistently effective for BPD core symptoms.[3][9]

A meta-synthesis of patient experiences (Stapleton & Wright, 2019) identified negative elements including lack of staff contact, negative staff attitudes, staff's lack of BPD knowledge, coercive involuntary admission, and poor discharge planning.[10] These process failures — rather than hospitalization per se — appear to drive negative outcomes.

4. Brief Admissions vs. Prolonged Admissions


Guideline Consensus: Brief, Goal-Directed Admissions


The dominant position across all major guidelines favors brief crisis admissions with discharge planning beginning on day one.[2][5] The Goal-Oriented Limited-Duration (GOLD) approach (Siefert, 2012) provides a structured framework: a preadmission treatment agreement with a planned discharge date, clear treatment goals, and specified staff roles. Setting a discharge date early allows the team to immediately begin working with feelings of rejection and abandonment rather than avoiding them.[5]

Evidence on Structured Brief Admission Models


The Brief Admission Skåne (BASRCT) model allows self-referral admissions of up to 3 days, maximum 3 times per month, integrated into existing treatment plans before crises occur.[11] However, the 2022 Cochrane review of crisis interventions for BPD found only two RCTs total and no clear evidence of benefit for either joint crisis plans or brief admission over treatment as usual.[12]

The Gold Card SA initiative in South Australia — a 4-session structured psychological intervention after acute crisis — demonstrated 63% reduction in ED presentations, 65% reduction in mental health admissions, and 82% reduction in bed days in an uncontrolled pre-post design (n=332).[13] A specialized short-term crisis track in Basel was associated with significantly shorter median length of stay and stable low rates of coercive measures over 8 years.[14]

Extended Treatment: Not Necessarily Harmful


The Fowler et al. data challenge the assumption that longer stays are inherently harmful.[6] Bartak et al. (2011) compared outpatient, day hospital, and inpatient psychotherapeutic treatment for Cluster B personality disorders (n=207) and found the inpatient group showed the largest improvements, with a marginally significant advantage over outpatient treatment for psychiatric symptoms (p=0.057).[15] Kujovic et al. (2024) compared 8-week vs. 12-week adapted inpatient DBT (n=175) and found both durations produced large effect sizes (BSL-23 d=1.29 and d=1.16 respectively) with no significant between-group differences.[16]

The key variable is not length of stay but structure of stay. Unstructured, open-ended admissions without clear goals are the problem — not duration per se.

5. Managing Core Inpatient Challenges


Splitting and Staff Conflict


Splitting — the tendency to view some clinicians as "all good" and others as "all bad" — is among the most disruptive dynamics on inpatient units. The JAMA review explicitly recommends that clinicians "facilitate open communication and agreement on a consistent approach with all treating clinicians" to prevent this dynamic.[3] Practical strategies include:

- Daily team huddles with explicit discussion of the patient's interpersonal patterns

- Single treatment plan communicated identically by all staff

- Rotating primary contacts to prevent exclusive attachment to one clinician

- Normalizing the dynamic in team formulation: splitting reflects the patient's internal experience of relationships, not staff failure

- Therapist consultation meetings — identified as one of five common elements across all effective BPD treatments ("therapists discuss cases, including personal reactions, with others")[1]

A quasi-experimental study on a psychiatric intensive care unit demonstrated that implementing a schema-informed case formulation for a patient with emotionally unstable personality disorder resulted in complete extinction of incidents requiring seclusion, restraint, and rapid tranquilization.[17]

Recurrent Self-Harm


The critical first step is functional behavioral analysis: identifying the exact problem behavior, triggering parameters, maintenance parameters, and vulnerability factors (sleep problems, substance misuse, social problems).[2] Interventions should be based on this analysis rather than reflexive escalation of observation levels. The Lancet seminar recommends teaching distress tolerance skills as the primary intervention for reducing high levels of distress.[2]

Chronic Suicidality


Chronic suicidality in BPD must be distinguished from acute suicidal crises. Gunderson's NEJM review recommends that when suicidal intent is denied, outpatient crisis management with concerned attention and safety planning is preferred over hospitalization.[18] The VA/DoD clinical practice guideline supports crisis response planning — a collaborative, step-by-step document including crisis signs, self-management skills, social supports, and crisis resources — which significantly reduced suicide attempts compared to treatment as usual.[19][20]

Affective Instability


Short-term symptoms of depression or anxiety that are part of BPD emotional instability and can be related to specific triggering situations (e.g., interpersonal problems) should not be misinterpreted as comorbid disorders and should be treated solely with psychotherapy.[3] This is one of the most common errors on inpatient units — treating BPD-related affective storms as if they were independent mood or anxiety disorders, leading to inappropriate medication escalation.

6. DBT-Informed Inpatient Approaches


Evidence Base


The systematic review by Bloom et al. (2012) evaluated 11 studies of DBT in inpatient settings and found considerable variation in implementation but consistent reductions in suicidal ideation, self-injurious behaviors, depression, and anxiety. Results for anger and violent behaviors were mixed.[21]

Bohus et al. (2004) conducted a controlled trial of 3-month inpatient DBT (n=50) showing significant improvements on 10 of 11 psychopathological variables, with 42% meeting criteria for clinically relevant recovery.[22] Tebbett-Mock et al. (2020) found that acute-care adolescent inpatient DBT (n=425) produced significantly fewer constant observation hours, fewer suicide attempts and self-injury incidents, fewer restraints, and fewer days hospitalized compared to historical TAU, saving $251,609 in staff time for constant observation.[23]

A dismantling RCT by Linehan et al. (2015) demonstrated that better and faster treatment effects occurred when group skills training was involved, supporting the value of skills groups as a core component even when full DBT cannot be delivered.[24] The McMain et al. (2017) RCT showed that brief DBT skills training produced superior reductions in self-harm, with the DBT group reaching near-zero rates by 32-week follow-up and lower hospitalization probability during treatment.[25]

Practical Inpatient DBT Adaptation


On acute units where full DBT is not feasible, the following DBT-informed elements can be incorporated:

- Daily distress tolerance skills groups (TIPP skills: Temperature, Intense exercise, Paced breathing, Progressive relaxation)

- Chain analysis of self-harm episodes as a structured alternative to punitive responses

- Diary cards tracking emotions, urges, and skill use

- Validation strategies balanced with change-oriented interventions

- Behavioral targets hierarchy: life-threatening behaviors → therapy-interfering behaviors → quality-of-life-interfering behaviors

7. Safety Planning


The Safety Planning Intervention (SPI) is a cognitive-behavioral intervention with six components: (1) identifying personalized warning signs, (2) internal coping strategies, (3) social contacts and settings for distraction, (4) individuals who can provide support, (5) professional and crisis resources, and (6) lethal means counseling.[26] In combination with follow-up contacts, SPI is associated with reduction in suicidality.[27] The VA/DoD guideline recommends SPI for any patient at intermediate or high acute or chronic suicide risk.[20]

No-suicide contracts are explicitly not recommended — they lack evidence of effectiveness, may feel coercive, and do not include personalized coping mechanisms.[27] Safety plans should be living documents, updated during the admission and reviewed at discharge.

8. Team-Based Behavioral Plans


Structure


Effective behavioral plans for BPD inpatients share common elements derived from expert consensus and the common factors literature:[1][3]

1. Formulation-driven: Based on a shared understanding of the patient's interpersonal patterns, triggers, and maintaining factors

2. Consistent across all staff: Every team member responds identically to target behaviors

3. Explicit contingencies: Clear "if-then" statements for predictable scenarios

4. Validation + limits: Acknowledging the patient's distress while maintaining boundaries

5. Regular review: Updated at least weekly in team meetings

Staff Training


Even brief training interventions produce meaningful shifts in staff attitudes. A one-day interactive training session significantly reduced stigmatizing attitudes (all measures p < 0.001).[28] A one-day Good Psychiatric Management (GPM) training improved attitudes on 11 of 13 items, with changes persisting at 6-month follow-up.[29] A one-day STEPPS workshop (n=271) produced significantly greater empathy, increased competency, and reduced dislike toward BPD patients.[30] Key effective training elements include co-production with people with lived experience, communicating a psychological model, and teaching clinical skills for practice.[31]

9. Medication Use and Overuse


The Evidence-Practice Gap


No medication has been approved by any regulatory agency for BPD, and no class of psychoactive medication has been consistently effective in RCTs for core BPD symptoms.[3][32] Yet approximately 80% of BPD patients take standing psychotropic medications, with 28.6% taking four or more simultaneously.[9][18] Among European BPD inpatients, approximately 70% receive antidepressants and 70% receive antipsychotics.[9] Out of 87 medications used in clinical practice for BPD, RCT data exist for only 9.[33]

What the Evidence Shows by Drug Class




The forest plot from the Lieslehto et al. (2023) Swedish nationwide cohort study illustrates the comparative suicide risk across pharmacotherapy classes, demonstrating that benzodiazepines carried the highest risk elevation while ADHD medications were uniquely protective.

NICE Crisis Pharmacotherapy Recommendations


For acute crisis management, NICE recommends a single drug at minimum effective dose for no longer than 1 week.[3] Preferred agents are sedative antihistamines (e.g., promethazine) or low-potency antipsychotics (e.g., quetiapine) for agitation or sleep problems.[2] Benzodiazepines and tricyclic antidepressants should be avoided.[2]

Deprescription as a Therapeutic Intervention


Soler et al. (2022) demonstrated that DBT skills training significantly reduced polypharmacy, with substantial decreases in the number of medications, medication load index, and sedation load index compared to controls.[39] This supports the principle that teaching coping skills can replace medications rather than the reverse.

10. Discharge Planning and Transitions of Care


Core Principles


- Begin discharge planning on day one — this is a consistent recommendation across all major guidelines[2][5]

- Set a discharge date at admission (GOLD approach) — this allows the team to work with abandonment fears proactively rather than reactively[5]

- Make binding agreements about further treatment between the patient and their therapeutic team, reviewed after crisis intervention[2]

- Coordinate across providers — all involved service providers should be informed about medication changes and treatment plan modifications[2]

- Ensure outpatient follow-up is scheduled before discharge — not merely "recommended"

- Update the safety plan at discharge with current triggers, coping strategies, and crisis contacts

Stepped Care and Continuity


Inconsistent treatment histories are associated with suicide attempts and completed suicides.[2] Stepped care models — where intensity of treatment is matched to clinical staging — can reduce the need for severe crisis interventions.[2] Inpatient DBT may function as an "intensive orientation" to outpatient DBT services, making the transition from inpatient to outpatient a therapeutic bridge rather than a cliff.[21]

Family Involvement


Family members of individuals with BPD have elevated levels of grief and burden compared with relatives of individuals with other severe mental disorders.[2] An RCT of group psychoeducation for 68 family members reduced emotional stress compared with wait-list control.[3] The Family Connections program (12 sessions, DBT-based) showed that patients of caregivers who received the intervention showed significant improvements in stress, depression, and anxiety.[40] Highest-rated sessions in family psychoeducation programs covered communication skills and coping with crises.[41]

12. Practical Inpatient Management Principles


The 15 Principles


1. Admit only for acute safety — confirmed suicidal intent, life-threatening self-injury, acute psychosis, or danger to others. NSSI without suicidal intent is not an indication for admission.[2]

2. Set a discharge date at admission. Communicate it clearly and begin discharge planning on day one.[5][2]

3. Conduct a functional behavioral analysis of the crisis behavior within the first 24 hours — identify triggers, maintaining factors, and vulnerability factors.[2]

4. Differentiate BPD affective instability from comorbid mood disorders. Short-term depression or anxiety related to interpersonal triggers should not be treated as independent disorders.[3]

5. Minimize medication changes. Resist the pressure to "do something" pharmacologically. If medications are used in crisis, use a single agent at minimum dose for ≤1 week. Never prescribe benzodiazepines.[3][2]

6. Deprescribe before prescribing. Discontinue medications with unclear benefit before adding new ones.[18]

7. Maintain team consistency. Hold daily huddles. Ensure every staff member communicates the same plan. Document the behavioral plan prominently.[3]

8. Validate distress while holding limits. The therapeutic relationship should balance appreciation of the patient's subjective needs with clear orientation toward problem-solving.[2]

9. Teach at least one distress tolerance skill during every admission (e.g., TIPP, ice diving, paced breathing).

10. Complete a safety plan (Stanley-Brown model) collaboratively before discharge. Update it if one already exists.[26]

11. Name the diagnosis. Psychoeducation about BPD is therapeutic — most patients experience relief upon learning their experiences constitute a coherent, treatable syndrome.[2]

12. Schedule outpatient follow-up before discharge — do not discharge with "follow up with your outpatient provider."

13. Involve family/supports with psychoeducation about the disorder, communication strategies, and crisis management.[3][40]

14. Use team formulation to understand splitting and countertransference. Discuss personal reactions to the patient in structured team meetings.[1]

15. Document the rationale for not admitting as carefully as the rationale for admitting. Both decisions carry risk.

15. Sample Family/Staff Communication Language


For Family Members


> "Your loved one has been diagnosed with borderline personality disorder. This is a real, well-studied condition — not a character flaw. It involves difficulty regulating emotions, which can lead to intense reactions that may seem out of proportion to the situation. The good news is that BPD is one of the most treatable conditions in psychiatry, and most people improve significantly over time."

> "When [patient] is in distress, the most helpful thing you can do is acknowledge their pain without trying to fix it immediately. You might say: 'I can see this is really hard for you. I'm here.' Try to avoid statements like 'just calm down' or 'you're overreacting' — these tend to escalate distress rather than reduce it."

> "We have a safety plan that [patient] helped create. We'd like to review it with you so you know what steps to take if a crisis occurs after discharge. This includes warning signs to watch for, coping strategies that work for [patient], and who to call."

For Staff (Shift Handoff Language)


> "[Patient] is on a structured behavioral plan — please review it before interacting. Key points: all staff respond identically to [specific behavior]. If [patient] asks to speak only to you or tells you that another staff member was unhelpful, respond with: 'The whole team is working together. Let me check in with the team and we'll get back to you.' Do not make individual promises or exceptions without team discussion."

> "If you're feeling frustrated, pulled in, or like you need to rescue this patient — that's expected and normal. These are common reactions when working with BPD. Please bring it to the team huddle rather than acting on it individually."

16. Summary


The evidence base for inpatient management of BPD is characterized by strong consensus on principles but a striking paucity of high-quality RCTs — the 2022 Cochrane review of crisis interventions found only two trials and no clear evidence of benefit for any specific crisis intervention over treatment as usual.[12] What the evidence does support is that psychotherapy is the definitive treatment,[3][2] medications have no consistent benefit for core BPD symptoms,[3][9] brief structured admissions with discharge planning from day one are preferred, and[2][5] team consistency is the single most important structural intervention on inpatient units.[3][1] The iatrogenic harm hypothesis, while clinically important to keep in mind, has limited direct empirical support — the documented harms are medication escalation and process failures (negative staff attitudes, poor discharge planning), not hospitalization itself.[6][10] Residents who learn to validate without rescuing, hold limits without punishing, and resist the urge to prescribe their way out of BPD crises will provide better care than those armed with any pharmacological algorithm.

Social Determinants of Health, Structural Competency, and Health Inequities in Inpatient Psychiatry: A Comprehensive Evidence Review


---

1. Scope and Framing


This review synthesizes the evidence on how social determinants of health (SDOH) shape psychiatric inpatient outcomes, how structural competency provides a framework for understanding and addressing these forces, and what interventions can mitigate the resulting inequities. The evidence draws from systematic reviews, meta-analyses, landmark trials, APA resource documents, the DSM-5-TR, VA/DoD guidelines, and major conceptual papers. The scope encompasses twelve SDOH domains and their intersection with five key psychiatric outcomes: readmission, suicide risk, medication adherence, ED boarding, and discharge failure.

---

2. Structural Competency: The Conceptual Foundation


From Cultural to Structural Competency


Structural competency, introduced by Metzl and Hansen (2014), shifts clinical attention from cross-cultural understanding of individual patients toward the social, economic, and political forces that produce health inequities at levels above the clinical encounter.[1] The framework was explicitly proposed as an evolution beyond cultural competency, which the authors argued relies on static understandings of culture and demonstrates limited effects on health outcomes.[1][2] Structural competency trains clinicians to recognize how upstream determinants — policies, institutions, economic systems, and power structures — shape how symptoms, attitudes, and diseases manifest in patients.[1]

The Five Core Competencies


Metzl and Hansen define five skills: (1) recognizing structures that shape clinical interactions, (2) developing an extra-clinical language of structure, (3) rearticulating "cultural" formulations in structural terms, (4) observing and imagining structural interventions, and (5) developing structural humility — recognizing the limits of one's own structural knowledge.[1] The critical reframe is that what clinicians typically attribute to "culture" (e.g., non-adherence, missed appointments, treatment refusal) should be understood as products of structural forces (transportation barriers, insurance gaps, food insecurity).[1]

Related Frameworks


Structural vulnerability (Bourgois, Holmes, et al., 2017) operationalizes these concepts for clinical practice through a structured assessment tool that extends the traditional social history to identify patients whose health problems are exacerbated by local hierarchies and power relationships.[3] Structural stigma (Sukhera et al., 2022) provides a four-component educational framework — Recognize, Reflect, Reframe, Respond — for dismantling stigma related to mental health and substance use in clinical learning environments.[4] The APA's DSM-5-TR (2022) formally acknowledges that racism exists at personal, interpersonal, systemic/institutional, and social structural levels, and that structural racism manifests in pervasive inequities in economic resources, power, and privilege.[5]

---

3. SDOH Domains and Their Impact on Psychiatric Outcomes


Housing Insecurity


Housing instability is the single most consistently replicated SDOH predictor of psychiatric readmission. A 2026 systematic review of 15 studies found housing instability was "repeatedly associated with psychiatric readmission across population-based, hospital-based, and clinical registry studies." Effect sizes are large: homelessness at index admission was associated with aHR 2.04 (95% CI 1.65–2.51) for 30-day readmission in a Vancouver cohort; an urban community hospital found an OR of 29 for early readmission among homeless patients; and homeless patients in England had 2.49 times the rate of emergency readmission versus housed patients.[2][6][7][8] Among homeless adults, mean antipsychotic medication possession ratio over 15 years was only 0.41 — well below the 0.80 threshold for clinical effectiveness — with duration of homelessness significantly associated with lower adherence.[3] The prevalence of lifetime suicide attempt among homeless individuals is 28.9% (95% CI 21.7–37.2%).[9]

Poverty and Socioeconomic Deprivation


A Finnish national study found a steep negative income gradient for first psychiatric hospital admissions, with the lowest income decile having incidence rate ratios of 2.94–4.46 compared to the highest decile.[10] Area-level deprivation shows a dose-response relationship with involuntary psychiatric hospitalization.[11] Among patients with schizophrenia, low socioeconomic level independently predicted medication nonadherence (OR 3.68, 95% CI 1.42–9.53).[12] Financial strain disproportionately affects psychiatric medication adherence compared to non-psychiatric medications.[12] Being on income assistance was associated with increased suicide attempt risk in the year after psychiatric discharge (HR 1.17, 95% CI 1.01–1.35).[13]

Cumulative Social Needs: The Dose-Response Relationship


Using the 2017 Nationwide Readmissions Database (n=13.2 million), a dose-response relationship was demonstrated: 30-day readmission was 11.5% with no health-related social needs (HRSN), 27.0% with 1 HRSN domain, and 63.5% with all 5 domains (aOR 12.55, 95% CI 9.04–17.43).[14] This compounding effect underscores that SDOH rarely operate in isolation.

The following figure from a VA study illustrates how social risks independently predict readmission and ED use even after clinical adjustment, with food insecurity, legal problems, and neighborhood deprivation showing the strongest associations.[15]

Racism and Structural Discrimination


Racial disparities in inpatient psychiatry are pervasive, well-documented, and operate across every stage of the care pathway.

Involuntary commitment: A major international meta-analysis (71 studies, 1,953,135 participants) found Black Caribbean patients had OR 2.53 (95% CI 2.03–3.16) and Black African patients OR 2.27 (1.62–3.19) for compulsory admission compared with White patients.[16] A US prospective cohort found Black patients were significantly more likely to be involuntarily admitted (aOR 1.57, 95% CI 1.26–1.95).[1]

Restraint and seclusion: A large retrospective study (n=29,739) found Black patients were significantly more likely to experience a restraint event (aOR 1.85) and had significantly longer restraint duration.[17] Among patients awaiting psychiatric hospitalization in the ED, Black race was associated with nearly 3-fold higher odds of physical restraint (OR 2.84, 1.03–7.84).[2]

Diagnostic disparities: The overdiagnosis of schizophrenia in Black patients is one of the most persistent disparities in psychiatry. A 2024 JAMA Psychiatry meta-analysis (64 studies, 54,929 people) confirmed Black individuals had a pooled OR of 2.07 (1.64–2.61) for schizophrenia diagnosis compared with White individuals, and structured assessments did not fully mitigate the disparity.[18] Critically, a 2025 national study found that after adjusting for a neighborhood Social Vulnerability Metric, the Black-White difference was attenuated and no longer statistically significant (aOR 2.49, 0.63–9.90), suggesting neighborhood-level social determinants partially explain the disparity.[19]

The forest plot from the van der Ven et al. JAMA Psychiatry meta-analysis illustrates the consistency of elevated schizophrenia diagnosis risk among Black compared with White individuals across studies:

Treatment quality: Black patients received disproportionately more PRN antipsychotics (17.7% vs. 8.2% of White patients) and were 58% more likely to receive antipsychotic PRNs over non-antipsychotic PRNs.[20] Black patients were significantly less likely to receive clozapine (OR 0.40) and more likely to receive haloperidol (OR 1.52).[3] Racial/ethnic minority patients were significantly more likely to be admitted to low-safety inpatient psychiatric facilities.[21]

Pathways to care: Black patients had significantly higher odds of police transport to the ED (aOR 1.64, 95% CI 1.57–1.72).[22] In England, Black Caribbean patients had OR 2.64 for police contact during pathways to psychiatric care, while GP involvement was less likely.[23]

Insurance Barriers


Children with government insurance were 18% more likely to be rehospitalized within 90 days than those with private insurance (aOR 1.18, 95% CI 1.04–1.34).[24] Medicaid patients aged 45–64 had the highest adjusted odds of 30-day readmission (aOR 1.67 vs. private insurance).[25] Having health insurance was a strong correlate of mental health treatment use (private insurance aOR 1.63; Medicaid aOR 2.66 for any mental illness), and uninsured individuals were 6.9 percentage points less likely to receive counseling than Medicaid enrollees.[26]

Rural Access Issues


More than half of all US counties — all rural — lack a practicing psychiatrist, psychologist, or social worker, and over 85% of federally designated mental health professional shortage areas are in rural communities.[27] Only 1.6% of rural zip codes have an office-based mental health specialist physician, compared to 17.6% of urban zip codes (UOR 13.52).[28] Psychiatry workforce adequacy in non-metropolitan areas is projected at 34.8% in 2026, declining to below 25% in some states by 2038.[29] Rural Medicare beneficiaries had lower rates of follow-up care after psychiatric hospitalization (HR 0.81, p < 0.001) and greater ED use postdischarge.[30]

Criminal Legal Involvement


Approximately 15–30% of county jail inmates have serious mental illness, compared to ~4% of the general community — there are now more than ten times as many individuals with SMI behind bars as in state psychiatric hospitals.[31][32] A 2025 meta-analysis of 131 publications across 43 countries found pooled prevalence of depression at 12.8% and any psychosis at 4.1% among incarcerated populations.[9] Prior incarceration was associated with significantly worse mental health at age 50 (aOR 1.9 for any mental health history; aOR 2.2 for past-year depression).[33] Justice system involvement was among the SDOH most strongly associated with suicide mortality in a 2025 JAMA Psychiatry umbrella review.[9] Suicide is the leading cause of death in jails, at a rate of 46 per 100,000.[32]

Food Insecurity


People with severe mental illness are 2.71 times more likely to report food insecurity than the general population (95% CI 1.72–3.25), with a prevalence of approximately 40%.[34] A systematic review and meta-analysis found food insecurity was the SDOH most consistently associated with lower medication adherence (aOR 0.56, 95% CI 0.42–0.70).[35] Food insecurity is associated with depression (OR 2.74) and anxiety (OR 2.41).[36] State SNAP eligibility expansion policies were associated with reduced rates of major depressive episodes and mental illness at the population level.[37]

Transportation Barriers


Twenty-eight percent of patients across three urban EDs delayed medical visits due to transportation issues, and transportation difficulties were associated with poor medication adherence.[38] Patients with schizophrenia spectrum disorders had 27.9% increased odds of treatment adherence when travel time to facilities was ≤30 minutes.[2] Transportation barriers are cited as a major barrier to treatment engagement across racial/ethnic groups, with Black participants particularly noting inability to afford public transportation for appointments and medication pickup.[34]

Digital Access Barriers


Approximately 6 million children and adolescents in the US have inadequate in-person and digital availability of child psychiatric services, with 51% of children in rural counties lacking both forms of access.[39] In 2020, 596 US counties (19%) lacked both psychiatrists and broadband coverage, representing 10.5 million residents; these counties had significantly higher rates of drug overdose mortality (40.9 vs. 11.1 per 100,000) and completed suicide (88.1 vs. 11.3 per 100,000).[2] Despite the promise of telehealth, its expansion has not equally benefited all populations — nonmetro respondents were less likely to use virtual mental health care (AOR 0.74), and older adults, those living in poverty, and those without a high school diploma were significantly less likely to use teletherapy.[40][41]

Family Instability and Trauma Exposure


The 2025 JAMA Psychiatry umbrella review found childhood maltreatment, sexual assault, foster care experience, and exposure to others' suicide were among the SDOH most strongly associated with suicide-related outcomes.[9] Among hospitalized Veterans, food insecurity, legal problems, and neighborhood deprivation were the strongest social risk predictors of 30-day readmission.[15] Lack of family support was associated with antipsychotic nonadherence (aOR 2.01, 95% CI 1.33–3.04).[1]

4. ED Boarding: A Structural Crisis


Psychiatric boarding — holding patients in the ED after the decision to admit — represents a structural failure with profound consequences. Psychiatric patients remain in the ED 3.2 times longer than non-psychiatric patients, with average boarding times of 7–11 hours nationally and frequently exceeding 24 hours.[3] A Canadian study found mental health patients' median boarding time was 120% longer than non-mental health patients, and this gap widened over time.[2]

Harms are well-documented: patients boarded >14 hours more frequently required high-observation beds (14% vs. 3.5%), antipsychotics (44% vs. 14%), or sedation (55% vs. 33%).[42] Among older adults, functional decline occurred in 12.6% and restraint episodes in 8.1%, with both events occurring only among those boarding ≥24 hours.[43] ED boarding duration is a direct risk factor for developing delirium or severe agitation during subsequent inpatient admission.[44]

Disparities are stark: Black patients boarded significantly longer than White patients for psychiatric admissions, and Medicaid patients were more than twice as likely to remain in the ED ≥24 hours compared to privately insured patients.[45][46] Boarding ≥24 hours was associated with a significant increase in perceived discrimination, particularly among patients from marginalized racial/ethnic groups.[47]

5. Post-Discharge Suicide Risk and SDOH


The post-discharge period carries extremely elevated suicide risk — pooled estimates show suicide rates of ~2,950 per 100,000 person-years in the first week after psychiatric discharge.[48] SDOH are critical risk factors: the 2024 VA/DoD Clinical Practice Guideline explicitly includes housing instability, homelessness, exposure to abuse/violence, interpersonal conflict, and financial and legal problems in suicide risk assessment.[48]

The following figure from the Na et al. (2025) JAMA Psychiatry umbrella review summarizes effect sizes of SDOH on suicide mortality, with justice system involvement, exposure to suicide, firearm accessibility, and divorced status showing the strongest risk associations:

Outpatient follow-up within 7 days was associated with HR 0.82 for suicide in a Korean population-based study and aRR 0.44 for suicide over 180 days among Medicaid-insured youth.[48] Non-Hispanic Black youth and those with managed care insurance were less likely to receive timely follow-up.[48] SDOH barriers — transportation, insurance, housing — impede the very follow-up that is protective against post-discharge suicide.

6. Interventions That Improve Outcomes


Housing First


Housing First (HF) consistently improves housing stability (SMD 1.24; 95% CI 0.86–1.62) and reduces ED visits (IRR 0.63; 95% CI 0.48–0.82) and hospitalizations (IRR 0.76; 95% CI 0.70–0.83) in meta-analyses of RCTs.[49] Long-term 7-year follow-up of the At Home/Chez Soi trial showed HF with ACT reduced days in hospital (RRR 0.32) and ED visits (RRR 0.57) among high-need participants.[50] However, direct improvements in psychiatric symptom severity are not consistently demonstrated.[49]

Critical Time Intervention (CTI)


CTI is a 9-month, 3-stage intervention that develops individualized community linkages during transitions. The original RCT found significantly lower psychiatric rehospitalization at 18 months (OR 0.11).[51] Brief CTI reduced 30-day readmission from 47% to 28% (p < .001).[52] A 2025 meta-analysis confirmed CTI significantly improved quality of life (SMD 0.23), reduced homelessness (SMD −0.20), and improved psychiatric symptoms (SMD −0.16).[53]

Collaborative Care Model (CoCM)


CoCM has the strongest evidence base among SDOH-responsive interventions, supported by over 90 RCTs.[53] The landmark IMPACT trial (n=1,801) found 45% of intervention patients achieved ≥50% reduction in depressive symptoms at 12 months vs. 19% of usual care (OR 3.45; 95% CI 2.71–4.38).[53] The SPIRIT trial — the largest mental health trial in rural primary care — demonstrated large, clinically meaningful improvements (Cohen d = 0.79–0.87) using telepsychiatry collaborative care in FQHCs without on-site psychiatrists.[53]

Assertive Community Treatment (ACT)


ACT reduces hospitalization, particularly among high utilizers. A systematic review with meta-regression (29 trials, 5,961 participants) found ACT works best when baseline hospital use is high.[54] The ACCESS-II study showed sustained 4-year improvements: hospitalization rates of 9–13%, involuntary admissions dropping from 35% to 8%, and significant improvements in psychopathology (d = 0.79).[55]

Peer Support


A 2023 meta-analysis of 28 RCTs found peer support had small but significant effects on clinical recovery (g = 0.19; 95% CI 0.11–0.27) and personal recovery (g = 0.15; 95% CI 0.04–0.27).[56] The Cochrane review found no clear effect on hospital admissions, though one RCT of peer mentors for recurrently hospitalized individuals found participants remained out of hospital significantly longer.[57][58]

SDOH Screening and Referral


The CMS Accountable Health Communities Model screened 482,967 participants and found statistically significant reductions in ED visits.[59] However, navigation services did not significantly increase community service connections or needs resolution compared to referral alone — referrals without ensuring receipt of services may be insufficient.[60]

Jail Diversion and Reentry


Mental health courts show a small effect on recidivism (d = −0.20) in meta-analysis, with MHC completion associated with greater reductions in jail days.[61] Mental health services access after release was associated with significant reduction in reincarceration risk, with each additional month between release and services access increasing reincarceration risk by 4%.[62] The Sequential Intercept Model provides the dominant conceptual framework for addressing the criminalization of mental illness across five interception points.[63]

Telepsychiatry


Multiple meta-analyses demonstrate telepsychiatry achieves clinical outcomes comparable to in-person care, with a 2023 meta-analysis of 32 RCTs finding no significant difference overall (p = 0.248) and superiority for depressive disorders (SMD −0.325).[64] However, telemental health may largely substitute for rather than supplement in-person care, resulting in only modest net increases in total specialty mental health contacts in rural areas.[65]

7. Staff Training and Structural Competency Education


Evidence on Training


A scoping review identified only 11 articles meeting inclusion criteria for structural competency training interventions, with most using active learning strategies and researcher-developed evaluation instruments.[65] A qualitative evaluation of structural competency training in a family medicine residency found residents reported positive impact on clinical practice but also feeling overwhelmed by increased recognition of structural influences on patient health.[65] At Vanderbilt, MHS majors who received structural competency education identified and analyzed structural factors at higher rates and in deeper ways than comparison groups.[66]

Teaching Frameworks


Castillo, Isom, DeBonis et al. (2020) proposed replacing or augmenting the ACGME's "systems-based practice" competency with a new core competency centered on health equity, social responsibility, and structural competency.[67] Hassan and Bui (2022) developed a structural analysis framework for incorporating structural competency into everyday clinical reasoning: (1) develop a prioritized clinical problem list, (2) identify social and structural root causes, (3) construct a prioritized structural problem list, and (4) brainstorm solutions to address structural barriers.[68] Weill Cornell developed the first OSCE designed to assess structural competency, focused on premature discharge scenarios.[69] As of academic year 2023–2024, the LCME requires medical schools to include content on basic principles of structurally competent health care.[69]

9. Resident Teaching Framework


The "STRUCTURE" Curriculum Model


A comprehensive resident teaching framework for SDOH in inpatient psychiatry, integrating the evidence reviewed above:

S — Screen systematically. Use validated SDOH screening tools (see checklist below) on every admission. Normalize screening as standard clinical practice, not an add-on.

T — Think structurally. Apply the Hassan & Bui structural analysis framework: for every clinical problem, identify the social and structural root causes alongside the biomedical ones.[68] When a patient is "non-adherent," ask what structural barriers prevented adherence before attributing it to the patient.

R — Recognize racism. Understand that racial disparities in diagnosis (schizophrenia overdiagnosis in Black patients, OR 2.07), involuntary commitment (aOR 1.57 for Black patients), restraint use (aOR 1.85 for Black patients), and treatment quality are well-documented and persistent.[18][1][17] Use structured diagnostic instruments. Question diagnostic assumptions.

U — Understand cumulative risk. Teach the dose-response relationship between social needs and outcomes (aOR 12.55 for 5 HRSN domains).[14] A patient with housing insecurity, food insecurity, and transportation barriers faces compounding — not merely additive — risk.

C — Connect to resources. Know the local resource landscape: housing programs, food banks, transportation assistance, legal aid, benefits enrollment. Referral alone is insufficient — ensure receipt of services.[60]

T — Transition with intention. Begin discharge planning on day one. Schedule outpatient follow-up before discharge. Use Critical Time Intervention principles. Ensure 7-day follow-up, which is associated with reduced suicide risk (HR 0.82) and readmission.[48]

U — Use team-based approaches. Integrate social workers, peer specialists, community health workers, and case managers into the treatment team. Structural problems require structural solutions — they cannot be solved by the physician alone.

R — Reflect on power. Practice structural humility.[1] Recognize how institutional power dynamics (involuntary commitment, restraint, discharge decisions) disproportionately affect marginalized patients. Discuss countertransference and implicit bias in team formulation.

E — Engage in advocacy. Move beyond individual patient care to systems-level change. Identify one structural barrier encountered during each rotation and propose a policy or institutional intervention.[67]

The following checklist integrates domains from the AHC HRSN Screening Tool, PRAPARE, and the structural vulnerability assessment tool, adapted for inpatient psychiatric settings:[3][70][71]

Admission SDOH Screen


Housing

- ☐ Current living situation (own/rent/shelter/street/doubled up/institutional)

- ☐ Housing stability in past 12 months (any moves, evictions, episodes of homelessness)

- ☐ Concerns about losing housing in next 2 months

- ☐ Safety of current housing (violence, structural hazards, environmental exposures)

Food Security

- ☐ "In the past 12 months, did you worry that food would run out before you had money to buy more?" (USDA 2-item screen)

- ☐ "In the past 12 months, did the food you bought not last and you didn't have money to get more?"

- ☐ Access to cooking facilities and food storage

Transportation

- ☐ Reliable transportation to medical appointments

- ☐ Transportation barriers to pharmacy access

- ☐ Distance to nearest outpatient mental health provider

Financial Security

- ☐ Employment status and stability

- ☐ Difficulty paying for basic needs (utilities, phone, medications)

- ☐ Current or recent loss of income

- ☐ Debt or legal financial obligations

Insurance and Healthcare Access

- ☐ Current insurance status and type

- ☐ Gaps in coverage in past 12 months

- ☐ Established outpatient mental health provider (name, contact)

- ☐ Established primary care provider

- ☐ Barriers to accessing outpatient care (cost, wait times, availability)

Legal/Criminal Justice

- ☐ Current or pending legal issues

- ☐ History of incarceration

- ☐ Current probation/parole status and officer contact

- ☐ Outstanding warrants or court dates

- ☐ Immigration status concerns affecting care-seeking

Safety and Violence

- ☐ Current or recent intimate partner violence

- ☐ Community violence exposure

- ☐ History of childhood maltreatment

- ☐ Firearm access in the home (lethal means counseling)

Social Support

- ☐ Emergency contact and relationship quality

- ☐ Family/friend support available after discharge

- ☐ Social isolation (lives alone, limited contacts)

- ☐ Caregiver burden on family members

Digital Access

- ☐ Access to smartphone or computer

- ☐ Reliable internet access at home

- ☐ Comfort with telehealth technology

- ☐ Phone number likely to remain active after discharge

Education and Literacy

- ☐ Highest education level

- ☐ Health literacy concerns (difficulty understanding medication instructions)

- ☐ Primary language and interpreter needs

Discrimination and Structural Vulnerability

- ☐ Experiences of discrimination in healthcare settings

- ☐ Immigration-related fears affecting care-seeking

- ☐ LGBTQ+ identity and related safety concerns

- ☐ Disability status and accommodation needs

Substance Use Context

- ☐ Substance use patterns and relationship to housing/social stability

- ☐ Access to harm reduction services

- ☐ History of overdose and naloxone access

11. Discharge Planning Implications


Structural Discharge Planning Principles


Every discharge plan should address the structural barriers identified on the SDOH screen. The following framework operationalizes this:

Before discharge, confirm:

1. Housing: Patient has a confirmed, safe place to go. If homeless or housing-insecure, connect with Housing First programs, shelters, or transitional housing. Do not discharge to the street.

2. Follow-up: Outpatient appointment scheduled within 7 days (associated with HR 0.82 for suicide and reduced readmission). Confirm the patient can physically get to the appointment (transportation plan).[48]

3. Medications: Prescriptions filled or in hand. Confirm insurance covers medications. If uninsured, connect with patient assistance programs or 340B pharmacy. Ensure medication list is reconciled and communicated to outpatient provider.

4. Food: If food-insecure, provide food bank referrals, SNAP application assistance, and ensure patient has food for the first 48 hours after discharge.

5. Safety plan: Updated with current triggers, coping strategies, crisis contacts, and lethal means counseling. Shared with the patient and at least one support person.

6. Insurance: If uninsured or coverage is lapsing, initiate Medicaid application or connect with benefits enrollment before discharge.

7. Legal: If criminal legal involvement, coordinate with probation/parole officer. Ensure court dates are known. If reentry from jail/prison, use CTI principles.

8. Communication: Outpatient provider notified of discharge with medication changes, safety plan, and SDOH concerns. Transition record transmitted within 24 hours.

9. Digital access: If telehealth follow-up is planned, confirm patient has device, internet, and comfort with technology. If not, arrange in-person follow-up or phone-based care.

10. Family/support: Family or support person educated about the diagnosis, warning signs, crisis plan, and how to access emergency services. Consider Family Connections or psychoeducation referral.

12. Summary


The evidence is unambiguous: social determinants of health are among the strongest predictors of psychiatric readmission, post-discharge suicide, medication nonadherence, and treatment disengagement, often exceeding the predictive power of clinical variables alone. Housing instability, food insecurity, poverty, racism, insurance barriers, criminal legal involvement, rural isolation, and digital exclusion operate synergistically — the dose-response relationship between cumulative social needs and readmission (aOR 12.55 for 5 domains vs. none) underscores that these are not background factors but central clinical concerns.[14]

Structural competency provides the conceptual framework for understanding these forces, but the evidence base for training outcomes remains limited to learner-level changes — no study has yet linked structural competency education to improved patient outcomes.[65] The most effective interventions — Housing First, Critical Time Intervention, Collaborative Care, ACT — address structural barriers directly, but most show stronger effects on utilization than on psychiatric symptom improvement per se. The critical gap is between screening and service receipt: identifying social needs without ensuring they are resolved is insufficient.[60]

For inpatient psychiatry, the practical imperative is clear: screen every patient for SDOH on admission, incorporate structural barriers into the treatment formulation, address modifiable barriers before discharge, and ensure the transition plan accounts for the structural realities of the patient's life. A discharge plan that ignores housing, food, transportation, and insurance is not a discharge plan — it is a readmission plan.

❌ Do Not | Why | Instead
Admit for NSSI without suicidal intent | Reinforces maladaptive contingencies; disrupts outpatient treatment[1] | Crisis plan review, outpatient intensification, brief ED observation
Start or increase benzodiazepines | Highest suicide risk of any drug class (HR 1.61); high addictive potential[1], [2] | Promethazine, low-dose quetiapine, or hydroxyzine PRN for ≤1 week
Add medications to "treat" BPD affective storms | No drug class consistently effective; drives polypharmacy[3], [4] | Validate distress, teach distress tolerance skills, wait for the storm to pass
Interpret splitting as the patient being "manipulative" | Splitting reflects internal relational templates, not intentional manipulation[3] | Name the dynamic in team formulation; maintain consistent responses
Use no-suicide contracts | No evidence of effectiveness; may feel coercive; patients may conceal ideation[5] | Collaborative safety planning (Stanley-Brown model)
Discharge without scheduled follow-up | Inconsistent treatment histories are associated with completed suicide[1] | Schedule a specific appointment with a specific provider before discharge
Respond to escalation with escalation | Punitive responses (seclusion, restraint, discharge threats) worsen outcomes[6] | Chain analysis, validation, clear contingencies from the behavioral plan
Make unilateral treatment decisions | Inconsistency across providers fuels splitting[3] | All decisions through team consensus; communicate identically
Extend admission without clear goals | Open-ended stays without structure risk regression and dependency[7] | GOLD approach: set discharge date, define goals, review daily
Ignore countertransference | Unprocessed staff reactions drive punitive or avoidant care[8] | Structured team consultation; normalize emotional reactions to BPD patients
Behavior | Staff Response | Rationale
Suicidal statements with intent | 1:1 assessment by MD; safety plan review; consider observation level change | Acute safety
NSSI urges verbalized | Validate distress → offer ice/TIPP skills → 15-min check-in after skill use | Reinforce skill use, not observation escalation
NSSI act on unit | Medical assessment → chain analysis within 4 hours → no change in privileges unless medically indicated | Avoid punitive response; understand function
Verbal aggression toward staff | Brief, calm limit-setting: "I can see you're upset. I want to help, but I need you to lower your voice." Disengage if escalation continues. Debrief in team huddle. | Prevent reinforcement of escalation
Requests to speak only to specific staff | Any available staff member responds. "The whole team is working together on your care." | Prevent splitting
Requests for PRN medications | Offer skill use first. If distress persists after 20 minutes of skill practice, PRN [specific agent, dose] available. Maximum [X] doses per 24 hours. | Skills before pills
Discharge threats / elopement statements | "Your discharge date is [date]. Let's talk about what's making you want to leave now." Do not discharge reactively. | Maintain structure; explore trigger
