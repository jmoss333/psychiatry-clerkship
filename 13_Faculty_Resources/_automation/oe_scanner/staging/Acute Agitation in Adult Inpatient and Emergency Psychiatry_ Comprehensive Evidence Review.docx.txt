



Acute Agitation in Adult Inpatient and Emergency Psychiatry: Comprehensive Evidence Review


Scope and Framing


This review synthesizes current evidence on the pharmacologic and nonpharmacologic management of acute agitation in adult psychiatric emergency and inpatient settings. It integrates Project BETA (Best practices in Evaluation and Treatment of Agitation) consensus statements, ACEP and APA guidance, systematic reviews, network meta-analyses, and comparative medication trials through mid-2026. The review addresses verbal de-escalation, trauma-informed care, pharmacotherapy (including route comparisons and combination strategies), eight special populations, restraint/seclusion reduction, and safety outcomes. It concludes with a risk-stratified PRN algorithm, medication table by scenario, contraindications summary, and a resident bedside checklist.

---

I. Verbal De-Escalation


Project BETA Framework


Project BETA, published in 2012 by the American Association for Emergency Psychiatry (AAEP), established noncoercive de-escalation as the intervention of choice for acute agitation.[1] The De-Escalation Workgroup (Richmond et al., 2012) articulated a 3-step paradigm: (1) verbally engage the patient, (2) establish a collaborative relationship, and (3) verbally de-escalate the patient out of the agitated state.[1] Four overarching objectives guide every encounter: ensure safety, help the patient regain behavioral control, avoid restraint, and avoid coercive interventions that escalate agitation.[1]

The workgroup's 10 Domains of De-Escalation provide a structured framework encompassing: respecting personal space, being non-provocative, establishing verbal contact, being concise, identifying wants and feelings, listening closely, agreeing or agreeing to disagree, setting clear limits, offering choices and optimism, and debriefing the patient and staff.[1] Effective de-escalation should take approximately 5–10 minutes to return an agitated person to a calm state.[2]

APA Guidance and the ERASER Mnemonic


The APA Resource Document on Seclusion or Restraint (2022) designates verbal de-escalation as first-line treatment, effective for a majority of patients in behavioral health crisis within 5 minutes.[3] The APA recommends all staff—including security and observation personnel—be trained in de-escalation, referencing the ERASER mnemonic as a teaching tool.[3] Environmental interventions endorsed by the APA include quiet rooms, sensory rooms, music, warm showers, weighted blankets, aromatherapy, exercise, and 1:1 observation with de-escalation-trained staff.[3]

Effectiveness Evidence


A cluster-randomized controlled trial across all psychiatric hospitals in Slovenia demonstrated that de-escalation training reduced aggressive events by 73% (IRR 0.268, 95% CI 0.221–0.342), severe aggressive events by 86%, and physical restraints by 70% compared to controls.[4] Implementation of Project BETA-aligned initiatives at a large county hospital ED reduced physical assaults on emergency medicine residents from 28% to 11.3% (p = 0.032), though gains eroded when in-person training was disrupted by COVID-19.[5] A systematic review of de-escalation training in health professions education (56 studies) found that curricula incorporating both didactic education and active skills practice (role-play/simulation) produced the strongest evidence of effectiveness.[6]

Critically, two Cochrane systematic reviews found no RCTs meeting inclusion criteria for de-escalation techniques alone, highlighting that the evidence base remains predominantly consensus-driven and observational despite universal guideline endorsement.[7][2]

II. Trauma-Informed Approaches


Trauma-informed care (TIC) applied to agitation management rests on three core recognitions: the prevalence of trauma, how trauma affects all individuals involved, and the need to prevent retraumatization.[8] The SAMHSA model emphasizes five interpersonal principles: safety, trustworthiness, choice, collaboration, and empowerment.[9]

The Six Core Strategies (6CS) framework—developed by the National Association of State Mental Health Program Directors—is the most extensively studied TIC-based restraint reduction model. A federally funded multisite study of 43 inpatient psychiatric facilities found that stabilized 6CS implementation reduced the percentage of patients secluded by 17% (p = .002), seclusion hours by 19% (p = .001), and proportion restrained by 30% (p = .03).[10] The Pennsylvania State Hospital System achieved near-complete cessation of mechanical restraint and seclusion over a 10-year period through continuous 6CS adherence, with all safety measures (assaults, aggression, self-injurious behavior) improved or unchanged.[11] A cluster-RCT in Finnish forensic wards demonstrated that seclusion-restraint-observation days declined from 30% to 15% in intervention wards without any increase in violence (p = .91).[12]

A scoping review of 31 studies found that TIC approaches (most commonly 6CS and the Sanctuary Model) were associated with decreased restraint and seclusion rates, though most evidence remains preliminary.[13]

III. Pharmacotherapy: Comparative Evidence


Antipsychotic–Benzodiazepine Combinations vs. Monotherapy


The largest and most recent evidence comes from the 2026 individual participant data network meta-analysis (IPD-NMA) by Siafis et al. (18 trials, 3,411 participants), published in The Lancet Psychiatry.[14] For moderate agitation, antipsychotic–benzodiazepine combinations were substantially more effective than haloperidol monotherapy for achieving sedation within 15–30 minutes (OR 12.93, 95% CrI 3.00–50.91; RR 1.58), followed by benzodiazepines alone (OR 5.52, 95% CrI 1.37–21.02) and other antipsychotics (OR 4.54, 95% CrI 1.35–14.45).[14] For severe agitation, combinations remained superior (OR 4.86, 95% CrI 1.28–17.54), but results for monotherapy were uncertain.[14] Haloperidol monotherapy was among the least effective options and carried the highest risk of extrapyramidal side effects; benzodiazepines (alone or in combination) increased hypotension risk.[14] Confidence in all estimates was very low.[14]

The Cochrane review on haloperidol for psychosis-induced aggression found that haloperidol alone produced significantly more adverse effects than haloperidol + promethazine (RR 2.01, 95% CI 1.07–3.80), with acute dystonia so common in the haloperidol-alone arm that the trial steering group deemed continuing monotherapy unethical.[15]

Oral vs. Intramuscular Strategies


A landmark quality-improvement study (n = 1,178 prospectively observed patients) from a safety-net ED found no difference in time to adequate sedation between oral and IM medications: median 15 minutes for both routes (median difference 1 min, 95% CI −0.6 to 2.6).[16] Additional sedation rates and adverse drug reaction rates were comparable.[16] This finding supports the Project BETA and APA recommendation to offer oral medications first whenever patients are cooperative.[3][17]

IM Olanzapine + Benzodiazepine: The FDA–ACEP Discordance


The FDA label warns against concomitant IM olanzapine and parenteral benzodiazepines due to potential for excessive sedation and cardiorespiratory depression.[28][29] Postmarketing data identified 29 fatalities among an estimated 539,000 exposures, with 66% involving concomitant benzodiazepines.[30] However, a large retrospective ED study (n = 693) found no difference in intubation rates between olanzapine-only (3.8%) and olanzapine + benzodiazepine (3.5%; difference 0.3%, 95% CI −3.0% to 3.7%).[31] The ACEP clinical policy recommends coadministration for severe agitation, creating a guideline–label discordance that clinicians must navigate.[31]

IV. Special Populations


Older Adults


Nonpharmacologic strategies are first-line per AGS 2023 Beers Criteria guidance.[32] Benzodiazepines should generally be avoided due to cognitive impairment, falls, and delirium risk; a Geriatric ED Guidelines 2.0 systematic review found midazolam had the highest adverse event rate (53%; OR 5.25 vs. haloperidol, 95% CI 2.64–10.45).[33] Quetiapine was the only agent with lower adverse events than haloperidol (OR 0.27, 95% CI 0.08–0.97).[33] All antipsychotics carry an FDA black box warning for increased mortality in dementia-related psychosis.[34] Recommended starting doses: quetiapine 12.5–25 mg PO, olanzapine 2.5–5 mg PO/IM, haloperidol 0.5–1 mg PO/IM.[35]

Pregnancy


No efficacy or safety RCTs exist for acute agitation management in pregnancy.[36] Haloperidol (high-potency antipsychotic) is the most commonly administered agent, based on retrospective data from a psychiatric emergency service.[36][37] Lorazepam is preferred among benzodiazepines due to lack of active metabolites, though 20–40% of neonates exposed in late pregnancy develop withdrawal symptoms.[38] Nonpharmacologic approaches should be attempted first.[37]

Alcohol/Substance Intoxication


Droperidol (5–10 mg IM) or olanzapine (where droperidol is unavailable) are recommended first-line for undifferentiated agitation including intoxication.[39][40] Benzodiazepines are specifically indicated for stimulant intoxication and alcohol/sedative-hypnotic withdrawal.[39][41] For alcohol withdrawal delirium, benzodiazepines should be administered IV to achieve light somnolence; antipsychotics may be used as adjuncts but not monotherapy.[41]

Delirium


Nonpharmacologic multicomponent interventions are the cornerstone.[42][32] No medications are FDA-approved for delirium.[34] When pharmacotherapy is needed for severe agitation, second-generation antipsychotics (quetiapine, olanzapine, risperidone) are preferred over haloperidol for faster onset and fewer EPS.[34] Benzodiazepines should be avoided unless the etiology is alcohol/benzodiazepine withdrawal or end-of-life delirium.[42][43] Trazodone is an alternative with lower EPS and QTc risk.[44]

Autism Spectrum Disorder / Intellectual Developmental Disability


Environmental modification is critical: quiet rooms, dimmable lights, reduced stimulation, clear/concrete communication, and family involvement.[45] Risperidone and aripiprazole are the only FDA-approved medications for irritability/aggression in ASD, with meta-analytic effect sizes of SMD 1.07 and 1.18, respectively.[46][47] Evaluate for organic causes (pain, infections) especially in nonverbal patients.[45] No specific RCTs exist for acute agitation management in ASD/IDD populations in emergency settings.

QTc Prolongation


Ziprasidone and iloperidone carry the highest QTc prolongation risk among antipsychotics; aripiprazole and lurasidone have minimal risk.[48] Benzodiazepines do not prolong QTc and are a reasonable alternative.[49] A clinical decision analysis found quetiapine optimal for delirium patients with baseline QTc ≥450 ms.[50] The Association of Medicine and Psychiatry recommends baseline ECG for patients with risk score ≥2; if QTc >500 ms, medication adjustments should be considered.[51] A secondary analysis of the MIND-USA trial found that neither haloperidol nor ziprasidone was associated with clinically significant QTc changes compared to placebo in ICU patients with normal baseline QTc.[52]

Parkinson Disease


Avoid all dopamine D2-blocking antipsychotics (haloperidol, olanzapine, risperidone, ziprasidone, aripiprazole), which worsen parkinsonism.[53][54] Only three antipsychotics are considered acceptable: pimavanserin (FDA-approved for PD psychosis), clozapine (efficacious but requires REMS), and quetiapine (most commonly prescribed, generally well tolerated).[53][55] For acute agitation, short-acting benzodiazepines (lorazepam, alprazolam) are the mainstay.[56] Optimize motor fluctuation treatment and taper anxiogenic antiparkinsonian drugs in order: anticholinergics → MAO-B inhibitors → amantadine → dopamine agonists → reduce levodopa.[56]

Catatonia


Recognition is critical: catatonia presents in 5–18% of psychiatric inpatients.[57] Lorazepam is both diagnostic and therapeutic—the lorazepam challenge test (1–2 mg IV) has response rates of 66–100%.[57] Scheduled lorazepam (minimum TID, up to 16 mg/day) is first-line treatment.[57][58] ECT is second-line, improving 80–100% of cases including up to 60% of benzodiazepine-refractory cases.[58] NMDA receptor antagonists (amantadine, memantine) are third-line options.[59][58] Antipsychotics may worsen catatonia and should be held; if needed for coexisting psychosis, clozapine is preferred.[59][57]

V. Restraint and Seclusion Reduction


The evidence consistently demonstrates that multimodal, trauma-informed programs can substantially reduce restraint and seclusion without increasing violence or adverse safety outcomes. The Pennsylvania State Hospital System's 10-year experience with 6CS showed physical restraint duration fell by 64% (6.6 to 2.4 minutes, p < .001) while all safety measures improved or were unchanged.[11] The Finnish cluster-RCT confirmed no increase in violence with seclusion-restraint reduction (p = .91 for between-group difference).[12] An ED-based agitation code team achieved a 27.3% proportionate decrease in restraint rates over 5 years.[60]

The CMS Inpatient Psychiatric Facility Quality Reporting program, requiring public reporting of restraint and seclusion measures, reduced restraint duration by 49% and seclusion by 54%.[61] Joint Commission 2024/2025 updated standards (effective January 1, 2025) require continuous in-person observation, regular interval assessments, physician evaluation before order expiration, and face-to-face evaluation within 1 hour of initiation.[62]

VI. Safety Outcomes and Monitoring


Staff Safety


Government-sector mental health workers experience the highest rate of nonfatal workplace violence at 77.1 incidents per 1,000 workers.[8] In a prospective ED study, staff received injuries in 12% of sedation events.[63] The APA 2025 resource document endorses the Assaulted Staff Action Program (ASAP) for post-assault support.[8]

Patient Adverse Events


A prospective Australian study of 904 patients receiving parenteral sedation found 15.9% experienced at least one adverse event, most commonly oxygen desaturation (7.4%), airway obstruction (3.6%), and bradycardia (1.9%). Risk factors included age ≥65 (OR 2.8), multiple sedatives within 60 minutes (OR 2.1), and alcohol intoxication (OR 1.8).[64] In older adults, adverse events occurred in 16.8% of patients, with midazolam posing the highest risk.[33]

Post-Sedation Monitoring Protocol


Expert recommendations for post-sedation monitoring include, at minimum: regular vital signs, level of sedation assessment, and continuous pulse oximetry (without supplemental oxygen); end-tidal CO₂ monitoring when available.[39] Clinical trials typically employed 1:1 nursing with sedation level and vital sign monitoring at 10, 20, 30, 45, and 60 minutes post-dose, along with cardiac rhythm monitoring.[22] The British National Formulary recommends monitoring for a minimum of 4 hours following IM antipsychotic administration.[65]

X. Resident Bedside Checklist for Acute Agitation


□ SAFETY FIRST

- Ensure clear exit path; remove potential weapons from environment

- Activate team response if patient is violent or imminently dangerous

- Maintain ≥2 arm lengths of personal space

□ ASSESS

- Vital signs (HR, BP, SpO₂, temperature, glucose) when safely obtainable

- Rapid etiology screen: psychosis vs. delirium vs. intoxication/withdrawal vs. medical vs. catatonia

- Check medication list for QT-prolonging drugs; review allergies

- Screen for special populations: age ≥65, pregnancy, Parkinson disease, ASD/IDD, known QTc prolongation

□ DE-ESCALATE (Project BETA 10 Domains)

- Respect personal space; use calm, non-provocative tone

- Introduce yourself; establish verbal contact concisely

- Identify wants and feelings; listen actively

- Offer choices ("Would you prefer a pill or a drink?")

- Set clear, enforceable limits

- Allow 5–10 minutes for de-escalation before pharmacotherapy

□ OFFER ORAL MEDICATION FIRST

- Olanzapine 5–10 mg ODT, OR lorazepam 1–2 mg PO, OR haloperidol 5 mg + lorazepam 2 mg PO

- Frame as collaborative: "This medication can help you feel calmer"

- Document patient preference and response

□ IF ORAL REFUSED OR INEFFECTIVE → IM MEDICATION

- Select agent based on etiology and population (see medication table)

- Default: IM olanzapine 10 mg, OR IM haloperidol 5 mg + promethazine 25–50 mg, OR IM droperidol 5–10 mg

- Do not combine IM olanzapine with parenteral benzodiazepine (FDA label)[28]

- Reassess in 15–30 minutes; may repeat dose once

□ POST-MEDICATION MONITORING

- Continuous pulse oximetry (no supplemental O₂ unless SpO₂ <92%)

- Vital signs + sedation level at 10, 20, 30, 45, 60 minutes post-dose[22]

- Assess for: airway compromise, SpO₂ <90%, SBP <90, dystonic reactions, seizures, vomiting

- ECG within 30 minutes of sedation if QTc-prolonging agent used[22]

- Patient should remain recumbent if drowsy; assess orthostatic vitals before ambulation[29]

- Monitor for minimum 4 hours after IM antipsychotic[65]

□ RESTRAINT (LAST RESORT ONLY)

- Document less restrictive interventions attempted and failed

- Physician order required; specify type, duration, and clinical justification

- Face-to-face evaluation within 1 hour of initiation (CMS requirement)[3]

- Maximum order duration: 4 hours for adults (Joint Commission)[62]

- Continuous in-person observation; reassess readiness for discontinuation at each interval[62]

- Pulse, BP, range of motion every 15 minutes; nutrition/hydration/elimination ongoing[68]

□ DEBRIEF

- Debrief with patient after de-escalation (trauma-informed practice)

- Debrief with team; document in chart

- Report any staff or patient injuries per institutional protocol

XI. Evidence Gaps and Emerging Data


Several critical uncertainties persist. No RCTs of verbal de-escalation alone have met Cochrane inclusion criteria, meaning the universal first-line recommendation rests on consensus and observational data.[7][2] The sustainability of restraint reduction programs without external oversight remains a significant concern, as demonstrated by the resumption of high seclusion/restraint use when 6CS adherence was discontinued.[69] The FDA warning against IM olanzapine + parenteral benzodiazepines conflicts with emerging ED safety data and ACEP recommendations, creating unresolved clinical uncertainty.[31] Evidence on equity in agitation management is limited, though some data suggest disproportionate restraint use among Black patients.[70] The role of underlying etiology in treatment selection remains poorly studied in comparative trials.[14] Emerging agents and formulations—including sublingual dexmedetomidine and novel rapid-acting formulations—may expand the therapeutic armamentarium but lack robust agitation-specific trial data.

---

XII. Summary


The totality of evidence supports a stepwise, etiology-informed approach to acute agitation: verbal de-escalation and environmental modification as universal first-line interventions, oral medication offered before intramuscular when feasible, and pharmacotherapy guided by clinical scenario and patient-specific factors. Haloperidol monotherapy should no longer be considered standard practice given its inferior efficacy and high EPS risk.[14][15] Antipsychotic–benzodiazepine combinations appear most effective for rapid tranquilization but carry increased hypotension risk.[14] Trauma-informed, multimodal programs (particularly the Six Core Strategies) can substantially reduce restraint and seclusion without compromising safety.[11][12] Sustained organizational commitment, ongoing training, and external oversight are essential to maintain gains.
