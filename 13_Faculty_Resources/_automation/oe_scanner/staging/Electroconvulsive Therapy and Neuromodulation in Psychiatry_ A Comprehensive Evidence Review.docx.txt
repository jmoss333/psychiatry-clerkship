


Electroconvulsive Therapy and Neuromodulation in Psychiatry: A Comprehensive Evidence Review


Scope and Framing


This review synthesizes the evidence base for ECT and neuromodulation therapies across psychiatric indications, organized for psychiatry residents preparing for clinical practice and board examinations. It covers ECT indications, technique optimization, cognitive effects, continuation/maintenance strategies, TMS, ketamine/esketamine, and emerging modalities. The focus is on landmark trials, meta-analyses, and current guideline recommendations through mid-2026.

---

PART I: ELECTROCONVULSIVE THERAPY


1. ECT for Severe Major Depression


ECT remains the most effective acute treatment for severe depression. The UK ECT Review Group (2003) meta-analysis demonstrated real ECT was significantly superior to sham ECT across 6 trials (standardized effect size −0.91, 95% CI −1.27 to −0.54), translating to a mean Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HDRS) difference of 9.7 points.[1] ECT was also superior to pharmacotherapy across 18 trials (SES −0.80, 95% CI −1.29 to −0.29).[1] The Pagnin et al. (2004) meta-analysis confirmed ECT superiority over sham, placebo, TCAs, and MAOIs.[2]

The Mutz et al. (2019) network meta-analysis — the largest comparative analysis of brain stimulation therapies (113 trials, 6,750 patients) — found bitemporal ECT had the highest odds of response versus sham (OR 8.91, 95% CI 2.57–30.91), followed by high-dose right unilateral ECT (OR 7.27, 95% CI 1.90–27.78).[3] Pooled response rates for ECT in depression range from 60–80%, with remission rates of 50–60%.[4] A large naturalistic study at Mayo Clinic (n=408) reported an 80.4% response rate and 52.7% remission rate.[1]

2. Psychotic Depression


Psychotic depression represents perhaps the strongest indication for ECT. The landmark CORE study (Petrides et al., 2001) treated 253 patients with bilateral ECT and found an overall remission rate of 87%, with psychotic depression achieving 95% remission versus 83% for nonpsychotic depression.[1] The van Diermen et al. (2018) meta-analysis of 34 studies confirmed that psychotic features independently predicted ECT remission (OR 1.47, P=0.001) and response (OR 1.69, P<0.001).[1] In late-life psychotic depression, remission rates of 68.9% versus 51.0% for nonpsychotic depression have been reported (OR 2.10, 95% CI 1.07–4.10).[1]

3. Catatonia


ECT improves 80–100% of catatonia cases, including up to 60% of patients who do not respond to benzodiazepines, according to the APA Resource Document on Catatonia (2025).[4] A systematic review and meta-analysis (Leroy et al., 2018) found a pooled improvement in catatonic symptoms with an SMD of −3.14 (95% CI −3.95 to −2.34).[4] Motor symptoms respond most rapidly, with a mean of 4.2 sessions to response.[4] The NEJM review by Heckers & Walther (2023) noted that both lorazepam and ECT lead to response in 60–100% of patients, with ECT effective after insufficient benzodiazepine response.[5] Bitemporal placement is generally preferred for catatonia, and for malignant catatonia, ECT should be started urgently.[4] Notably, no sham-controlled RCTs exist for ECT in catatonia.[4]

4. Acute Mania


A 50-year review by Mukherjee et al. (1994) found ECT associated with remission or marked improvement in 80% of manic patients.[4] The Popiolek et al. (2022) Swedish registry study (n=571) reported an 84.4% response rate, with response rates reaching 91.5% in the most severely ill patients.[6] A meta-analysis of 12 RCTs (Zhang et al., 2021; n=863) confirmed ECT plus pharmacotherapy outperformed pharmacotherapy alone (SMD −3.50, 95% CI −4.57 to −2.44).[7] ECT is no longer considered first-line for mania in contemporary guidelines but remains highly effective for pharmacotherapy-resistant or severe cases.[8]

5. Schizophrenia


The Cochrane Review (Sinclair et al., 2019) found moderate-certainty evidence that ECT plus antipsychotics yields greater response than antipsychotics alone.[9] The most compelling evidence is in clozapine-resistant schizophrenia (CRS). The landmark Petrides et al. (2015) RCT randomized 39 CRS patients to ECT plus clozapine versus clozapine alone: 50% of the ECT group met response criteria versus 0% in the clozapine-alone group.[10] A meta-analysis of 18 RCTs (Wang et al., 2018; n=1,769) confirmed ECT augmentation of clozapine was superior for response (53.6% vs. 25.4%, NNT=3).[11] However, a recent sham-controlled RCT (Melzer-Ribeiro et al., 2024; n=40) found no significant difference between ECT and sham-ECT in well-defined CRS patients with confirmed clozapine levels ≥350 ng/mL, challenging the open-label evidence.[12] NICE does not recommend ECT for schizophrenia except in catatonia.[9]

6. ECT in Pregnancy


No RCTs exist for ECT in pregnancy. A review of 339 cases (Anderson & Reti, 2009) found at least partial remission in 78% of cases with efficacy data, with 25 fetal/neonatal complications (only 11 likely ECT-related).[13] A 2024 systematic review of 130 pregnant women found most complications were low-to-moderate grade and not life-threatening.[14] The VA/DoD (2022) guideline gives a strong recommendation for ECT in pregnancy as a listed indication.[2] ECT is considered relatively safe during pregnancy with a multidisciplinary team approach, though monitoring for premature contractions is recommended.[15]

7. Geriatric Depression


The PRIDE study (Kellner et al., 2016) is the landmark trial in this population. Phase 1 treated 240 patients ≥60 years with right unilateral ultrabrief pulse ECT at 6× seizure threshold plus venlafaxine, achieving a 61.7% remission rate with a mean of 7.3 treatments.[9] A study of the oldest-old (≥85 years; n=522) found response rates of 81.2% and remission of 53.3% — actually higher than younger counterparts — with fewer adverse events.[16] Older age is an independent predictor of better ECT response (SMD 0.35, P<0.001).[1]

PART II: COGNITIVE ADVERSE EFFECTS


Types and Time Course


The Semkovska & McLoughlin (2010) meta-analysis (84 studies, n=2,981) provides the most comprehensive cognitive mapping. Significant decreases were found in 72% of cognitive variables at 0–3 days post-ECT, with effect sizes ranging from −1.10 to −0.21.[17] By 4–15 days, all but one variable had confidence intervals including zero, and after 15 days, 57% of variables showed improvement beyond baseline (effect sizes 0.35–0.75).[17]

Autobiographical memory is the most persistent concern. A 2025 meta-analysis (9 studies, 432 patients) found ECT patients had significantly larger autobiographical memory loss than controls (SMD = 0.55, 95% CI 0.35–0.75), and results suggested lost memories are not regained at long-term follow-up.[18] Bilateral ECT produced a large effect on autobiographical memory (SMD = 0.82) compared to a small effect with right unilateral ECT (SMD = 0.32).[18]

Risk Factors


The Sackeim et al. (2007) study — the first large-scale prospective naturalistic study of ECT cognitive effects (n=347) — identified key risk factors: bilateral electrode placement, sine wave stimulation (now obsolete), advancing age, lower premorbid intellectual function, and female gender.[20] Importantly, cognitive outcomes varied significantly across treatment sites, demonstrating that ECT technique drives cognitive outcomes.[20] A 10-year study (n=199) found total number of previous ECT sessions had no effect on cognitive performance, and neurofilament light chain (a biomarker for neuroaxonal damage) showed no change during ECT.[21][22]

Counterintuitively, patients with low baseline MMSE (<24) actually showed significant MMSE improvement during ECT, and pretreatment cognitive impairment should not lead clinicians to withhold ECT.[23] The following figure from the CARE Network study shows that younger patients (18–29 years) had the highest rates of cognitive decline (~45%) while older patients (80+) had the highest rates of cognitive improvement (~26%).

Figure 2 Percentage of people in MoCA change categories by age group.

No Cumulative Brain Damage


A Danish registry study (n=168,015) found ECT was not associated with increased risk of incident dementia.[4] Subjective memory complaints correlate more with mood state than objective neuropsychological performance.[25]

PART III: ELECTRODE PLACEMENT AND TECHNIQUE


Right Unilateral vs. Bitemporal


The Sackeim et al. (2000) dose-response RCT established that right unilateral (RUL) ECT requires 5–6× seizure threshold to match bilateral ECT efficacy.[26] The Kolshus et al. (2017) meta-analysis (7 RCTs, n=792) comparing high-dose (>5× ST) RUL versus moderate-dose bitemporal ECT found no difference in efficacy (Hedges's g = −0.03) but significant cognitive advantages for RUL: faster reorientation (mean difference −8.28 min) and less retrograde autobiographical memory loss (Hedges's g = −0.46).[27] The EFFECT-Dep trial (n=138) confirmed noninferiority of twice-weekly high-dose RUL to bitemporal ECT.[28]

Ultrabrief vs. Brief Pulse


Ultrabrief pulse (UBP) ECT offers cognitive advantages but potentially at the cost of reduced efficacy. The Tor et al. (2015) meta-analysis found brief pulse was slightly more efficacious (SMD = 0.25) but caused significantly more cognitive side effects across all domains.[29] However, a 2025 comprehensive meta-analysis (Jelovac & McLoughlin; 30 studies, n=1,478) raised concerns: pooled UBP remission was only 32.2% (95% CI 26.1–39.0%), with 28.1% requiring switching to brief-pulse ECT.[30] The decision between UBP and brief pulse should be individualized based on the relative priority of efficacy versus cognitive sparing.

Bifrontal Placement


The Kellner et al. (2010) three-arm RCT (n=230) found all three placements (bifrontal, bitemporal, RUL) were effective, with remission rates of 61%, 64%, and 55% respectively.[31] The cognitive profile of bifrontal placement is similar to bitemporal.[4]

PART IV: CONTINUATION AND MAINTENANCE ECT


The Relapse Problem


Without continuation treatment, relapse rates after successful ECT are devastating. The Sackeim et al. (2001) placebo arm demonstrated an 84% relapse rate over 24 weeks.[9] Even with continuation pharmacotherapy, a meta-analysis of 32 studies found 51.1% relapse by 12 months.[32]

Landmark Continuation Studies


The Sackeim et al. (2001) RCT established the pharmacotherapy standard: nortriptyline plus lithium reduced relapse to 39% versus 60% with nortriptyline alone and 84% with placebo.[9] The CORE study (Kellner et al., 2006) was the first RCT comparing continuation ECT (C-ECT) to pharmacotherapy: C-ECT and nortriptyline plus lithium were equivalent (37.1% vs. 31.6% relapse).[33] The PRIDE Phase 2 (Kellner et al., 2016) tested combined C-ECT plus pharmacotherapy versus pharmacotherapy alone in elderly patients: the ECT plus medication group had significantly lower HDRS scores at 24 weeks (adjusted mean difference 4.2, 95% CI 1.6–6.9), with >80% sustained response over 6 months.[34] The Nordenskjöld et al. (2013) Swedish RCT extended follow-up to 1 year: 32% relapsed with C-ECT plus pharmacotherapy versus 61% with pharmacotherapy alone (HR 2.32).[35]

The most recent meta-analysis (Jelovac et al., 2025; 4 RCTs, n=254) confirms that C-ECT combined with pharmacotherapy reduces the relative risk of relapse by 43% (RR 0.57, 95% CI 0.37–0.88; NNT=7).[36] A 2026 global Delphi consensus recommended pharmacotherapy with lithium plus an antidepressant for all patients, with continuation ECT via tapering for patients at high relapse risk.[37]

Typical Tapering Schedule


The most commonly used protocol: weekly × 4 weeks → biweekly × 8 weeks → monthly thereafter.[33] The PRIDE study used the STABLE algorithm (Symptom-Titrated, Algorithm-Based Longitudinal ECT): 4 fixed weekly treatments, then additional ECT only as needed based on symptom monitoring.[34] Cognitive safety of C/M-ECT has been confirmed across multiple studies, with no detectable adverse cognitive effects compared to pharmacotherapy alone at 6 months.[38][39]

PART V: TRANSCRANIAL MAGNETIC STIMULATION


Foundational Evidence


The O'Reardon et al. (2007) pivotal trial randomized 301 medication-free patients with TRD to active or sham TMS over the left DLPFC (10 Hz, 120% motor threshold, 5 days/week for 4–6 weeks): response was 24% active versus 15% sham, remission 17% versus 8%.[40] This led to FDA clearance of the NeuroStar system in 2008.[41] Meta-analytic data across 29 RCTs show pooled OR for response of 3.3 (NNT=6) and for remission of 3.3 (NNT=8).[41]

Theta Burst Stimulation


The THREE-D trial (Blumberger et al., Lancet 2018) — the largest brain stimulation trial at the time (n=414) — established that intermittent theta burst stimulation (iTBS, ~3 minutes) was noninferior to standard 10 Hz rTMS (37.5 minutes): response 49% vs. 47%, remission 32% vs. 27% (P=0.0011 for noninferiority).[42] This dramatically reduced treatment time and led to FDA clearance of iTBS. The FOUR-D trial (2022) confirmed noninferiority of bilateral TBS in older adults (≥60 years).[39]

Stanford Neuromodulation Therapy (SNT/SAINT)


The SNT protocol delivers 50 iTBS sessions over 5 days with fcMRI-guided targeting. The open-label pilot (Cole et al., AJP 2020) reported 90.5% remission.[43] The sham-controlled RCT (Cole et al., AJP 2022; n=29) found response of 71.4% versus 13.3% sham, remission 57.1% versus 0% (Cohen d = 1.4).[44] A replication RCT (Kratter et al., World Psychiatry 2026; n=48) confirmed remission of 50.0% versus 20.8% sham (P=0.035).[45] The Taylor et al. (2026) JAMA Psychiatry trial demonstrated connectivity-based targeting yielded response of 80% versus 60% with scalp-based targeting.[46] Durability remains a concern: only 33% of all participants remained in remission at 12 weeks.[47]

The following figure from the Ramos et al. (2025) trial of pragmatic accelerated TBS shows the divergence in HDRS-17 scores between active and sham groups, with effects becoming most apparent after week 2.

Figure 2 Change in 17-Item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HDRS-17) Score in the Randomized, Sham-Controlled Phase of the Study

TMS vs. ECT


ECT remains more efficacious than rTMS: meta-analyses show remission of 52% with ECT versus 33.6% with rTMS (NNT=6 favoring ECT).[49] However, ECT superiority is more apparent in psychotic depression; rTMS may be comparably effective in nonpsychotic depression.[50] TMS requires no anesthesia, has no cognitive side effects, and patients can drive themselves to treatment.[51]

PART VI: KETAMINE AND ESKETAMINE


IV Ketamine


Ketamine (0.5 mg/kg IV over 40 minutes) produces rapid antidepressant effects through NMDA receptor antagonism, glutamate surge, and enhanced neuroplasticity via BDNF/mTORC1 signaling.[52] Meta-analytic data show depression scores significantly reduced versus placebo with SMD 0.68 at 24 hours, with NNT for response of 3 at 1 day to 1 week.[53][54]

The ELEKT-D Trial


The ELEKT-D trial (Anand et al., NEJM 2023; N=403) compared IV ketamine (6 infusions over 3 weeks) to ECT (~9 sessions over 3 weeks) in nonpsychotic TRD. Response was 55.4% with ketamine versus 41.2% with ECT (difference 14.2 percentage points; P<0.001 for noninferiority).[55] Ketamine was noninferior to ECT, with the point estimate favoring ketamine. ECT was associated with significantly greater memory impairment (delayed recall T-score decrease: −9.7 vs. −0.9).[55] However, limitations include the open-label design, predominantly outpatient population (89%), and use of RUL rather than bilateral ECT.[55]

Esketamine (Spravato)


Esketamine nasal spray was FDA-approved in March 2019 for TRD (with an oral antidepressant) and subsequently for MDD with acute suicidal ideation.[57] It is a Schedule III controlled substance available only through a REMS program requiring certified healthcare settings and ≥2-hour post-dose monitoring.[57] Among the TRANSFORM trials, only TRANSFORM-2 met its primary endpoint (MADRS LS mean difference −4.0, P=0.020).[58] A 2025 IPD meta-analysis of 7 RCTs (N=1,505) found esketamine plus antidepressant reduced MADRS scores versus placebo (MD −2.94, 95% CI −5.39 to −0.48), a statistically significant but small effect.[59] A 2025 meta-analysis in the American Journal of Psychiatry characterized the effect size as modest (0.15–0.23), comparable to atypical antipsychotic augmentation.[60]

The SUSTAIN-1 relapse prevention trial showed continued esketamine decreased relapse risk by 51% in remitters and 70% in responders versus placebo.[61] The ESCAPE-TRD trial (Reif et al., NEJM 2023; N=676) demonstrated esketamine plus SSRI/SNRI was superior to quetiapine XR plus SSRI/SNRI for remission at week 8 (27.1% vs. 17.6%, P=0.003).[62] SUSTAIN-3 provided up to 6.6 years of open-label safety data with no new safety signals.[63]

Suicidality


IV ketamine produces rapid anti-suicidal ideation effects (SMD −0.51 at 4 hours), but effects do not persist beyond 72 hours.[65] The ASPIRE trials showed rapid reduction in depressive symptoms with esketamine, but the between-group difference in suicidality severity was not statistically significant.[66] The FDA label states that "the effectiveness of Spravato in preventing suicide or reducing suicidal ideation or behavior has not been established".[67]

PART VII: EMERGING NEUROMODULATION


Vagus Nerve Stimulation (VNS)


FDA-approved for TRD in 2005 (≥4 failed trials). The RECOVER trial (N=493) missed its primary endpoint (percent time in MADRS response) but showed significant benefits on multiple secondary endpoints including quality of life and psychosocial function.[68] A 5-year registry (n=795) showed cumulative response of 67.6% with VNS versus 40.9% with treatment as usual.[69] The VA/DoD issued a "weak against" recommendation for routine use.[19]

Magnetic Seizure Therapy (MST)


The CREST-MST trial (Blumberger et al., Lancet Psychiatry 2026; N=236) — the largest randomized convulsive therapy trial ever conducted — established noninferiority of MST to ultrabrief RUL ECT for remission (22.5% vs. 27.8%), with significantly less autobiographical memory worsening (2.7% vs. 17.3%, P=0.0003).[24] MST remains investigational and not FDA-approved.

The following figure from the Deng et al. (2024) trial shows comparable HDRS-24 trajectories and Kaplan-Meier remission curves for MST and ECT.

Figure 3 24-Item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HDRS-24) Scores and Kaplan-Meier Curves for the Probability of Remission Among the Magnetic Seizure Therapy (MST) and Electroconvulsive Therapy (ECT) Groups

Deep Brain Stimulation (DBS)


DBS remains investigational for depression. The BROADEN trial (SCC target; N=90) found no significant difference versus sham at 6 months (20% vs. 17% response).[71] However, open-label data consistently show ~50% response at 6 months and sustained remission over 2–8 years.[71] Network meta-analysis identified the medial forebrain bundle as associated with the highest response rate (86%).[72] The disconnect between promising open-label and negative sham-controlled results remains a central challenge.

Transcranial Direct Current Stimulation (tDCS)


Not FDA-cleared for any psychiatric indication. The largest multicentre RCT (DepressionDC, N=150) was completely negative (MADRS change −8.2 active vs. −8.0 sham, Cohen d=0.03).[73] The most recent IPD meta-analysis (2026; n=1,246) found a small effect (d=0.24), with effect sizes shrinking in larger trials.[74]

Focused Ultrasound


Entirely investigational. Small pilot studies show promising target engagement (subcallosal cingulate, amygdala) with moderate-to-large pooled effects (Hedges' g=0.88), but no large RCTs exist.[9][75]

RESIDENT REFERRAL CRITERIA: WHEN TO REFER FOR ECT


Urgent/emergent referral:

- Catatonia unresponsive to benzodiazepines (especially malignant catatonia with autonomic instability, hyperthermia, or refusal of oral intake)[4]

- Active suicidality with imminent risk requiring rapid response

- Severe psychotic depression with food/fluid refusal or medical compromise

- Neuroleptic malignant syndrome (overlaps with malignant catatonia)

Strong indications (discuss early in treatment course):

- Psychotic depression (95% remission rate with ECT)[1]

- Severe MDD with failure of ≥2 adequate antidepressant trials

- Severe mania refractory to pharmacotherapy

- Clozapine-resistant schizophrenia (50% response with ECT augmentation)[10]

- Depression in pregnancy when pharmacotherapy is inadequate or contraindicated[2]

- Geriatric depression, especially with psychotic features or medical comorbidity limiting pharmacotherapy[9]

Consider referral:

- Patient preference for ECT

- Prior positive response to ECT

- Need for rapid response (medical comorbidity, severe functional impairment)

- Intolerance of multiple medication trials

When to refer for TMS:

- MDD with failure of 1–2 adequate medication trials (optimal window)[41]

- Patient preference for non-pharmacological treatment

- Medication intolerance or contraindications

- Pregnancy (emerging evidence supports safety)[76]

When to refer for ketamine/esketamine:

- TRD after multiple adequate pharmacotherapy trials[67]

- Acute suicidal ideation requiring rapid symptom reduction (bridge therapy)[65]

- Patients who decline or cannot access ECT

COUNSELING SCRIPT FOR ECT


The following is a framework for informed consent discussions:

What ECT is: "ECT is a medical procedure performed under general anesthesia in which a brief, controlled electrical stimulus is applied to the brain to produce a generalized seizure lasting about 30–60 seconds. The entire procedure takes about 15–20 minutes. You will be asleep and will not feel the seizure."

Why it is recommended: "ECT is the most effective treatment available for severe depression [or the specific indication]. Response rates are approximately 60–80%, which is higher than any medication. For your specific condition [psychotic depression/catatonia/etc.], the response rate is even higher."

Treatment course: "A typical course involves 6–12 treatments given 2–3 times per week. Most patients begin to notice improvement after 3–4 treatments. After the acute course, we will discuss a plan to prevent relapse, which may include medications, ongoing ECT at decreasing frequency, or both."

Side effects: "The most common side effects are headache, muscle aches, and nausea on the day of treatment — these are usually mild and treatable. The most significant concern is memory effects. You may have difficulty remembering new information during the treatment course (this typically resolves within 2–4 weeks after the last treatment) and some difficulty recalling events from around the time of treatment. In some patients, memory for personal events from weeks to months before treatment may be affected, and some of these memories may not fully return. We use techniques — specifically, right-sided electrode placement and ultrabrief pulse width — that significantly reduce memory effects while maintaining effectiveness."

What ECT does not do: "ECT does not cause brain damage. Large studies have shown no association with dementia, and brain biomarkers of injury do not change during ECT. It does not change your personality."

Anesthesia and safety: "You will receive a short-acting anesthetic and a muscle relaxant. The medical risks are primarily those of brief general anesthesia. Serious complications are rare — the mortality rate is approximately 1 in 50,000–100,000 treatments, comparable to minor surgical procedures."
