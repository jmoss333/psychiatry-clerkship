


Child and Adolescent Psychiatry for Adult Psychiatrists: A Comprehensive Evidence Review


Scope and Framing


This review synthesizes the evidence on ten domains of child and adolescent psychiatry most commonly encountered by adult psychiatrists working in emergency departments and inpatient units. It emphasizes adolescents (13–17) while covering the full pediatric range where relevant. The review draws on AACAP practice parameters, AAP clinical reports, systematic reviews, landmark RCTs, and emergency psychiatry consensus statements. It is designed as a resident education and teaching resource.

---

I. Adolescent Psychiatric Boarding


Scale of the Crisis


Pediatric mental health ED visits approximately doubled from 2011 to 2020, rising from 4.8 million (7.7% of all pediatric ED visits) to 7.5 million (13.1%), with suicide-related visits showing the greatest increase (average annual percent change 23.1%).[1] Among youth requiring inpatient psychiatric care, 23–58% experience boarding, with average ED boarding durations of 5–41 hours and inpatient medical unit boarding of 2–3 days.[2] The COVID-19 pandemic dramatically worsened the crisis: prolonged boarding (≥2 midnights) increased by 76.4% in pandemic year 2, with adolescents aged 13–17 disproportionately affected (87.2% increase).[3]

More than 1 in 10 mental health ED visits among Medicaid-enrolled youth lasted >2 days, with striking state-level variation (3–27%).[4] In Massachusetts, 45% of pediatric psychiatric ED visits resulted in boarding ≥12 hours, and nearly half of boarding episodes did not result in inpatient admission.[5] In October 2021, the AAP, AACAP, and Children's Hospital Association jointly declared a national emergency in child and adolescent mental health.[5]

Disparities and Risk Factors


Transgender youth board longer with lower admission rates; Hispanic ethnicity is associated with nearly threefold odds of ED length of stay >12 hours; and youth with autism spectrum disorder have higher baseline boarding rates (18.4% vs. 10.3%) that widened further during the pandemic.[3][5][6] Aggressive behaviors increase boarding duration by 193%, and prior psychiatric admissions by 88%.[7]

Harms


The majority of boarding youth report worsening mental health symptoms, with 72.7% reporting increasing anxiety.[8] Only 14% of pediatric hospitalists report that boarding youth receive medication management, and only 18% report psychotherapy access.[3] Parents frequently liken the environment to incarceration.[3] Disposition is often driven by bed availability rather than clinical need — only 56% of youth determined to need inpatient care actually received it after boarding.[5]

Guidelines and Interventions


The AAP 2023 policy statement recommends trauma-informed ED protocols, low-stimulation safe areas, embedded mental health professionals, telehealth consultation, and lethal means restriction counseling.[9] A national Delphi consensus panel (2023) recommended a 24-hour threshold for transferring boarding patients from the ED to an inpatient floor, that pediatric patients should not be cared for in the same space as adults (87% agreement), and that emergency medicine or hospitalists maintain primary ownership with child psychiatry in a consultative role.[10]

Comprehensive hospital-level interventions at one institution reduced mean boarding by 53% (9.1 to 4.3 days) and increased access to acute psychiatry programs.[7] Standardized ED stabilization protocols significantly reduced psychiatric facility admissions (22.6% vs. 58.2%) and increased safe discharges home.[11]

II. First-Episode Psychosis in Minors


Presentation Differences from Adults


Approximately 100,000 adolescents and young adults in the U.S. experience FEP annually, with peak onset between ages 15–25.[12] Key developmental differences include: perceptual symptoms (hallucinations) are more prominent in younger patients while delusions and thought disorder increase with age; flat affect is more severe in younger patients; visual hallucinations are more common in children; and delusions are less elaborate.[13][14] Auditory hallucinations are common in children (median 17% of 9–12-year-olds) but most do not signify psychotic disorder.[12]

Early-onset patients have significantly longer duration of untreated psychosis (median 91 vs. 58 days in adults), more prodromal symptoms, and higher rates of premorbid neurodevelopmental difficulties.[15][16] DUP is the strongest predictor of functional outcome at 2-year follow-up in pediatric FEP.[17]

Diagnostic Complexity


A thorough differential is essential: psychotic mood disorders, ASD, intellectual disability, substance-induced psychosis, trauma-related presentations, and medical causes (autoimmune encephalitis, seizure disorders, 22q11.2 deletion syndrome, metabolic disorders) must be excluded.[18][19] Notably, 23.5% of children admitted for first psychotic symptoms had substance-/medication-induced or medically-associated psychosis.[20] Daily cannabis use confers 3.2× higher odds of psychotic disorder, and cannabis-induced psychosis has a 36–46% transition rate to schizophrenia spectrum disorders.[12]

AACAP Practice Parameter


The AACAP Practice Parameter (2001, updated 2013) establishes that EOS is diagnosed using the same DSM criteria as adults and recommends antipsychotic medications as first-line, with SGAs generally favored due to lower EPS risk.[18][21] Clozapine is reserved for treatment-resistant cases after failure of at least two adequate antipsychotic trials.[18] First-episode patients should receive maintenance treatment for 1–2 years after the initial episode.[18]

Coordinated Specialty Care


The landmark RAISE-ETP trial (N = 404, ages 15–40) demonstrated that NAVIGATE (a CSC program including individualized medication management, family education, CBT-based individual therapy, and supported employment/education) produced greater improvement in quality of life and psychopathology, with effects more pronounced with shorter DUP.[22] OnTrackNY increased education/employment rates from 40% to 80% and decreased hospitalization rates from 70% to 10%.[23]

III. Adolescent Suicidality


Epidemiology


Suicide is the second leading cause of death for youth aged 10–24, with 59,827 deaths from 2011–2020.[24] The suicide rate among 10–19-year-olds increased 85.3% between 2007 and 2017.[25] During 2020–2021, ED visits for suspected suicide attempts among girls aged 12–17 increased 50.6% compared to 2019.[26] Firearms (51%), suffocation (33.5%), and overdose (5.9%) are the leading methods.[24]

Figure 1 Mean Annual Rates of Emergency Department Visits and Hospitalizations for Suicidal Ideation and Suicide Attempts Among Children and Adolescents Aged 10 to 18 Years, 2016-2021

Screening Tools


The Ask Suicide-Screening Questions (ASQ) is the most validated pediatric suicide screening tool: 97% sensitivity, 88% specificity in the pediatric ED, validated for ages 10–21.[24][28] The C-SSRS provides severity assessment and is recommended by the Joint Commission, with worst-point lifetime suicidal ideation predicting future attempts.[29] The CASSY (computerized adaptive screen) shows similar sensitivity to the ASQ but performs significantly better among patients already presenting with psychiatric symptoms (AUROC 0.72 vs. 0.57).[30]

The USPSTF (2022) found insufficient evidence to recommend for or against universal suicide risk screening in pediatric primary care, creating tension with AAP, AACAP, and Joint Commission recommendations for universal screening.[31]

Safety Planning in Adolescents — A Critical Caveat


A 2025 JAMA Pediatrics meta-analysis (10 studies) — the first dedicated to standalone SPIs in youth — found no association between SPI and reduction in suicidal ideation, suicide-related behaviors, or suicide attempts at 1–6 months.[32] This contrasts sharply with the adult evidence base. Family-based adaptations showed more promising results.[32] MI-enhanced safety planning (MI-SafeCope) for hospitalized adolescents showed significantly higher self-efficacy to refrain from suicidal action and greater likelihood of safety plan use.[33]

Evidence-Based Interventions


DBT-A is the only well-established (Level 1) intervention for reducing self-injurious thoughts and behaviors in youth.[34] The McCauley et al. (2018) multisite RCT (N = 173) found DBT reduced suicide attempts (OR 0.30), NSSI (OR 0.32), and total self-harm (OR 0.33) compared to supportive therapy, though the advantage diminished during 6–12 month follow-up.[35] Long-term follow-up at 12.4 years showed lower odds of suicidal ideation in adulthood (OR 0.16).[36]

The following figure from McCauley et al. shows the trajectory of suicide attempts, NSSI, and self-harm over 12 months, demonstrating DBT's advantage during active treatment:

Figure 2 Changes in Suicide Attempts, Nonsuicidal Self-injury, Self-harm, and Suicidal Ideation

Family-based interventions are classified as Level 2 (probably efficacious) for reducing youth suicidal ideation.[34] The SAFETY program (CBT/DBT-informed family treatment) showed 100% survival without suicide attempt at 3 months vs. 67% for enhanced TAU (NNT = 3).[37]

Means Restriction


For adolescent suicide by firearm, parents owned the firearm 66% of the time for ages 10–14 and 51% for ages 15–19.[38] Only 21.9% of pediatric primary care providers consistently screen for firearms.[39] The AAP, AACAP, and SAMHSA all recommend assessing for access to lethal means and counseling on safe storage.[24]

IV. Family Engagement


Evidence for Family Involvement


Family-based crisis intervention (FBCI) in the ED resulted in significantly more suicidal adolescents discharged home safely with outpatient follow-up compared to TAU, with higher family empowerment and no completed suicides.[40] Any family involvement with inpatient staff was significantly associated with attending outpatient follow-up within 7 days (OR 2.79) and 30 days (OR 3.07) after discharge.[41]

A structured psychoeducational group for adolescents with early-onset psychosis and their families reduced ED visits from 39% to 15% at initial follow-up and from 50% to 13% at 2-year follow-up.[42][43] The Cochrane review of family-based interventions for schizophrenia found reduced days in hospital (MD −2.43 days) and probable reduction in relapse.[44]

AACAP Guidance on Family Assessment


The AACAP Practice Parameter for Assessment of the Family (2007) establishes that a family assessment is always indicated in the psychiatric evaluation of a child or adolescent.[45] The clinician begins by identifying family strengths, then defines the problem while observing family interactions. An individual interview with the adolescent is essential, and the clinician must maintain neutrality.[45]

V. Consent, Assent, and Confidentiality


Consent Framework


Parents provide informed permission for diagnosis and treatment, with the assent of the child whenever developmentally appropriate.[46] The AACAP Practice Parameter on Psychotropic Medication (2009) requires documenting assent of the child and consent of the parents before initiating medication and at important treatment transitions.[46]

Psychiatric Hospitalization


In practically all states, if a physician determines hospitalization is medically indicated and a parent consents, the minor is admitted — even if the minor objects (Parham v. J.R., 1979).[47] About two-thirds of states allow some minors to independently consent to mental health hospitalization, with minimum ages ranging from no minimum to age 16.[47]

Confidentiality


Confidentiality is essential for adolescent care — adolescents are less likely to seek care or disclose sensitive information without it.[48] Key domain-specific rules:

- Substance use: 42 CFR Part 2 provides strong protections; records cannot be shared with parents without the minor's written consent at federally funded facilities[48]

- Sexual/reproductive health: All states allow minor consent for STI services[48]

- Mental health: ~2/3 of states allow some minor consent; ages vary widely[48]

- Mandatory breach: Required for danger to self or others, abuse, or severe disorganization[12][24]

The 21st Century Cures Act creates significant confidentiality risks through mandated easy access to electronic health information, though confidentiality rights themselves have not been altered.[49][50]

VI. Neurodevelopmental Disorders


Disproportionate ED Utilization


Adolescents with ASD have a psychiatric ED visit rate 9.9 times higher than those without ASD or ADHD.[51] The most common presenting complaint is aggression toward others (36.4%), followed by suicidal thoughts and self-injurious behaviors.[52] ASD is an independent risk factor for boarding ≥24 hours (OR 1.82).[53]

Diagnostic Overshadowing


Psychiatric disorders occur at least 3 times more often in children with ID than in typically developing children.[54] Symptoms are frequently assumed to be inherent to the underlying disability, leading to substantial underdiagnosis.[54] Behavioral outbursts in ASD/ID may be driven by co-occurring medical issues causing pain (ear infections, abdominal pain), particularly in nonverbal children.[9]

Suicidality Risk


Suicidality risk is elevated and often overlooked: ASD without ID with comorbid ADHD carries the highest suicide risk (attempt OR 7.25; suicide OR 13.09).[55] Suicidality assessment tools have not been validated specifically for individuals with ID or minimally verbal ASD.[24]

Management Adaptations


ASD-specific de-escalation strategies include clear, concrete, short statements; reduced sensory stimulation; lower-volume tone; refraining from physical contact; and consulting families for calming strategies.[9] Children with ASD are significantly more likely to be physically restrained (RR 2.8), managed in seclusion (RR 3.3), and receive medication for behavioral de-escalation (RR 2.8).[56]

Medication Cautions


Risperidone (≥5 yr) and aripiprazole (≥6 yr) are the only FDA-approved medications for irritability in ASD.[57][58] Methylphenidate is effective for ADHD symptoms in ASD but with lower response rates and higher discontinuation rates due to increased adverse events, including behavioral activation.[58][59] The AACAP Practice Parameter for ID states that children with ID/IDD may be more sensitive to medication side effects, and conservative dosing is recommended.[54]

VII. Eating Disorder Medical Risk


Hospitalization Criteria


The AAP/SAHM criteria use percent median BMI (%mBMI) rather than absolute BMI, because absolute BMI varies with age and sex during growth.[60] Any one of the following justifies hospitalization: ≤75% mBMI, HR <50 bpm daytime, BP <90/45, temperature <96°F, orthostatic HR increase >20 bpm, electrolyte abnormalities, syncope, seizures, or acute food refusal.[60]

Atypical anorexia nervosa (significant weight loss but "normal" weight) now comprises nearly one-third of inpatient eating disorder admissions, with comparably significant medical severity to AN in terms of bradycardia and orthostatic changes.[61][62] Weight alone is a misleading indicator of disease severity.

Refeeding: Paradigm Shift


The landmark STRIPED trial (N = 120, ages 12–24) demonstrated that higher-calorie refeeding (2000 kcal/d, increasing by 200 kcal/d) restored medical stability significantly earlier (HR 1.67), shortened hospital stay by 4.0 days, and saved $19,056 per participant — with no increase in electrolyte abnormalities or adverse events.[61] The APA (2023) now recommends initial calorie prescriptions of 1,500–2,000 kcal/d.[63]

Cardiac Risk


Approximately one-third of deaths in AN are attributed to cardiovascular complications.[64] QTc-prolonging medications and electrolyte abnormalities compound arrhythmic risk.[60] Bupropion is contraindicated in AN and bulimia nervosa due to seizure risk.[63]

Monitoring Protocol


Refeeding monitoring requires serum K, Mg, and phosphorus before initiation and every 12 hours for 3 days in high-risk patients; vital signs every 4 hours for the first 24 hours; daily weights; and thiamine supplementation (2 mg/kg up to 100–200 mg/d in pediatrics) before feeding commences.[65]

VIII. Pediatric Agitation Management


Guideline Framework


Three key guidelines anchor management: the AACAP Practice Parameter (2002) on prevention and management of aggressive behavior, the AAEP/AACAP BETA Consensus Statement (2019) recommending a multimodal, etiology-driven approach, and the AAP Clinical Report (2023) reaffirming behavioral de-escalation as first-line.[66][67][9]

De-Escalation First


All guidelines uniformly position verbal de-escalation and environmental modification as first-line.[67][9] Pediatric-specific techniques include: physically lowering oneself to the child's height, asking questions rather than giving commands, providing simple single-step instructions, offering choices and distraction, and minimizing stimulation.[68][69] For ASD, provide soothing sensory items (fidget toys, noise-canceling headphones).[69]

Pharmacologic Management


No medications are FDA-approved specifically for acute agitation in children/adolescents. The PEAChY-O RCT (N = 348, ages 9–17) found no significant difference between oral olanzapine and diazepam (61% vs. 57% successful sedation at 1 hour), with ~40% failure rate for both.[66][70] IM olanzapine in a 10-year pediatric retrospective study (N = 285) showed 17% required additional sedation within 1 hour, with low adverse event rates.[71]

Restraint Time Limits


Regulatory requirements differ critically from adults: 1 hour for children <9 years, 2 hours for ages 9–17, vs. 4 hours for adults ≥18.[66] Face-to-face evaluation is required within 1 hour of initiation, and reevaluation every 2 hours for patients ≤17.[66] The Joint Commission (2025) eliminated separate "physical holding" standards after finding that 63 of 79 reported restraint fatalities among children/adolescents were from physical holding without mechanical devices.[72]

IX. Trauma-Informed Care


Prevalence


Trauma exposure is extremely common: 61.8% of adolescents report at least one lifetime potentially traumatic event, and among youth in a psychiatric ED, 52% reported at least one type of trauma exposure.[73][74] An estimated 50–80% of psychiatric inpatients have a history of trauma.[75]

SAMHSA Framework


The SAMHSA model incorporates the "4 Rs": Realizing the widespread effect of trauma, Recognizing how trauma affects clients and staff, Responding by applying trauma knowledge into practice, and Resisting re-traumatization.[76] Six key principles guide implementation: safety, transparency, peer support, collaboration, empowerment, and cultural sensitivity.[77]

Retraumatization Prevention


Seclusion and restraint can trigger or escalate PTSD symptoms, particularly in youth with prior abuse histories.[75] Involuntary hospitalization negatively impacts trust in 75% of youth studied, with many reporting unwillingness to disclose suicidal feelings post-discharge.[78] The Six Core Strategies (6CS) approach reduced seclusion by 17%, seclusion hours by 19%, and restraint by 30% across 43 inpatient facilities.[79]

Evidence-Based Interventions


TF-CBT is the most evidence-supported intervention for pediatric PTSD (g = 1.06 vs. passive controls in a 2025 network meta-analysis of 70 RCTs).[80] The Child and Family Traumatic Stress Intervention (CFTSI) is a brief (5–8 session) early post-trauma intervention that significantly reduced PTSD diagnoses at 3-month follow-up.[81] No pharmacological treatment is approved for pediatric PTSD; psychotherapy remains first-line.[82]

X. Psychotropic Prescribing Differences


Antidepressants


Only fluoxetine (≥8 yr) and escitalopram (≥12 yr) are FDA-approved for pediatric MDD.[31][84] The FDA black box warning (2004, expanded 2007) applies to all antidepressants in patients <25 years, based on a pooled risk ratio of 1.95 for suicidal ideation/behavior.[85] However, the NNH for suicidality is approximately 112, compared to an NNT of 10 for depression response, yielding a benefit-to-risk ratio of approximately 11:1.[86] No completed suicides occurred in any pediatric antidepressant RCT.[87] Tricyclic antidepressants are not effective for adolescent depression (unlike in adults) and carry higher lethality risk in overdose.[88]

Antipsychotic Metabolic Risk


Children and adolescents have higher and faster metabolic adverse effects than adults.[89] Weight gain hierarchy from meta-analysis: ziprasidone (−0.04 kg) < aripiprazole (+0.79 kg) < quetiapine (+1.43 kg) < risperidone (+1.76 kg) < olanzapine (+3.45 kg) vs. placebo.[90] The AACAP recommends BMI and waist circumference monthly for the first 3 months, then at 6 months, then yearly, with fasting glucose, lipid panel, and HbA1c at 3 months then yearly.[12]

Benzodiazepines


No benzodiazepines have FDA approval for pediatric anxiety disorders, and RCTs do not support their efficacy in this population.[92][93] SSRIs are first-line for pediatric anxiety; duloxetine is the only FDA-approved medication for pediatric GAD (≥7 yr).[94]

Off-Label Prescribing


Off-label prescribing is pervasive: excluding ADHD medications, 95–96% of pediatric psychotropic prescriptions are off-label.[95][96] The most common reason is age (72.2%), followed by indication (23.3%).[95]

XIV. Family Meeting Pearls


1. Structure the meeting deliberately. Begin by identifying all attendees and their relationship to the patient. Clarify the purpose and expected duration. The AACAP recommends starting by identifying family strengths and resources before defining problems.[45]

2. Interview the adolescent alone first. An individual interview is essential — adolescents may not disclose suicidal ideation, substance use, or abuse in front of parents. Establish what is confidential and what will be shared before the family meeting.[45][49]

3. Maintain neutrality. Do not side with parents against the child or vice versa. Conflict that emerges during the session provides powerful clinical data about family dynamics.[45][112]

4. Address confidentiality explicitly. Explain what information will and will not be shared with parents. Adolescents are more likely to engage when confidentiality boundaries are clear. Breach only for danger to self/others, abuse, or severe disorganization.[48][24]

5. Involve families in safety planning. Most adolescent safety planning studies that showed benefit included caregiver involvement.[24][32] Parents need specific guidance on lethal means restriction — 66% of adolescent firearm suicides involve a parent-owned weapon.[38]

6. Provide structured psychoeducation. Use the meeting to deliver diagnosis-specific education. Structured psychoeducation for early-onset psychosis reduced ED visits from 39% to 15%.[42] Parents with higher self-efficacy are more likely to follow through on discharge recommendations.[113]

7. Plan discharge collaboratively. Family involvement in discharge planning is significantly associated with outpatient follow-up within 7 days (OR 2.79).[41] Confirm follow-up appointments, review the safety plan, and identify the highest-risk period (first week post-discharge).

8. Assess family functioning. Screen for parental mental illness, substance use, domestic violence, and adverse childhood experiences. Family dysfunction is both a risk factor for the child's presentation and a barrier to treatment engagement.[45]

9. Use MI spirit with resistant families. Avoid confrontation with parents who are angry, dismissive, or in denial. Reflect their concerns, validate their distress, and elicit their goals for their child. Parental distress is common but does not predict engagement — self-efficacy does.[113]

10. Document the meeting. Record attendees, key discussion points, agreements reached, safety plan review, discharge plan, and any confidentiality decisions. This protects both the patient and the clinician.

XVI. Algorithm: Matching Intervention to Clinical Scenario




Decision Logic:

1. Is the patient medically stable? → If not, medical stabilization first (eating disorder protocol, intoxication management, medical workup for psychosis)

2. Is the patient safe? → If not, 1:1 observation, safety plan with family, means restriction counseling, de-escalation for agitation

3. Is there a neurodevelopmental disorder? → If yes, rule out pain/medical causes, modify environment, use conservative dosing, consult family

4. Is there a family available? → If yes, involve in safety planning, psychoeducation, and discharge planning; family involvement improves 7-day follow-up (OR 2.79)

5. What is the disposition? → Confirm follow-up within 7 days; review safety plan at discharge; provide 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline number; consider caring contacts

Summary


Adult psychiatrists encountering pediatric patients face a fundamentally different clinical landscape shaped by developmental neurobiology, family systems, legal frameworks, and a narrower evidence base. The most critical differences include: use of %mBMI rather than absolute BMI for eating disorders; parental consent plus child assent for all treatment decisions; shorter restraint time limits and stricter monitoring requirements; higher metabolic vulnerability to antipsychotics with olanzapine explicitly cautioned against as first-line by the FDA; no evidence supporting benzodiazepines for pediatric anxiety; and the finding that standalone safety planning lacks demonstrated efficacy in youth, necessitating family-based adaptations.[32][102][66][92][60] The 2021 joint declaration of a national emergency in child and adolescent mental health underscores the urgency of equipping all psychiatrists — not just child specialists — with the knowledge to safely and effectively care for this population.[5]
