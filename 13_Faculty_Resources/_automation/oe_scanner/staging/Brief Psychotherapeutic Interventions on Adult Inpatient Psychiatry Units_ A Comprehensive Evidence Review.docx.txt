Brief Psychotherapeutic Interventions on Adult Inpatient Psychiatry Units: A Comprehensive Evidence Review


Scope and Framing


This review synthesizes the evidence for brief psychotherapeutic interventions deliverable by psychiatry residents during short (3–7 day) adult inpatient psychiatric admissions. It covers safety planning, brief CBT, behavioral activation, DBT-informed skills, motivational interviewing, problem-solving therapy, supportive psychotherapy, crisis intervention, and psychoeducation. The focus is on interventions with RCT or meta-analytic support, guideline endorsement, and practical feasibility in acute settings. Pediatric and long-stay (>3 month) inpatient data are excluded except where directly informative.

---

I. The Overarching Evidence Base: Does Inpatient Psychotherapy Work?


Multiple meta-analyses converge on a small-to-moderate benefit of adding psychotherapy to standard inpatient care. The Schefft et al. (2019) meta-analysis of 14 RCTs (1,080 patients with MDD) found a pooled effect of SMD 0.24 (95% CI 0.12–0.37; NNT = 7.4) with zero heterogeneity across CBT, behavioral activation, psychodynamic, and interpersonal modalities.[1] Cuijpers et al. (2021) extended this to 32 RCTs in institutional settings, finding g = 0.42 (0.29–0.56), sustained at 9–15 months.[2] The Cohen-Chazani et al. (2022) meta-analysis (37 samples, 4,443 patients across diagnoses) found d = 0.43 for the specific contribution of psychotherapy beyond ward treatment, with no significant effect of therapy orientation — diagnosis moderated outcomes more than modality.[3] The Paterson et al. (2018) meta-analysis confirmed small-to-moderate improvements in depression, anxiety, and readmissions, though blinded RCTs showed smaller effects.[4]

A critical 2026 JAMA Psychiatry viewpoint by Kleiman et al. argues that the field must stop treating inpatient units as "short-term outpatient" and instead design interventions around stand-alone, open-enrollment modules targeting transdiagnostic crisis mechanisms (emotion dysregulation, cognitive inflexibility, behavioral inactivity, life-threatening behaviors), deliverable by non-specialist staff.[5]

II. Safety Planning Intervention (SPI) and Suicide Prevention


The Stanley-Brown Safety Planning Intervention


The strongest evidence for any single brief inpatient intervention comes from safety planning. The Stanley et al. (2018) VA cohort comparison (N = 1,640 across 9 EDs) found SPI with structured follow-up calls (SPI+) was associated with ~45% fewer suicidal behaviors (OR 0.56; 95% CI 0.33–0.95) and more than double the odds of outpatient engagement over 6 months.[6] Critically, the protective effect was not mediated by increased treatment engagement, suggesting SPI has an independent mechanism.[6]

Figure 1 Suicidal Behavior in 6-Month Follow-up for Safety Planning Intervention With Structured Follow-up Telephone Contact (SPI+) and Usual Care

The Nuij et al. (2021) meta-analysis of 6 studies (N = 3,536) confirmed safety planning-type interventions reduce suicidal behavior (RR 0.57; 95% CI 0.41–0.80; NNT = 16), though not suicidal ideation.[7] Safety plan quality matters: among hospitalized veterans, higher whole-plan quality was associated with decreased suicide attempts (AHR 0.79; 95% CI 0.66–0.95), with Step 1 (warning signs) quality most strongly protective (HR 0.48).[8] Alarmingly, 52.5% of at-risk veterans in one sample lacked a documented safety plan.[8]

Crisis Response Planning


Bryan et al. (2017) demonstrated that crisis response planning (an abbreviated 4-step SPI) significantly reduced suicide attempts and improved treatment engagement compared to no-suicide contracts in active-duty military.[9] The 2024 VA/DoD guideline recognizes SPI as standard of care in both VA and DoD settings.[10]

Brief Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Suicide Prevention (BCBT-Inpatient)


The Diefenbach et al. (2024) RCT (N = 200) is the largest and most definitive inpatient suicide prevention psychotherapy trial. Up to 4 sessions of BCBT-Inpatient added to TAU (which already included safety planning and caring contacts) reduced post-discharge suicide attempts by 60% (OR 0.40; 95% CI 0.20–0.80; NNT = 7) and psychiatric readmissions by 71% in patients without SUD over 6 months.[11]

The MSPIRE RCT (Bryan et al., 2025) replicated these findings in military populations, with BCBT reducing cumulative attempt rates to ~6% vs. ~28% for present-centered therapy.[12]

Caring Contacts


The 2024 VA/DoD guideline suggests sending periodic caring communications for 12 months post-hospitalization to reduce suicide attempts (moderate quality evidence).[10] A systematic review found caring contacts may reduce attempts by ~43% at 1-year follow-up, though a single postcard is insufficient — repeated communication for ≥12 months is required.[10][9]

Post-Discharge Risk Window


The post-discharge period carries extraordinary risk: pooled suicide rates are 2,950 per 100,000 person-years in the first week and 2,060 per 100,000 person-years in the first month after psychiatric discharge.[13] Only 35% of patients attend follow-up within 1 week.[14] The Doupnik et al. (2020) JAMA Psychiatry meta-analysis of 14 studies (N = 4,270) found brief suicide prevention interventions in acute care reduced subsequent attempts (pooled OR 0.69) and increased linkage to follow-up (pooled OR 2.74).[15]

III. DBT-Informed Skills Training


Inpatient Evidence


The evidence for DBT in acute inpatient settings is consistently positive but methodologically limited, consisting primarily of retrospective studies and small trials.[16] Bohus et al. (2004) demonstrated that 3-month inpatient DBT for BPD (N = 50) was superior to waitlist on 7 of 9 psychopathological variables, with 42% achieving clinical recovery.[17] Kujovic et al. (2024) found that 8-week and 12-week adapted inpatient DBT produced comparable large effect sizes (BSL-23: d = 1.29 vs. 1.16), suggesting shorter programs may be viable.[18]

Jones et al. (2025) conducted a feasibility RCT of digital DBT (5 online modules over 5–10 days) for acutely suicidal inpatients (N = 42), finding a large effect on suicidality (Cohen's d = −1.0) with 75% adherence.[19] The "Living Through Distress" DBT-informed skills group showed significant reductions in self-harm and increased distress tolerance maintained at 3-month follow-up.[20][21]

The Dismantling Evidence: Skills Training as the Active Ingredient


The landmark Linehan et al. (2015) dismantling RCT (N = 99) compared standard DBT, DBT skills training + case management, and DBT individual therapy + activities group. Conditions including skills training were significantly superior for reducing NSSI (F = 59.1, p < .001) and depression, while all three reduced suicide attempts comparably.[22] This supports skills training as a necessary and potentially sufficient component — directly relevant to inpatient settings where individual DBT therapy is impractical.

Meta-Analytic Evidence


DeCou et al. (2019; 18 controlled trials) found DBT significantly reduced self-directed violence (d = −0.32) and crisis service use (d = −0.38), but the effect on suicidal ideation was not significant.[24] The 2024 VA/DoD guideline concluded there is insufficient evidence to recommend for or against DBT specifically for suicide attempts, reflecting uncertainty about DBT's effect on attempts as distinct from self-harm broadly.[10]

IV. Behavioral Activation (BA)


BA's simplicity and low cognitive demands make it particularly well-suited to acute inpatient settings. Hopko et al. (2003) conducted the first inpatient BA pilot RCT, demonstrating superiority over supportive treatment with a large effect size.[26] Folke et al. (2015) showed that a 5-day, 10-session BA protocol produced marked improvements in 4 of 6 inpatients.[27] Melicherova et al. (2024; N = 375) found group BA and group CBT produced comparable depression reductions (d = 0.83 vs. 1.08) with no significant between-group differences.[28]

The Cochrane review by Uphoff et al. (2020; 15 RCTs, 2,208 participants) found BA superior to TAU for depression (SMD −0.78; 95% CI −1.05 to −0.51) and anxiety (SMD −0.33).[29] A 2024 JAMA review confirmed BA's effect on depression vs. usual care (SMD 0.73; 95% CI 0.52–0.95), comparable to CBT (0.67) and problem-solving therapy (0.64).[30] BA is not significantly different from other active treatments, supporting its use as a pragmatic, easily taught alternative to more complex modalities.[31]

V. Motivational Interviewing (MI)


Inpatient Psychiatric Settings


The Bagøien et al. (2013) RCT is the most directly relevant trial: 135 patients with comorbid substance use admitted to a psychiatric emergency unit received 2 sessions of MI + TAU vs. TAU alone. At 2-year follow-up, the MI group showed a net reduction of 7.3 days of substance use per month (95% CI 1.9–12.6, p < 0.01), suggesting a delayed but substantial benefit from a very brief intervention.[32] Baker et al. (2002) found a single 30–45 minute MI session for psychiatric inpatients with SUD produced modest short-term effects on polydrug use, though effects were not sustained at 12 months.[33]

Treatment Engagement


A meta-analysis by Lawrence et al. (2017) found MI as a pre-treatment intervention significantly increased subsequent treatment attendance, with effects concentrated in non-treatment-seeking populations (OR ≈ 5, no heterogeneity).[34] This supports MI's particular value for engaging ambivalent or involuntary patients encountered on inpatient units.

Figure 2 Forest plot: attendance to treatment [Colour figure can be viewed at wileyonlinelibrary.com]

Medication Adherence


Palacio et al. (2016; 17 RCTs) found MI improved medication adherence (pooled RR 1.17; 95% CI 1.05–1.31).[35] For bipolar disorder, Pakpour et al. (2017; N = 270) found 5 sessions of MI + psychoeducation significantly improved adherence scores.[36] The VA/DoD SUD guideline emphasizes using MI style during all therapeutic encounters as a fundamental engagement strategy.[37]

Cochrane Evidence


The 2023 Cochrane review (93 studies, 22,776 participants) found MI showed small-to-moderate benefit vs. no intervention post-treatment (SMD 0.48), weakening over time, with no clear benefit vs. TAU at most time points.[38]

VI. Problem-Solving Therapy (PST)


The Hatcher et al. (2011) Zelen RCT (N = 1,094) found that among patients with repeat self-harm history, PST significantly reduced re-presentations (13.5% vs. 22.1%, RR = 0.39, NNT = 12).[39] Turgut & Eskin (2025; N = 39) showed both individual and group PST produced large effect sizes for reducing suicide probability and depression.[40] A 2026 meta-analysis found PST demonstrated a 55% reduction in suicide deaths at 6–12 month follow-up — one of only two intervention types to show significant effects on suicide mortality.[41]

The VA/DoD guideline describes PST as "brief, easily taught, and usable by various providers".[10] The 2024 JAMA review confirmed PST's effect on depression (SMD 0.64) is comparable to CBT and BA.[30]

VII. Psychoeducation


The Cochrane review by Xia et al. (2011; 44 RCTs, 5,142 participants, mostly inpatients with schizophrenia) found psychoeducation significantly reduced non-compliance (RR 0.52, NNT = 11), relapse (RR 0.70, NNT = 9), and readmission (RR 0.71, NNT = 5).[42] Barnicot et al. (2020) confirmed psychoeducation as having the strongest evidence base among psychological interventions for acute inpatients with schizophrenia-spectrum disorders.[43] Vickar et al. (2009) found intensive inpatient-initiated psychoeducation reduced 6-month rehospitalization from 56% to 20% (p = 0.038).[44]

Despite this evidence, a survey of psychiatry trainees found 81–84% reported non-uniform psychoeducation content and delivery, with 98% using unstructured conversation and only 25% using supplemental materials.[45]

---

VIII. Supportive Psychotherapy and Common Factors


Direct RCT evidence for manualized supportive psychotherapy in inpatient settings is sparse — it typically serves as the control condition rather than the experimental intervention.[26] The Massachusetts General Hospital model teaches four key brief inpatient psychotherapy techniques: support, affective experience and expression, chain analysis, and identification of relational styles/maladaptive relational patterns.[46] Qualitative evidence highlights that patients value the "human" therapeutic relationship as the foundation for therapeutic work in acute settings, with psychological formulation providing meaning-making and hope.[47] The finding that no single therapy orientation demonstrates superiority in inpatient settings (Cohen-Chazani et al., 2022) suggests common therapeutic factors — alliance, validation, support — account for much of the benefit.[3]

X. Contraindications and Common Pitfalls


Structural pitfalls are the most consequential barriers. Kleiman et al. (2026) identify the fundamental mismatch: outpatient evidence-based treatments assume stable attendance, cumulative learning, and continuity — assumptions that rarely hold in inpatient settings with rapid turnover, frequent cancellations, and diagnostic heterogeneity.[5] Even the briefest manualized treatments often exceed typical lengths of stay.[5]

Clinical contraindications and cautions:

- Acute psychosis with disorganization: Insight-oriented or cognitively demanding interventions (CBT restructuring, PST) are poorly tolerated; prioritize supportive contact, simple behavioral strategies, and psychoeducation[55]

- Severe intoxication or withdrawal: Defer MI and structured interventions until the patient is alert and oriented; SAMHSA recommends providing brief interventions "as early as possible during hospitalization when the patient is alert and oriented"[54]

- Involuntary admission and coercion: The therapeutic alliance is uniquely challenged; Bolsinger et al. (2019) found that coercive admission circumstances, lack of autonomy, and environmental factors (noise, lack of privacy) significantly impair relationship-building. MI spirit (autonomy support, collaboration) is particularly important here[56][34]

- Trauma exposure: Avoid detailed trauma processing during acute crisis; focus on stabilization, grounding, and safety[57]

- Substance use disorder comorbidity: Moderates treatment effects — BCBT-Inpatient's readmission benefit was attenuated in patients with SUD; bipolar disorder patients did not benefit from SPI+ in the Stanley et al. study[11][6]

- Safety plan quality: Mandating safety plans without ensuring quality is insufficient — poor-quality plans may provide false reassurance[8][58]

- No-suicide contracts: Should be abandoned — they have no evidence of efficacy and crisis response planning is significantly superior[9]

Resident-specific pitfalls:

- Attempting multi-session manualized protocols that exceed the patient's length of stay

- Providing unstructured psychoeducation without standardized content[45]

- Neglecting to document and communicate psychotherapeutic interventions to the outpatient team

- Failing to match intervention intensity to patient acuity and cognitive capacity

XIII. Top 10 Bedside Psychotherapy Skills for Inpatient Psychiatry


1. Safety Planning (Stanley-Brown 6-Step Model)

The single most evidence-based brief intervention. Complete all 6 steps: warning signs, internal coping strategies, social contacts for distraction, people to contact for help, professionals/agencies to contact, making the environment safe. Personalize each step; quality predicts outcomes.[6][8]

2. Validation and Therapeutic Alliance

The foundation for all other interventions. Reflect the patient's emotional experience without judgment. In acute settings marked by coercion and loss of autonomy, the "human" relationship is what patients value most.[47][56] Use Linehan's 6 levels of validation: being present, accurate reflection, articulating the unverbalized, validating in terms of past history, validating in terms of present context, radical genuineness.

3. Chain Analysis

Map the behavioral sequence from vulnerability factors through prompting event, thoughts, emotions, urges, behavior, and consequences. Identifies intervention points and builds patient insight. Core to both DBT and the MGH inpatient psychotherapy model.[46][22]

4. Behavioral Activation Scheduling

Collaboratively schedule one concrete pleasant or mastery activity. Start small — even getting out of bed, showering, or walking the hallway counts. Track mood before and after. BA's simplicity and low cognitive demands make it ideal for acutely ill patients.[26][29]

5. Distress Tolerance Skills (TIPP)

Teach one concrete skill: Temperature (ice water on face), Intense exercise (wall push-ups, pacing), Paced breathing (exhale longer than inhale), Progressive/Paired muscle relaxation. These produce immediate physiological down-regulation and are the most immediately applicable DBT skills.[22][20]

6. Motivational Interviewing Spirit

Use OARS (Open questions, Affirmations, Reflections, Summaries) in every encounter. Elicit change talk with the "readiness ruler." Particularly valuable for medication ambivalence, substance use, and treatment engagement in non-treatment-seeking patients.[34][32]

7. Psychoeducation Delivery

Provide structured, diagnosis-specific information about the illness, medications, warning signs of relapse, and when to seek help. Use standardized materials when available. The strongest evidence base of any inpatient psychological intervention for psychosis.[42][43]

8. Problem-Solving in 4 Steps

Define the problem concretely → brainstorm solutions without judgment → evaluate pros/cons → select and plan implementation of one solution. Effective for patients with concrete stressors (housing, relationships, finances) driving the crisis.[39][10]

9. Collaborative Formulation

Develop a brief, shared understanding of why the crisis happened now — linking history, vulnerabilities, triggers, and maintaining factors. This provides meaning-making and hope, and improves staff understanding of the patient's presentation.[47][55]

10. Discharge Preparation and Bridging

Review the safety plan, confirm follow-up appointments, identify the highest-risk period (first week post-discharge), rehearse coping strategies for anticipated stressors, and ensure the patient has crisis line numbers. This is the intervention with the highest potential impact given the extreme post-discharge risk window.[13][15]

XIV. Suggested Resident Teaching Handout


"BRIEF THERAPY ON THE INPATIENT UNIT: A Pocket Guide for Psychiatry Residents"


Core Principle: Every patient encounter is a therapeutic opportunity. Even 5-minute interactions can be structured to deliver evidence-based interventions. The goal is not to replicate outpatient therapy but to target crisis mechanisms, build coping skills, and bridge to post-discharge care.[5]

The 4 Transdiagnostic Targets (Kleiman et al., 2026):[5]

1. Emotion dysregulation → Distress tolerance skills, validation

2. Cognitive inflexibility → Collaborative formulation, psychoeducation

3. Behavioral inactivity → Behavioral activation scheduling

4. Life-threatening behaviors → Safety planning, chain analysis, means restriction

Before Every Encounter, Ask Yourself:

- What is this patient's primary crisis mechanism?

- What is one skill or insight this patient can leave with today?

- What is the discharge plan, and how does today's work support it?

The "3-3-3" Framework for Each Admission:

- 3 assessments: Suicide risk (C-SSRS), substance use (AUDIT/DAST), readiness for change

- 3 interventions: Safety plan, one skills-based intervention matched to diagnosis, psychoeducation

- 3 discharge tasks: Confirm follow-up within 7 days, review safety plan, provide crisis resources

What NOT to Do:

- ✗ No-suicide contracts (no evidence; use safety planning instead)[9]

- ✗ Detailed trauma processing during acute crisis (stabilize first)

- ✗ Unstructured "how are you feeling?" without a therapeutic frame

- ✗ Attempting a full course of manualized therapy in 3 days

- ✗ Assuming psychoeducation happened if it wasn't documented

XV. Algorithm: Matching Intervention to Clinical Scenario




Decision Logic:

1. All patients → Safety plan + psychoeducation + discharge bridging (non-negotiable minimum)

2. Suicidal? → Add BCBT-Inpatient session if ≥4 days of stay; if shorter, prioritize high-quality SPI

3. Self-harm/BPD? → Chain analysis + distress tolerance skill + DBT skills group

4. Depressed? → Behavioral activation scheduling (lowest cognitive demand, highest feasibility)

5. Substance use? → MI session; SBIRT if not yet screened

6. Psychotic? → Psychoeducation first; brief CBTp formulation if patient is engaged and able

7. Disengaged/involuntary? → Lead with MI spirit and validation; avoid confrontation

Summary


The evidence supports a small-to-moderate benefit (d = 0.24–0.43) of adding psychotherapy to standard inpatient care, with no single modality demonstrating superiority — common therapeutic factors likely account for much of the benefit.[3][1] The strongest evidence for specific brief interventions comes from safety planning (RR 0.57 for suicidal behavior),[7] BCBT-Inpatient (60% reduction in post-discharge attempts),[11] psychoeducation for psychosis (NNT = 5 for readmission), and[42] behavioral activation for depression (SMD −0.78 vs. TAU).[29] DBT skills training appears to be the active ingredient of DBT for reducing NSSI, and MI is most effective for engaging non-treatment-seeking patients.[22][34] The critical gap is that most evidence comes from studies with longer stays than typical US admissions, and the field needs interventions designed specifically for the inpatient context — stand-alone, modular, transdiagnostic, and deliverable by trainees.[5] Every patient encounter, however brief, can be structured to deliver an evidence-based micro-intervention.

Time | Intervention | Technique | Evidence Base
5 min | Validation + agenda-setting | Reflect emotion, name the experience, set collaborative goal for the encounter | Common factors; alliance predicts outcomes[1], [2]
5 min | Brief MI "readiness ruler" | "On a scale of 0–10, how important is it to you to [take medication/reduce drinking]? Why not a lower number?" | MI meta-analysis; effective for engagement[3], [4]
5 min | Distress tolerance coaching | Teach one TIPP skill (Temperature, Intense exercise, Paced breathing, Progressive relaxation) | DBT skills evidence[5], [6]
5 min | Behavioral activation micro-dose | Identify one pleasant or mastery activity for today; schedule it | BA Cochrane review[7], [8]
10 min | Safety plan review/update | Review and refine existing safety plan steps; ensure warning signs and coping strategies are personalized | SPI evidence; quality predicts outcomes[9], [10]
10 min | Chain analysis | Map the sequence: vulnerability → prompting event → thoughts → emotions → behavior → consequences | MGH model[11]; DBT evidence[5]
10 min | Psychoeducation module | Structured teaching on diagnosis, medications, or relapse warning signs using standardized materials | Cochrane: NNT = 5 for readmission[12]
10 min | Problem-solving micro-session | Define one problem → brainstorm 3 solutions → evaluate pros/cons → select one → plan first step | PST evidence; NNT = 12 for repeat self-harm[13], [14]
20 min | Full safety planning intervention | Complete 6-step Stanley-Brown SPI with lethal means counseling | ~45% reduction in suicidal behavior[9], [15]
20 min | MI session for substance use | Explore ambivalence, develop discrepancy, support self-efficacy, elicit change talk | Bagøien: 7.3 fewer SU days/month at 2 years[16]
20 min | BCBT-Inpatient session | Narrative assessment of crisis, crisis response plan, reasons for living, hope kit, coping cards | 60% reduction in post-discharge attempts[17]
20 min | DBT skills group module | Single-topic open-enrollment group: one distress tolerance or emotion regulation skill with practice | Linehan dismantling; skills = active ingredient[5]
20 min | Supportive formulation session | Collaborative psychological formulation linking history, triggers, and current crisis; instill hope | Qualitative evidence; meaning-making valued by patients[18]
