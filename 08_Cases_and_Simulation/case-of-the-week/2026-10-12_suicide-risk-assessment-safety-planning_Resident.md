# Case of the Week — Acute-on-Chronic Risk After an Interpersonal Crisis

## Suicide Risk Assessment & Safety Planning: Resident Version

*Psychiatry Clerkship Library — Case of the Week · 2026-10-12 · De-identified synthetic teaching case (no real patient details). Based on articles retrieved from PubMed; DOI links in the reference list.*

---

## Stem (learner-facing)

**Part 1.** You are the on-call PGY-2 called to the ED at 02:00 for a 29-year-old woman brought in by EMS after her roommate called emergency services. Earlier tonight, after a breakup announced by text, she sent the roommate a message saying she "didn't want to wake up tomorrow." On arrival she was tearful and intoxicated (BAL 180 mg/dL). History includes one prior suicide attempt at age 24 during a similar interpersonal rupture (briefly medically admitted, left against advice before psychiatric follow-up), chronic difficulty with emotion regulation and abandonment sensitivity, and intermittent outpatient therapy with poor continuity. No current psychiatric medications.

At 02:00 she is sleeping. The ED attending asks: "Can you just see her now so we can disposition her?"

**Part 2.** At 07:30, clinically sober, she is embarrassed and minimizing: "It was a dramatic text. I was drunk. I'm not going to do anything." On structured interview she endorses recurrent passive death wishes for years ("baseline"), with active ideation last night that has now receded. She denies current intent. She describes chronic emptiness, mood that shifts hours-to-hours with interpersonal events, and a pattern of crisis-driven ED contacts without sustained outpatient treatment. She lives with the roommate, who is supportive but exhausted. She takes no steps to leave and asks, "Can I just go home and sleep?"

---

## Guided discussion

**Q1. The ED wants a disposition at 02:00 while she is intoxicated and asleep. What do you tell them, and why?**
→ *Teaching point:* A suicide risk assessment performed during intoxication is neither valid nor defensible: intoxication acutely elevates risk (disinhibition, affective constriction), obscures mental status, and frequently reverses with sobriety — in either direction. The correct move is interval safety (observation, belongings secured, no means at bedside) and reassessment when sober, with collateral gathered in the meantime (roommate, records from the prior attempt). Document the plan and the rationale, not just "will reassess" [1].

**Q2. Formulate her risk using an acute-on-chronic framework. Why is "she denies intent this morning" insufficient as a formulation?**
→ *Teaching point:* Distinguish **chronic (baseline) risk** — prior attempt (the single strongest historical predictor of eventual suicide death), chronic passive ideation, borderline-pattern emotion dysregulation, treatment discontinuity, alcohol misuse — from **acute (state) risk drivers** — interpersonal loss, intoxicated active ideation with a communicated death wish, sleep disruption. The formulation is a narrative synthesis ("chronically elevated baseline, acutely elevated last night, now returning toward baseline as the precipitant is metabolized and sobriety returns"), tied to modifiable drivers. Flat predictions fail: 50 years of risk-factor research shows prediction of suicidal behavior remains near chance for individual patients, which is precisely why formulation-plus-mitigation beats risk stratification scores used alone [2]. Structured instruments (C-SSRS) standardize *elicitation* of ideation, intent, plan, and behavior — they do not output a disposition [3].

**Q3. How does a borderline personality–pattern presentation change the risk calculus — in both directions?**
→ *Teaching point:* Chronic suicidality in BPD does not immunize against death: lifetime suicide mortality in BPD is substantial, and prior attempts plus alcohol use compound it. At the same time, reflexive hospitalization for every crisis can reinforce crisis-contingent care, disrupt outpatient treatment, and carry iatrogenic harm; many experts favor brief crisis stabilization with rapid return to (or initiation of) structured outpatient treatment such as DBT, reserving admission for acute-on-chronic escalations with new intent, psychosis, or failed outpatient stabilization. The discipline is to treat *this episode's* acute drivers while building the longitudinal structure that actually modifies chronic risk. Avoid both anchoring errors: "she always says this" and "any ideation = admit."

**Q4. Walk through a Stanley–Brown safety planning intervention with her, step by step. What makes it effective, and what is it *not*?**
→ *Teaching point:* The SPI is a collaborative, personalized, written hierarchy: (1) warning signs (for her: rejection event, urge to send "goodbye" texts, drinking alone); (2) internal coping (distress-tolerance skills); (3) social contacts/settings as distraction; (4) specific people she will tell (roommate, named); (5) professionals and crisis services (therapist, crisis line — see the contacts below, ED); (6) means safety (below). Effectiveness evidence: in a multisite ED cohort, SPI plus structured telephone follow-up roughly halved suicidal behavior at 6 months (3.03% vs 5.29%) and doubled outpatient engagement vs usual care [4]; meta-analysis of safety-planning-type interventions shows reduced suicidal behavior (though not reduced ideation) [5]; systematic review supports feasibility and acceptability across settings [6]. It is **not** a no-suicide contract (no evidence of benefit, potential to impede disclosure), and a plan written *at* the patient rather than *with* her is theater. Quality marker: she can tell you her own warning signs and first two steps without reading the sheet.

**Q5. Conduct lethal means counseling here. What do you ask, and how do you handle ambivalence or a firearm you can't verify?**
→ *Teaching point:* Ask specifically about access: medications stockpiled at home (including OTC analgesics), alcohol supply, and firearms — hers or anyone's in the home. Counseling is collaborative harm-reduction: temporary off-site storage or trusted-person custody of medications beyond a small supply, locking and separating anything that remains, and enlisting the roommate in the plan. Means restriction exploits the brevity of most suicidal crises and method substitution being incomplete; clinician discomfort and skill gaps — not patient refusal — are the main barriers to these conversations happening at all [7]. For firearms specifically, framing around safety and temporary storage (not ownership politics) preserves the alliance; firearm access converts low-planning crises into fatal ones at far higher rates than other means [8]. Document what was asked, what access exists, the agreed storage plan, and who verified it.

**Q6. She is discharged with a safety plan. Design the post-discharge bridge. What does the evidence support?**
→ *Teaching point:* The highest-risk period is the days to weeks after ED/hospital discharge, and the first follow-up appointment is where plans die. Evidence-supported elements: structured telephone follow-up contacts tied to the safety plan [4]; brief single-encounter interventions with active linkage — pooled OR 0.69 for subsequent attempts and OR 3.04 for attending follow-up [9]; caring contacts as a low-cost adjunct; a concrete, dated intake (ideally ≤72 h) with a named clinician rather than a referral list; and explicit re-engagement protocols for missed appointments (call, reassess, outreach to the identified support person within the bounds of consent). For her, add warm handoff to a DBT-informed program and address alcohol use directly (it is an independent, modifiable driver).

**Q7. Her roommate asks, "What do I do if she texts me something like that again?" What can you disclose, and how do you use collateral without violating confidentiality?**
→ *Teaching point:* Receiving information is always permitted. Disclosing requires consent — obtain it explicitly while building the safety plan (name the roommate in steps 3–4) — but in an emergency involving imminent risk, limited disclosure necessary to protect life is ethically and legally defensible in most US jurisdictions. Coach the roommate concretely: take statements seriously, ask directly, stay with her, contact the crisis line together (see the contacts below), and call emergency services only if there is immediate danger. Document the consent conversation.

**Q8 (documentation pearl). Write the one-paragraph risk formulation for the chart. What must it contain to be clinically and medicolegally sound?**
→ *Teaching point:* (a) Chronic risk factors and acute drivers, named; (b) protective factors, honestly weighed; (c) what was done to modify risk (sober reassessment, SPI, means counseling with named storage plan, linkage with dated appointment); (d) the reasoning connecting formulation to disposition ("acute drivers mitigated; risk approaching chronic baseline, best managed by outpatient structure"); (e) foreseeable-change contingencies. "Denies SI, no plan or intent, contracts for safety" is the exemplar of what *not* to write [1,2].

## Differential (ranked)

1. **Borderline personality disorder with acute suicidal crisis** (DSM-5-TR criterion 5 behavior) precipitated by interpersonal rejection — pattern of affective instability, abandonment sensitivity, chronic emptiness, recurrent crises.
2. **Alcohol use disorder** — binge-pattern use entangled with crises; independently elevates both attempt and death risk; may meet criteria in its own right.
3. **Major depressive episode, superimposed** — screen carefully once sober; chronic dysphoria in BPD can mask or coexist with MDD (changes pharmacotherapy calculus).
4. **PTSD / complex trauma presentation** — frequent comorbidity; interpersonal triggers and dissociative features overlap with BPD phenomenology.
5. **Bipolar II disorder** — mood lability here is hours-long and interpersonally cued (against sustained hypomanic episodes), but screen before any antidepressant.
6. **Substance-induced depressive disorder** — if depressive symptoms track exclusively with alcohol use.

## Workup & management

- **Workup:** sober structured interview (C-SSRS); collateral (roommate; records from age-24 attempt); BAL trend, UDS, CBC, CMP, LFTs; pregnancy test (treatment planning); AUDIT; screen for MDD, PTSD, hypomania once sober.
- **Acute management:** interval observation until sober; environment safety in the ED; sober reassessment; collaborative SPI; lethal means counseling including medication supply limits and roommate-verified storage; alcohol brief intervention.
- **Disposition:** discharge home with roommate (consented, briefed) is defensible given receding acute drivers, engagement with planning, secured means, and a dated bridge appointment — admission (voluntary first) if new intent, inability to engage, failed means securing, or absent support. Document the formulation per Q8.
- **Longitudinal:** DBT referral (strongest evidence for recurrent suicidality in BPD); treat AUD (naltrexone discussion, referral); avoid reflexive polypharmacy; medication only for bona fide comorbid syndromes; schedule follow-up contact after the first outpatient visit to confirm engagement.

## Facilitator notes (not for learner handout)

- **Timing:** 30 minutes. Run Q1 as a rapid-fire page simulation; slow down at Q2 (formulation) and Q4 (have residents actually role-play two SPI steps in pairs).
- **Common misconceptions:** chronic suicidality means admission is never indicated (false — acute-on-chronic escalation changes the calculus); risk scales can select disposition (prediction near chance at the individual level [2]); safety plan = handout (it is a co-constructed intervention with dose-response in quality); no-suicide contracts have any protective value (none).
- **Discussion spice (evidence tensions):** SPI trials are largely cohort or cluster designs, not individually randomized RCTs — have residents critique the Stanley 2018 design (nonrandomized comparison of VA EDs) [4]; meta-analytic effect on *behavior* without effect on *ideation* [5] is a nice springboard for "what are we actually treating?"; Franklin's near-chance prediction finding [2] vs institutional demand for stratification tools.
- **Safety framing:** keep all discussion method-nonspecific (counting pills, naming calibers, or rehearsing methods adds nothing pedagogically); model direct, nonjudgmental elicitation. If a learner discloses personal distress, pause and use your program's support pathway.
