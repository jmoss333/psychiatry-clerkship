# Case of the Week — Acute-on-Chronic Risk After an Interpersonal Crisis

## Suicide Risk Assessment & Safety Planning: Resident Version

*Psychiatry Clerkship Library — Case of the Week · 2026-10-12 · De-identified synthetic teaching case (no real patient details). Based on articles retrieved from PubMed; DOI links in the reference list.*

---

## Stem (learner-facing)

**Part 1.** You are the on-call PGY-2 called to the ED at 02:00 for a 29-year-old woman brought in by EMS after her roommate called emergency services. Earlier tonight, after a breakup announced by text, she sent the roommate a message saying she "didn't want to wake up tomorrow." On arrival she was tearful and intoxicated (BAL 180 mg/dL). History includes one prior suicide attempt at age 24 during a similar interpersonal rupture (briefly medically admitted, left against advice before psychiatric follow-up), chronic difficulty with emotion regulation and abandonment sensitivity, and intermittent outpatient therapy with poor continuity. No current psychiatric medications.

At 02:00 she is sleeping. The ED attending asks: "Can you just see her now so we can disposition her?"

**Part 2.** At 07:30, clinically sober, she is embarrassed and minimizing: "It was a dramatic text. I was drunk. I'm not going to do anything." On structured interview she endorses recurrent passive death wishes for years ("baseline"), with active ideation last night that has now receded. She denies current intent. She describes chronic emptiness, mood that shifts hours-to-hours with interpersonal events, and a pattern of crisis-driven ED contacts without sustained outpatient treatment. She lives with the roommate, who is supportive but exhausted. She takes no steps to leave and asks, "Can I just go home and sleep?"

---

## Guided discussion

**Q1. The ED wants a disposition at 02:00 while she is intoxicated and asleep. What do you tell them, and why?**
→ *Teaching point:* Assess immediate safety and medical needs now, including impaired consciousness and possible co-ingestion. Intoxication can limit a meaningful interview, so maintain a safe observation setting, gather collateral and reassess when she can participate; do not use a fixed blood alcohol threshold or sobriety alone to authorize discharge [1]. Document what can be assessed now, the remaining uncertainty, the observation plan and who will reassess.

**Q2. Formulate her risk using an acute-on-chronic framework. Why is "she denies intent this morning" insufficient as a formulation?**
→ *Teaching point:* Distinguish **chronic (baseline) risk** — prior attempt, chronic passive ideation, possible longstanding emotion dysregulation, treatment discontinuity and alcohol-associated crises — from **acute (state) risk drivers** — interpersonal loss, intoxication and reported active ideation last night. Her denial of current intent is one finding; whether acute risk has subsided remains to be assessed using the history, collateral, access to means and feasibility of supports. Franklin et al. found that longitudinal risk-factor prediction was only slightly better than chance, with substantial methodological limits and an average study duration of nearly ten years [2]; that is not a direct test of this morning's assessment or proof that one formulation method outperforms another. Posner et al. found C-SSRS ideation and behavior subscales sensitive to change over time [3]. Use structured elicitation to inform clinical judgment, not a score to select disposition [1].

**Q3. How does a borderline personality–pattern presentation change the risk calculus — in both directions?**
→ *Teaching point:* A personality-disorder formulation is provisional until the longitudinal history, pervasiveness, impairment and alternatives have been assessed. Neither a suspected BPD diagnosis nor the label "baseline suicidality" establishes that the present crisis is safe. Assess this episode and choose the least restrictive setting that can safely address it with the supervising clinician [1]. Discuss continuity of structured psychotherapy, including whether a DBT-informed service fits her assessed needs, without claiming that this stem establishes a diagnosis or that admission is necessarily harmful. Avoid both anchoring errors: "she always says this" and "any ideation = admit."

**Q4. Walk through a Stanley–Brown safety planning intervention with her, step by step. What makes it effective, and what is it *not*?**
→ *Teaching point:* Collaboratively rehearse recognizing a worsening crisis, coping and help-seeking, involving available supporters and professional services, and reducing access to lethal means. Elicit her own examples; do not treat suggested warning signs or a roommate's availability as established facts. Use the [official Stanley–Brown resource](https://suicidesafetyplan.com/) rather than reproducing its form. In the nonrandomized VA ED cohort, SPI plus telephone follow-up was associated with lower odds of suicidal behavior (OR 0.56) and higher odds of attending outpatient care (OR 2.06) over six months. Participants had already been assessed as not requiring inpatient hospitalization [4]. Nuij et al. pooled six studies and found lower suicidal behavior (RR 0.570), but no significant effect on ideation [5]. Ferguson et al. reported feasibility and acceptability findings, while heterogeneous designs and combined interventions limited attribution to SPI alone [6]. These findings support a collaborative intervention, not a contract guaranteeing safety or evidence that this patient is ready for discharge.

**Q5. Conduct lethal means counseling here. What do you ask, and how do you handle ambivalence or a firearm you can't verify?**
→ *Teaching point:* Ask about access to medications, firearms and other lethal means, including access through other household members. Agree on feasible steps that actually reduce access, involve a willing support person with consent, and verify the arrangement before relying on it in a discharge decision [1]. Firearm storage arrangements must be lawful; a locked container is not protective if the patient retains access. Siry et al. interviewed clinicians about the Lock to Live decision aid and identified implementation issues involving perceived utility, time constraints and workflow; this qualitative study did not measure a reduction in suicide deaths [7]. Houtsma et al. reviewed firearm access and storage as modifiable contributors to firearm-suicide risk [8]; the review does not establish this patient's individual probability of death. Firearm access matters because it changes lethality: household firearm access was associated with about three times the odds of suicide across 16 observational studies (pooled OR 3.24) [10], and in a cohort of 26 million California adults, handgun owners had higher suicide rates than nonowners (adjusted HR 3.34 in men, 7.16 in women) [11]. In one health system, documented lethal-means assessment after reported suicidal ideation was associated with fewer suicide attempts or deaths within 180 days (0.83% vs 3.3%; quasi-experimental, not randomized) [12]. No high-quality randomized trial has shown that changing access to lethal means reduces suicide attempts or deaths; at the population level, stricter firearm regulations were associated with a small reduction, if any [13]. In a community cohort of people whose first suicide attempt reached medical care, 48 of the 81 later suicide deaths (59%) occurred on that first attempt, and 72.9% of the attempters who died used guns (odds ratio for gunshot death vs other methods, 140) [14]. Remove unverified assumptions about access from the formulation and escalate if an adequate safety arrangement cannot be confirmed.

**Q6. If the completed assessment supports discharge, design the follow-up bridge. What does the evidence support?**
→ *Teaching point:* Arrange prompt, dated follow-up and active contact, including a named service responsible for reassessment and missed appointments. Telephone follow-up was part of SPI+ [4]. Doupnik et al. found brief acute-care interventions associated with fewer subsequent attempts (pooled OR 0.69) and greater linkage to follow-up (OR 3.04), but not reduced depression symptoms [9]. The pooled findings do not isolate which component caused benefit or prove a specific appointment interval. Confirm the roommate's willingness and limits, discuss a structured psychotherapy referral after diagnostic assessment, and address alcohol use. Record who will contact her, how barriers will be addressed, and how unresolved safety concerns will be escalated.

**Q7. Her roommate asks, "What do I do if she texts me something like that again?" What can you disclose, and how do you use collateral without violating confidentiality?**
→ *Teaching point:* Invite collateral without unnecessarily disclosing protected information, and seek the patient's consent for the roommate's role in planning. Explain confidentiality limits with the supervising clinician and follow institutional privacy policy and applicable law when considering emergency disclosure; do not teach a blanket consent rule. Coach the roommate to take renewed suicidal statements seriously, contact the clinical or crisis team promptly, and seek emergency help for immediate danger. Do not make an exhausted roommate the sole safety system. Document consent, permitted disclosures, the roommate's actual willingness and the escalation plan.

**Q8 (documentation pearl). Write the one-paragraph risk formulation for the chart. What must it contain to be clinically and medicolegally sound?**
→ *Teaching point:* (a) Chronic risk factors and acute drivers, named; (b) protective factors, honestly weighed; (c) only the assessment and mitigation actually completed, with pending actions, unresolved questions and a named clinician or service responsible for follow-through; (d) the reasoning connecting formulation to disposition (what supports the selected level of care, which risks remain, and why the proposed mitigation is feasible); (e) foreseeable-change contingencies. Do not document a verified storage plan or dated appointment unless it has been confirmed. "Denies SI, no plan or intent, contracts for safety" is the exemplar of what *not* to write [1,2].

## Differential (provisional; clarify with interview)

1. **Possible borderline personality disorder or personality-related emotion dysregulation with an acute suicidal crisis** — the described pattern warrants longitudinal assessment; the stem does not establish the full diagnostic criteria, pervasiveness or exclusions.
2. **Alcohol intoxication with possible alcohol use disorder** — the acute exposure is known; assess the longitudinal pattern, consequences, dependence and withdrawal risk before diagnosing AUD.
3. **Possible major depressive episode** — assess mood, anhedonia, symptom count, duration and impairment; the stem does not establish a depressive episode or confirmed BPD.
4. **Possible trauma-related disorder** — ask sensitively about trauma and related symptoms; neither trauma exposure nor dissociation is supplied in the stem, and interpersonal triggers alone do not establish PTSD.
5. **Bipolar II disorder** — mood lability here is hours-long and interpersonally cued (against sustained hypomanic episodes), but screen before any antidepressant.
6. **Substance-induced depressive disorder** — if depressive symptoms track exclusively with alcohol use.

## Workup & management

- **Workup:** immediate medical assessment followed by an interview when she can participate; structured suicide assessment; collateral from the roommate and prior records; assess alcohol use and withdrawal risk. Select testing based on clinical findings and treatment considerations, not an automatic panel or alcohol-level threshold. Assess depressive, trauma-related and bipolar symptoms without assuming they are present.
- **Acute management:** safe observation and ongoing medical assessment while participation is impaired; reassessment when she can participate; collaborative SPI; lethal means counseling based on actual access. Consider medication supply limits and storage with a support person only after confirming the person's willingness, capacity and a feasible, lawful arrangement; do not assume the roommate can take this role. Address alcohol use and assess withdrawal risk.
- **Disposition:** not yet determined by the stem. Current denial of intent and clinical sobriety do not establish return to baseline or discharge safety. The supervising team must assess remaining risk and confirm engagement, actual means safety, willing support and prompt follow-up before considering discharge; if risk cannot be managed in that setting, continue a safe assessment setting and consider admission under local policy [1]. Document the reasoning and uncertainty per Q8.
- **Longitudinal:** offer continuity of structured psychotherapy suited to the completed formulation; assess whether a DBT-informed service fits her needs. Assess for AUD before choosing disorder-specific treatment; medication decisions require diagnostic and contraindication review. Arrange contact to confirm engagement. These are provisional teaching options for faculty review, not a regimen established by this stem.

## Facilitator notes (not for learner handout)

- **Timing:** 30 minutes. Run Q1 as a rapid-fire page simulation; slow down at Q2 (formulation) and Q4 (have residents actually role-play two SPI steps in pairs).
- **Common misconceptions:** chronic suicidality means admission is never indicated (false — acute-on-chronic escalation changes the calculus); risk scales can select disposition (the longitudinal prediction literature has substantial limits [2]); safety plan = handout (rehearse a collaborative plan and confirm feasibility); a no-suicide contract establishes safety (do not use a contract as evidence of safety or as a substitute for assessment and collaborative planning [1]).
- **Discussion spice (evidence tensions):** Critique Stanley 2018 as a nonrandomized comparison of VA EDs, with limited generalizability to this patient and no proof that SPI alone caused the association [4]. Nuij rated four of six studies at high risk of bias and identified possible publication bias; the pooled benefit is not a guarantee for an individual patient [5]. Doupnik could not establish an effect on suicide deaths, and possible publication bias affected the linkage outcome [9]. Discuss heterogeneity in Ferguson [6] and the limits of applying long-term predictor studies to an acute bedside decision [2].
- **Safety framing:** keep all discussion method-nonspecific (counting pills, naming calibers, or rehearsing methods adds nothing pedagogically); model direct, nonjudgmental elicitation. If a learner discloses personal distress, pause and use your program's support pathway.
