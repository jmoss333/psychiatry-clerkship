# Case of the Week — "I Can't Do This Anymore"

## Suicide Risk Assessment & Safety Planning: MS3 Version

*Psychiatry Clerkship — Case of the Week · 2026-10-12 · De-identified synthetic teaching case (no real patient details). Based on articles retrieved from PubMed; DOI links in the reference list.*

---

## Stem (learner-facing)

**Part 1.** A 52-year-old man is brought to the emergency department by his sister on a Sunday evening. Over the phone that afternoon he told her, "I can't do this anymore — everyone would be better off without me." He lost his job three months ago, his divorce was finalized last month, and he has been sleeping poorly and drinking more than usual. He has no prior psychiatric treatment. In the ED he is calm, cooperative, and makes good eye contact. He says, "I shouldn't have said that to her. I'm fine. I just want to go home."

Vital signs are normal. He is clinically sober (last drink the previous evening). Physical exam is unremarkable.

**Part 2 (after the learner discusses initial approach).** On direct, nonjudgmental questioning, he acknowledges two weeks of near-daily thoughts that he would be "better off dead," and in the past several days has had thoughts of ending his life. He describes feeling like a burden and hopeless about finding work at his age. He denies having taken any steps toward acting on these thoughts tonight. He mentions, when asked about his home environment, that he keeps firearms at home. PHQ-9 is 19 (moderately severe depression). He asks again to be discharged.

---

## Guided discussion

**Q1. The patient says "I'm fine, I just want to go home." What is your next step, and how do you ask about suicide?**
→ *Teaching point:* Ask directly and specifically — "Have you had thoughts of killing yourself?" Direct questioning does not increase suicidal ideation and is the foundation of risk assessment. Screen with a validated tool (e.g., the Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale, C-SSRS, which structures questions from passive ideation through intent and behavior) rather than relying on spontaneous disclosure [1]. A reassuring surface presentation ("smiling, pleasant") does not rule out risk.

**Q2. What elements make up a suicide risk assessment? Organize his risk factors and protective factors.**
*Define first:* **Ideation** = thoughts of suicide (passive = wish to be dead; active = thoughts of killing oneself). **Intent** = expectation and commitment to act. **Plan** = a formulated method/time. **Means** = access to a way of acting.
→ *Teaching point:* Risk assessment is a structured synthesis, not a checklist score: ideation (passive → active), intent, plan, access to lethal means, prior attempts (the strongest single historical predictor), psychiatric illness (here, likely major depressive disorder), substance use, recent losses, demographic risk (middle-aged and older men have high suicide death rates), and protective factors (connected sister, help arrived tonight). His acute-on-chronic picture: new active ideation, mounting stressors, alcohol use, firearm access — against engageable family support and no prior attempts [2].

**Q3. Why does his alcohol use matter for tonight's assessment, and what else is on your differential for his presentation?**
→ *Teaching point:* Alcohol intoxication disinhibits and acutely elevates suicide risk, and assessments made while intoxicated are unreliable — reassess when sober (he is now). Intoxication can also mask or mimic depression. The differential for "hopeless statement + functional decline" includes major depressive disorder, substance-induced mood disorder, adjustment disorder with depressed mood, and an underlying medical contributor — see the ranked differential below.

**Q4. He has firearms at home. How do you address this, and does it change disposition?**
→ *Teaching point:* Ask every at-risk patient about access to lethal means, and counsel on reducing access ("lethal means counseling") — e.g., temporary off-site storage with a trusted person, or locked and separated storage — delivered collaboratively, not confiscation-first. Means restriction works because suicidal crises are often brief, and reducing access during the crisis window saves lives; clinicians underuse this conversation [3]. Firearm access in the setting of active ideation substantially raises the stakes of any discharge plan.

**Q5. The team decides he can be managed without admission *if* a strong plan is in place. What is a safety planning intervention (SPI), and what goes in it?**
→ *Teaching point:* A safety plan is a brief, collaborative, written intervention — **not** a "no-suicide contract," which is ineffective and obsolete. Standard SPI steps: (1) personal warning signs; (2) internal coping strategies; (3) people and places that distract/support; (4) specific people to ask for help; (5) professional and crisis resources (see the contacts below); (6) making the environment safer (lethal means). In an ED trial, safety planning plus structured telephone follow-up roughly halved subsequent suicidal behavior and doubled outpatient engagement compared with usual care [4]; meta-analysis confirms safety-planning-type interventions reduce suicidal behavior [5].

**Q6. What determines admission versus discharge here? What would make you insist on hospitalization?**
→ *Teaching point:* Disposition turns on the balance of acute risk versus what can be mitigated tonight: intent or preparatory behavior, inability to engage in safety planning, intoxication that cannot be reassessed, psychosis, no reliable support, or means that cannot be secured all push toward admission (involuntary if necessary and criteria are met). Discharge requires an engaged patient, a concrete safety plan, secured means, a support person, and rapid follow-up — brief single-encounter interventions with linkage to care reduce subsequent attempts (pooled OR ≈ 0.7) and triple follow-up engagement [6].

**Q7. Three days later his sister calls the clinic: he missed his intake appointment. Why does this matter and what should happen?**
→ *Teaching point:* The highest-risk window is the days to weeks after an ED visit or hospital discharge. A missed appointment after an ED visit for suicidal ideation is a red flag requiring active outreach (call the patient, re-engage, reassess risk) — not passive rescheduling. Structured post-discharge contact is a core component of what made follow-up-based interventions effective [4,6].

## Differential (ranked)

1. **Major depressive disorder, single episode, moderate-severe, with suicidal ideation** — ≥2 weeks of low mood, anhedonia, insomnia, worthlessness/burden, hopelessness; PHQ-9 19.
2. **Alcohol use disorder / substance-induced depressive disorder** — escalating drinking; mood symptoms could be partly substance-driven; reassess longitudinally and sober.
3. **Adjustment disorder with depressed mood** — clear stressors (job loss, divorce), but symptom severity and pervasive hopelessness favor MDD.
4. **Depressive disorder due to another medical condition** — screen (e.g., hypothyroidism); no current signs.
5. **Bipolar depression** — always screen for prior mania/hypomania before starting an antidepressant; none elicited.

## Workup & management

- **Workup:** sober clinical interview with collateral from sister; C-SSRS; PHQ-9 (done); CIWA-Ar monitoring not needed (no withdrawal signs) but quantify alcohol history (AUDIT-C); basic labs as indicated (CBC, CMP, TSH, B12), blood alcohol level on arrival, urine drug screen.
- **Safety-critical steps:** direct risk assessment; lethal means counseling with a concrete firearm storage plan involving the sister; written safety plan built with the patient; crisis line on the plan (see the contacts above).
- **Disposition (this case):** if he engages, means are secured, and the sister can stay with him — discharge with urgent (≤72 h) psychiatric follow-up and structured telephone follow-up; otherwise admit.
- **Treatment of the underlying episode:** psychoeducation; initiate an SSRI (e.g., sertraline) with follow-up within 1–2 weeks; brief counseling on reducing alcohol use; psychotherapy referral (CBT).

## Facilitator notes (not for learner handout)

- **Timing:** 20–30 minutes. Reveal Part 2 only after the group commits to how they would ask about suicide.
- **Common misconceptions to surface:** (a) asking about suicide "plants the idea" — it does not; (b) "no-suicide contracts" are protective — they are not and are no longer recommended; (c) a calm, pleasant patient requesting discharge is low risk; (d) risk assessment produces a number that decides disposition — prediction tools alone perform poorly, and the assessment informs clinical judgment rather than replacing it.
- **Pivot questions:** "What would have to be true for you to feel comfortable discharging him?" · "Who secures the firearms, concretely, before he leaves?" · "What changes if he had a prior attempt?"
- **Safety framing:** keep all discussion method-nonspecific; model asking directly and compassionately. If any learner discloses personal distress, pause teaching and follow your program's support pathway.
