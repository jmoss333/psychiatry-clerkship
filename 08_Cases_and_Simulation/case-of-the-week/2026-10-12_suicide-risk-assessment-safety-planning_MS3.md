# Case of the Week — "I Can't Do This Anymore"

## Suicide Risk Assessment & Safety Planning: MS3 Version

*Psychiatry Clerkship — Case of the Week · 2026-10-12 · De-identified synthetic teaching case (no real patient details). Based on articles retrieved from PubMed; DOI links in the reference list.*

---

## Stem (learner-facing)

**Part 1.** A 52-year-old man is brought to the emergency department by his sister on a Sunday evening. Over the phone that afternoon he told her, "I can't do this anymore — everyone would be better off without me." He lost his job three months ago, his divorce was finalized last month, and he has been sleeping poorly and drinking more than usual. He has no prior psychiatric treatment. In the ED he is calm, cooperative, and makes good eye contact. He says, "I shouldn't have said that to her. I'm fine. I just want to go home."

Vital signs are normal. He is clinically sober (last drink the previous evening). Physical exam is unremarkable.

**Part 2 (after the learner discusses initial approach).** On direct, nonjudgmental questioning, he acknowledges two weeks of near-daily thoughts that he would be "better off dead," and in the past several days has had thoughts of ending his life. He describes feeling like a burden and hopeless about finding work at his age. He denies having taken any steps toward acting on these thoughts tonight. He mentions, when asked about his home environment, that he keeps firearms at home. PHQ-9 is 19 (a moderately severe depressive-symptom score; a diagnosis still requires an interview). He asks again to be discharged.

---

## Guided discussion

**Q1. The patient says "I'm fine, I just want to go home." What is your next step, and how do you ask about suicide?**
→ *Teaching point:* Ask directly and specifically — "Have you had thoughts of killing yourself?" Use direct questioning and a structured interview rather than relying on spontaneous disclosure [2]. Posner et al. found that the C-SSRS ideation and behavior subscales were sensitive to change over time [1]; that validation does not make a score a disposition rule. Use the official instrument and local assessment pathway; do not reproduce its items here. A reassuring surface presentation ("smiling, pleasant") does not rule out risk.

**Q2. What elements make up a suicide risk assessment? Organize his risk factors and protective factors.**
*Define first:* **Ideation** = thoughts of suicide (passive = wish to be dead; active = thoughts of killing oneself). **Intent** = expectation and commitment to act. **Plan** = a formulated method/time. **Means** = access to a way of acting.
→ *Teaching point:* Risk assessment is a structured synthesis, not a checklist score: ideation (passive → active), intent, plan, access to lethal means, prior attempts and self-harm, possible psychiatric illness, substance use, recent losses, and potential protective factors (his sister brought him for help). This stem establishes recent active ideation, stressors, increased alcohol use, and firearm access. Prior attempts, current intent and plan, and the sister's ability to help remain to be elicited; absence of prior treatment does not establish absence of attempts. A depressive disorder is possible, not established. Powsner et al. emphasize that screening, assessment, and mitigation are distinct tasks and that complex presentations require clinical assessment [2].

**Q3. Why does his alcohol use matter for tonight's assessment, and what else is on your differential for his presentation?**
→ *Teaching point:* Assess current safety and medical needs immediately. If intoxication limits participation, continue observation and reassess when the patient can participate meaningfully; sobriety alone does not establish safety [2]. This patient is described as clinically sober, but the quantity, pattern, dependence and withdrawal history remain unknown. The differential for his hopelessness and recent suicidal ideation includes major depressive disorder, substance-induced mood disorder, adjustment disorder with depressed mood, and an underlying medical contributor — see the ranked differential below.

**Q4. He has firearms at home. How do you address this, and does it change disposition?**
→ *Teaching point:* Ask about access to lethal means and collaboratively arrange a feasible reduction in access before any discharge [2]. Involve a willing support person with consent; any firearm storage arrangement must be lawful and actually prevent the patient's access during the crisis. Do not assume that a promise to lock something establishes that access has been removed. Firearm access matters because it changes lethality: household firearm access was associated with about three times the odds of suicide across 16 observational studies (pooled OR 3.24) [7], and in a cohort of 26 million California adults, handgun owners had higher suicide rates than nonowners (adjusted HR 3.34 in men, 7.16 in women) [8]. In one health system, documented lethal-means assessment after reported suicidal ideation was associated with fewer suicide attempts or deaths within 180 days (0.83% vs 3.3%; quasi-experimental, not randomized) [9]. No high-quality randomized trial has shown that changing access to lethal means reduces suicide attempts or deaths; at the population level, stricter firearm regulations were associated with a small reduction, if any [10]. Siry et al. interviewed clinicians about the Lock to Live decision aid and identified implementation issues involving perceived utility, time constraints and workflow; this qualitative study did not measure a reduction in suicide deaths [3].

**Q5. While the team completes the assessment, how would you collaboratively develop a safety plan? Does completing one establish that discharge is safe?**
→ *Teaching point:* A safety plan is a collaborative intervention, not a promise that the patient will stay safe or a substitute for assessment. Teach the process in the patient's own language: recognize a worsening crisis, rehearse coping and help-seeking, identify available supporters and professional services, and address access to lethal means. Use the [official Stanley–Brown resource](https://suicidesafetyplan.com/) rather than reproducing its form. In a nonrandomized VA ED cohort, SPI plus telephone follow-up was associated with lower odds of suicidal behavior (OR 0.56) and higher odds of attending outpatient care (OR 2.06) over six months; hospitalization had already been judged not clinically indicated [4]. Nuij et al. pooled six studies and found lower suicidal behavior (RR 0.570), but no significant effect on ideation [5]. These findings do not establish that this patient can be discharged.

**Q6. What determines admission versus discharge here? What would make you insist on hospitalization?**
→ *Teaching point:* The stem does not establish a safe disposition. Complete assessment of intent, plan, preparatory behavior, prior attempts, mental state, capacity to participate, access and available supports with the supervising clinician. Persistent intent, an inability to collaborate, or risk that cannot be mitigated in the proposed setting warrants urgent escalation and consideration of admission under local policy [2]. If discharge is clinically justified, confirm the means-safety arrangement, collaborative plan, support and scheduled prompt follow-up. Doupnik et al. found brief acute-care interventions associated with fewer subsequent attempts (pooled OR 0.69) and greater linkage to follow-up (OR 3.04), but not reduced depression symptoms [6]; odds of attendance are not the same as tripling the attendance rate.

**Q7. Three days later his sister calls the clinic: he missed his intake appointment. Why does this matter and what should happen?**
→ *Teaching point:* Arrange active outreach after a missed appointment: try to contact him, reassess current safety, address barriers and involve agreed supports within consent and local policy. Escalate unresolved immediate safety concerns to the supervising team. Follow-up contact was part of the SPI+ package [4]; these studies do not isolate its effect or establish an exact optimal appointment interval [4,6].

## Differential (provisional; clarify with interview)

1. **Possible depressive disorder, including MDD** — insomnia, hopelessness, burden and suicidal ideation warrant assessment. The stem does not establish depressed mood or anhedonia, the full symptom count, duration of a depressive syndrome, or functional impairment. PHQ-9 19 alone does not diagnose MDD or establish episode severity.
2. **Alcohol-related symptoms / possible alcohol use disorder** — increased drinking warrants assessment of use, consequences, dependence and temporal relationship to mood symptoms; AUD is not yet established.
3. **Adjustment disorder with depressed mood** — consider the relationship to job loss and divorce after assessing whether another disorder better explains the symptoms; suicidal ideation does not by itself distinguish this from MDD.
4. **Medical contributors** — evaluate according to history and examination rather than assuming an unreported condition is present or absent.
5. **Bipolar depression** — ask about prior mania/hypomania and family history before antidepressant treatment; this history is not supplied.

## Workup & management

- **Workup:** interview with collateral from the sister; structured suicide assessment; review the PHQ-9 responses in context; quantify alcohol use and assess withdrawal risk. A calm examination does not establish the absence of future withdrawal risk. Select medical testing from the history, examination and local ED policy; do not treat a blanket laboratory panel as the psychiatric assessment.
- **Safety-critical steps:** ongoing observation appropriate to current risk; direct assessment; a concrete, lawful means-safety plan that is verified rather than assumed; a collaborative safety plan and the crisis resources above.
- **Disposition (not yet determined):** the supervising team must complete the missing assessment before deciding. Consider discharge only when the clinical formulation supports it and the proposed support, means safety and prompt follow-up have been confirmed; otherwise continue a safe assessment setting and consider admission [2]. A safety plan or the sister's presence alone does not establish discharge safety.
- **Treatment (conditional on diagnosis):** do not initiate an SSRI solely from this stem or the PHQ-9 total. If a depressive disorder is confirmed after assessment of bipolar and substance-related explanations, discuss appropriate psychotherapy and/or medication with the supervising clinician and the patient, with an explicit monitoring and follow-up plan. Address alcohol use and immediate safety regardless of the eventual diagnosis.

## Facilitator notes (not for learner handout)

- **Timing:** 20–30 minutes. Reveal Part 2 only after the group commits to how they would ask about suicide.
- **Common misconceptions to surface:** (a) avoiding direct questions when a patient minimizes; (b) a no-suicide contract establishes safety — do not use a contract as evidence of safety or as a substitute for assessment and collaborative planning [2]; (c) a calm, pleasant patient requesting discharge is low risk; (d) risk assessment produces a number that decides disposition — prediction tools alone perform poorly, and the assessment informs clinical judgment rather than replacing it.
- **Evidence limits:** Nuij rated four of six studies at high risk of bias and identified possible publication bias [5]. Doupnik could not establish an effect on suicide deaths, and possible publication bias affected the linkage outcome [6]. Teach the reported outcomes and uncertainty rather than promising an individual result.
- **Pivot questions:** "What would have to be true for you to feel comfortable discharging him?" · "Who secures the firearms, concretely, before he leaves?" · "What changes if he had a prior attempt?"
- **Safety framing:** keep all discussion method-nonspecific; model asking directly and compassionately. If any learner discloses personal distress, pause teaching and follow your program's support pathway.
