# Case of the Week — The Student Who Stopped Sleeping (MS3 Version)

## Bipolar Mania: Recognition & Acute Management

## Case Stem (Learner-Facing)

Jordan is a 21-year-old college junior with no prior psychiatric hospitalizations, brought to the emergency department by two roommates at 3 a.m. Over the past 10 days, Jordan has slept roughly 2–3 hours per night yet reports feeling "better than I've ever felt — I finally see how everything connects." Jordan has emailed the university provost a 40-page proposal to "restructure the entire economics department," spent $4,800 of tuition money on audio equipment for a podcast started yesterday, and been asked to leave the library twice for talking loudly and rapidly to strangers.

In the ED, Jordan is wearing sunglasses indoors, pacing, and speaking so quickly it is difficult to interrupt ("pressured speech"). Thoughts jump between loosely connected topics ("flight of ideas"). Jordan is irritable when redirected but denies wanting to harm anyone. Jordan describes mood as "electric." There are no hallucinations. Jordan states, "God has bigger plans for me than this hospital," but is vague about specifics.

Collateral from a parent (by phone, with Jordan's permission): about two years ago Jordan had a "burned-out semester" — several months of low mood, hypersomnia, and withdrawal from classes that resolved without treatment. There is a maternal uncle who "had highs and lows" and took lithium. Jordan drinks alcohol socially, and the roommates report Jordan used a friend's stimulant medication "once or twice" during finals last spring, but not in the past month, as far as they know.

Vitals: HR 104, BP 138/86, T 37.1°C, RR 16. Exam: hyperverbal, distractible, no focal neurologic findings. Urine toxicology and labs are pending.

## Guided Discussion Questions

**Q1. What features of Jordan's presentation meet DSM-5 criteria for a manic episode? Which single feature most distinguishes mania from hypomania?**

*Teaching point:* A manic episode requires a distinct period of abnormally elevated, expansive, or irritable mood **and** persistently increased energy/activity lasting **≥1 week** (or any duration if hospitalization is required), plus ≥3 additional symptoms (≥4 if mood is only irritable): remember **DIGFAST** — Distractibility, Impulsivity/risky behavior, Grandiosity, Flight of ideas, Activity increase, Sleep need decreased, Talkativeness. Jordan has decreased need for sleep (not insomnia — Jordan feels *rested*), grandiosity, pressured speech, flight of ideas, distractibility, goal-directed hyperactivity, and high-risk spending. The key discriminator from hypomania is **severity of consequences**: marked functional impairment, psychotic features, or need for hospitalization makes it mania — duration alone does not settle it [1].

**Q2. Jordan "feels great" and denies any problem. Why is collateral information especially important in suspected mania, and what specific collateral would you seek?**

*Teaching point:* Insight is characteristically impaired in mania; patients rarely present themselves because elevated mood does not feel like illness. Collateral (family, roommates, prior records) establishes the timeline, baseline personality, prior depressive episodes (present here: the "burned-out semester"), family history of bipolar disorder or lithium response, substance use, and safety concerns (spending, driving, sexual risk, aggression). A prior unrecognized depressive episode plus a first manic episode is a classic route to a new diagnosis of **bipolar I disorder** — which requires only one lifetime manic episode [1,2].

**Q3. Build a ranked differential for this presentation. What must be ruled out before the episode is attributed to bipolar I disorder?**

*Teaching point:* See the ranked differential below. The crucial habit: **first mania is a medical and toxicologic workup, not just a psychiatric diagnosis.** Substance-induced states (stimulants, cocaine, corticosteroids, antidepressant-associated activation) and medical causes (hyperthyroidism, CNS disease) must be reasonably excluded; the history of stimulant misuse makes urine toxicology and a careful substance timeline essential — although a syndrome that *persists well beyond* intoxication argues for a primary mood episode [1,2].

**Q4. What is your initial workup for a first manic episode?**

*Teaching point:* Core panel: CBC, CMP (renal and hepatic function — needed before lithium/valproate anyway), TSH, urine toxicology, pregnancy test when applicable, and consideration of B12, and neuroimaging **only** when there are atypical features (new onset at older age, focal neurology, delirium-like picture). ECG if antipsychotics or lithium are planned (QTc, and lithium affects the sinus node). Baseline weight/BMI, lipids, and A1c matter because second-generation antipsychotics cause metabolic harm and you are choosing a drug the patient may stay on [1,4].

**Q5. Jordan is escalating — pacing faster, now shouting at staff. Walk through management of acute agitation in suspected mania, from least to most restrictive.**

*Teaching point:* Verbal de-escalation and a low-stimulation room come first; offer oral medication before parenteral (oral SGA such as olanzapine or risperidone, with a benzodiazepine such as lorazepam as adjunct); IM medication only if oral is refused and danger is imminent; physical restraint is a last resort with prompt medical review. Document the least-restrictive rationale. Safety of patient and staff is the immediate priority — the definitive antimanic plan comes after [1,3].

**Q6. What are the first-line medication strategies for acute mania, and how would you choose among them for Jordan?**

*Teaching point:* First-line options are **lithium, valproate ("divalproex"), or a second-generation antipsychotic (e.g., quetiapine, risperidone, aripiprazole, asenapine, cariprazine, paliperidone)** — alone for less severe episodes, or **combination therapy (lithium or valproate + SGA)** for severe mania, which acts faster and is more effective than monotherapy [1,4]. **Antidepressants must be stopped** during mania. For Jordan: young, first episode, classic euphoric mania, family history of lithium-treated illness, and likely need for maintenance — lithium is an attractive backbone (with renal/thyroid baseline labs), typically paired initially with an SGA for rapid control. Valproate is teratogenic — pregnancy testing and counseling are mandatory in patients who can become pregnant [1,2,4].

**Q7. Before discharge planning, what safety issues must be addressed in mania even when the patient denies suicidal ideation?**

*Teaching point:* Bipolar disorder carries one of the highest suicide risks in psychiatry, concentrated in **depressive and mixed phases and the post-episode recovery period** — the danger is not over when the mania ends [2]. Screen directly for suicidal ideation now and at follow-up; if concerns emerge, escalate to your supervisor immediately rather than managing alone. Address the *manic* dangers too: reckless spending, driving, sexual risk-taking, and reputational harm. Discharge requires a concrete safety plan (warning signs, coping steps, support contacts, means safety discussed in general terms, and emergency contacts), a named follow-up appointment, psychoeducation with the family, and sleep protection — sleep loss is both a symptom and a trigger [1,2].

## Ranked Differential Diagnosis

1. **Bipolar I disorder, first manic episode** — leading diagnosis: ≥1 week of elevated/irritable mood with ≥3–4 DIGFAST symptoms, marked impairment, probable prior untreated depressive episode, family history.
2. **Substance/medication-induced bipolar-type episode** — stimulant misuse history and pending toxicology; sustained symptoms over 10 days beyond any plausible intoxication window argue against this as the sole cause.
3. **Mania secondary to another medical condition** — hyperthyroidism, CNS lesion, autoimmune encephalitis; screen with TSH, exam, targeted labs; imaging only if atypical features.
4. **Schizoaffective disorder / primary psychotic disorder** — grandiose religious statement borders on psychosis; in schizoaffective disorder, psychosis persists ≥2 weeks *without* mood symptoms — not established here.
5. **ADHD or personality-based impulsivity** — distractibility and impulsivity overlap, but neither produces a discrete episode of decreased need for sleep with grandiosity; episodic course is the key.

## Workup & Management (Summary Table)

| Phase | Actions |
|---|---|
| Immediate (ED) | Safety assessment; verbal de-escalation; offer oral SGA ± lorazepam; urine tox, CBC, CMP, TSH, pregnancy test; ECG |
| Diagnosis | DSM-5 manic episode criteria + collateral timeline; rule out substance/medical causes |
| Acute treatment | Stop any antidepressant; lithium or valproate or SGA; combination (Li/VPA + SGA) for severe mania; hospitalize (Jordan meets criteria: impairment, risk, first episode) |
| Monitoring | Lithium level 5–7 days after start/dose change (target ~0.8–1.2 mEq/L acutely); renal/thyroid for lithium; LFTs/CBC for valproate; metabolic baseline for SGA |
| Discharge | Safety plan, psychoeducation (sleep regularity, substance avoidance, early-warning signs), family involvement, prompt outpatient follow-up |

## Facilitator Notes (Do Not Distribute to Learners)

- **Flow (20–30 min):** 5 min stem read-aloud; Q1–Q3 as a group (10 min); Q4–Q6 rapid-fire (10 min); close with Q7 safety discussion (5 min). If short on time, prioritize Q1, Q3, Q6, Q7.
- **Common MS3 errors to surface:** calling this "hypomania" because Jordan is functional enough to email the provost (the spending, hospitalization need, and impairment make it mania); anchoring on stimulant use and dismissing the primary mood disorder; forgetting that one manic episode alone = bipolar I (no depressive episode required); choosing an antidepressant for the historical depressive symptoms.
- **Step 2 CK hooks:** DIGFAST criteria; mania vs. hypomania discriminators; first-line = lithium/valproate/SGA and combination for severe mania; lithium therapeutic index and monitoring; valproate teratogenicity; antidepressant monotherapy is contraindicated in bipolar I.
- **Safety framing:** Keep all suicide-related discussion at the level of recognition, screening, escalation to supervisors, and safety planning. Do not discuss methods or means specifics beyond "means safety counseling."
- **Equity/communication angle (optional 3 min):** how to obtain collateral while preserving rapport and confidentiality with a patient who has impaired insight; involuntary hold criteria vary by state — invite learners to name the local standard.
