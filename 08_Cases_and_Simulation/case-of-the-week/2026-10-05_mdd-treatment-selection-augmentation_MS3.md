# Case: The Antidepressant That "Isn't Working" — MS3

## Major Depressive Disorder: Treatment Selection & Augmentation

*Case of the Week — 2026-10-05 · MS3 (psychiatry clerkship) version*
*De-identified synthetic teaching case. Based on articles retrieved from PubMed; see numbered references with DOI links at the end.*

---

## Stem (learner-facing)

**Part 1.** A 34-year-old woman presents to the outpatient psychiatry clinic, referred by her primary care physician. For the past four months she has had low mood most of the day nearly every day, loss of interest in painting and running (activities she used to love), poor concentration at her accounting job, early-morning awakening, a 10-lb unintentional weight loss, fatigue, and persistent feelings of worthlessness. She denies current thoughts of suicide ("I would never do that — but I do sometimes wish I could just not wake up"). She has no psychotic symptoms.

Eight weeks ago her PCP started sertraline 50 mg daily, increased to 100 mg daily after two weeks. She has taken it "most days." Her PHQ-9 today is 18 (it was 21 before starting sertraline). She asks: *"Does this mean antidepressants don't work for me?"*

**Part 2 (revealed during discussion).** On focused questioning she denies any lifetime episode of abnormally elevated, expansive, or irritable mood with increased energy lasting ≥4 days. She drinks 1–2 glasses of wine per week, denies other substances. TSH, CBC, and metabolic panel from her PCP are normal. She is not pregnant and takes no other medications. Her maternal aunt "did well on an antidepressant plus something else."

---

## Guided discussion

**Q1. Before changing any medication, what diagnostic questions must be revisited in a patient whose depression "isn't responding"?**
→ *Teaching point:* So-called non-response is often **pseudo-resistance**. Re-confirm the diagnosis (screen every "resistant" depression for **bipolar disorder** — an antidepressant alone can destabilize it), check **adherence** ("most days" matters), verify the **dose and duration were adequate** (≥4–6 weeks at a therapeutic dose), and rule out medical mimics (hypothyroidism, anemia, OSA) and ongoing substance use. Guidelines recommend this re-assessment as the first step whenever response is inadequate [1].

**Q2. How should we measure whether a treatment is working?**
→ *Teaching point:* **Measurement-based care** — a validated scale (PHQ-9) at every visit, with defined targets. *Response* = ≥50% score reduction; *remission* = PHQ-9 <5 (the goal of treatment, because residual symptoms predict relapse). Her PHQ-9 going 21→18 after 6 weeks at 100 mg is **inadequate improvement**, not failure of "antidepressants" as a class [1].

**Q3. Her early-morning awakening, weight loss, and guilt feel "biological" to her. What DSM-5-TR criteria does she meet, and what severity is this?**
→ *Teaching point:* She meets criteria for **major depressive disorder** (≥5 of 9 symptoms, ≥2 weeks, including depressed mood or anhedonia, with functional impairment): depressed mood, anhedonia, insomnia, weight loss, fatigue, worthlessness, poor concentration — 7 of 9. PHQ-9 of 18 = moderately severe. Passive death wishes ("wish I could just not wake up") **count as passive suicidal ideation and must be asked about directly** — asking does not increase risk.

**Q4. What is the role of suicide risk assessment at this visit, and what belongs in a safety plan?**
→ *Teaching point:* Every depression visit — especially at treatment transitions — includes risk assessment: current ideation (passive vs. active), plan or intent, prior attempts, access to lethal means, protective factors. For passive ideation without plan or intent, outpatient management with a collaborative **safety plan** is appropriate: personal warning signs, internal coping strategies, people and settings that distract, contacts for help (including crisis lines — see the contacts below), means restriction counseling, and clear instructions for escalation (call the clinic, go to the ED). Document it. Escalate to urgent/emergent evaluation if ideation becomes active, a plan or intent emerges, or risk factors accumulate.

**Q5. She has had an adequate trial of one SSRI without remission. Walk through the three broad medication strategies now.**
→ *Teaching point:* (a) **Optimize** — push sertraline toward maximum tolerated dose if partial response and tolerating; (b) **Switch** — to another first-line antidepressant (different SSRI, SNRI, bupropion, mirtazapine), favored when there has been *minimal* response or intolerable side effects; (c) **Augment/combine** — add a second agent (e.g., an atypical antipsychotic such as aripiprazole, or combine with bupropion), favored when there has been a *partial* response worth keeping [1,3]. Psychotherapy (CBT) should be offered at every step — combination of medication and therapy outperforms either alone for moderate-to-severe MDD [1].

**Q6. What did STAR*D teach us about what happens as patients move through successive medication trials?**
→ *Teaching point:* In STAR*D, remission rates fell with each successive step (~37% → ~31% → ~14% → ~13%), with a cumulative remission of about two-thirds over four steps — and relapse rates rose for patients who needed more steps [2]. Clinical translation: each failed adequate trial lowers the odds the *next* monotherapy works, which is why augmentation and treatment-intensification decisions arrive early, and why remission (not just "a little better") is the target.

**Q7. If the team chooses augmentation with aripiprazole, what counseling and monitoring does the medical student owe this patient?**
→ *Teaching point:* Atypical antipsychotic augmentation has the strongest trial support among add-on strategies (e.g., VAST-D: aripiprazole augmentation modestly outperformed switching to bupropion) [3], but it is not free: counsel on **akathisia** (restlessness — the most common reason patients stop), weight gain and metabolic effects (baseline and follow-up weight, glucose, lipids), and sedation. Start low (2–5 mg), reassess with the PHQ-9 in 2–4 weeks, and plan an exit strategy if there is no benefit.

## Differential (ranked, with discriminating features)

1. **Major depressive disorder, moderately severe, single episode** — meets full criteria; inadequate response to one adequate SSRI trial. *Leading diagnosis.*
2. **Bipolar depression** — must be excluded by history in every "treatment-resistant" depression; no lifetime hypomanic/manic symptoms here. Antidepressant monotherapy risks switch if missed.
3. **Depression secondary to a medical condition** — hypothyroidism, anemia, OSA; screening labs normal, but ask about snoring/daytime sleepiness.
4. **Substance/medication-induced depressive disorder** — minimal alcohol, no other substances or implicated medications.
5. **Persistent depressive disorder / adjustment disorder** — duration and symptom count fit MDD better; no clear precipitating stressor with subsyndromal picture.

## Workup & management

- **Confirm adequacy**: adherence review, dose/duration check, collateral if available.
- **Labs** (already done; repeat only as indicated): TSH, CBC, CMP; consider B12/vitamin D, urine drug screen, pregnancy test before medication changes.
- **Measurement-based care**: PHQ-9 every visit; target remission (<5) [1].
- **Risk assessment + safety plan** at every medication change (see Q4).
- **First moves**: optimize sertraline to maximum tolerated dose **or** switch vs. augment per the partial-response logic above [1,3]; add evidence-based psychotherapy (CBT or IPT) [1].
- **Safety-critical counseling**: for any antidepressant in patients ≤24 years old, discuss the boxed warning about treatment-emergent suicidal ideation and schedule early follow-up (she is 34 — still schedule follow-up in 2–4 weeks).
- **Know when to escalate**: psychotic features, active suicidal ideation with plan/intent, inability to care for self → urgent psychiatric evaluation; consider ECT referral pathways for severe, refractory, or life-threatening presentations.

## Facilitator notes (not for learner handout)

- **Timing**: 20–30 minutes. Reveal Part 2 after Q1 so students experience hunting for pseudo-resistance themselves.
- **Common misconceptions**: (1) "Failed sertraline = SSRIs don't work" — reframe with STAR*D step logic; (2) forgetting the bipolar screen — make at least one student actually role-play asking about hypomania; (3) treating PHQ-9 improvement 21→18 as success — anchor on remission; (4) equating passive death wishes with "no SI" — model asking the follow-up questions aloud.
- **Pivot questions**: "What would make you NOT simply raise the dose?" · "What one historical fact would completely change the treatment plan?" (answer: hypomania) · "Her aunt responded to an antidepressant 'plus something else' — does family history of augmentation response change your plan?" (evidence is weak; good for discussing evidence vs. heuristics).
- **Safety framing**: keep all suicide-related discussion at the level of recognition, risk stratification, safety planning, and escalation. Do not discuss methods or lethality specifics.
- **Board hook (Step 2 CK)**: adequate trial = 4–6 weeks at therapeutic dose; partial response → augment, no response → switch; aripiprazole augmentation → watch for akathisia; always screen for bipolarity before antidepressants.
