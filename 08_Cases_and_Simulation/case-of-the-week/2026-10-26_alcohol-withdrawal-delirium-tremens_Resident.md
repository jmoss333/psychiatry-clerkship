# Case of the Week — Benzodiazepine-Resistant Withdrawal in the ICU (Resident Version)

## Alcohol Withdrawal & Delirium Tremens: Risk Stratification, Phenobarbital, and Resistant Withdrawal

*De-identified synthetic teaching case. All details are fictional composites — no real patient information. Based in part on articles retrieved from PubMed; see the numbered references with DOI links at the end.*

**Learner level:** Psychiatry residents (C-L / addiction rotation) · **Discussion time:** ~25–30 minutes · **Format:** facilitated case conference

---

## Stem (learner-facing)

**Part 1 — Consultation.** You are the consultation-liaison resident. The MICU requests "management of agitation" in a 47-year-old woman admitted 18 hours ago with alcohol-associated pancreatitis. She reports a decade of daily vodka use (~a pint daily), last drink the morning of admission. History includes one prior ICU admission for withdrawal two years ago during which she "needed the breathing tube," hypertension, and anxiety disorder treated with as-needed hydroxyzine. Admission blood alcohol level was 180 mg/dL; she is now tremulous and diaphoretic with HR 118, BP 164/98. CIWA-Ar is charted at 14, but she is also in significant abdominal pain and has received hydromorphone.

**Part 2 — Escalation.** Over the next 12 hours she receives lorazepam 4 mg IV cumulative via a symptom-triggered CIWA protocol but remains tremulous, now intermittently disoriented, HR 128, and increasingly agitated. The team asks: "Can we just keep going up on lorazepam, or should we switch to something else? And is the CIWA even valid here?" Magnesium is 1.3 mg/dL, phosphate 1.9 mg/dL, potassium 3.2 mEq/L. QTc 465 ms.

**Part 3 — Resistant course.** By 48 hours she has required escalating diazepam boluses (cumulative >100 mg diazepam-equivalents in 8 hours) with persistent severe agitation, visual hallucinations, fluctuating sensorium, and fever 38.3°C — delirium tremens with emerging benzodiazepine resistance. The fellow asks you to lead the discussion on phenobarbital, adjuncts (dexmedetomidine, ketamine, antipsychotics), and airway planning.

---

## Guided discussion

**Q1. Was symptomatic presentation at a blood alcohol level of 180 mg/dL on admission predictive? How do you risk-stratify beyond the CIWA-Ar?**
→ *Teaching point:* Withdrawal signs **while the blood alcohol level is still substantially positive** mark severe physiologic dependence and predict complicated withdrawal, as do prior DT/ICU-level withdrawal or withdrawal seizures, mechanical ventilation during a prior episode, heavy daily use, comorbid acute illness (pancreatitis), and electrolyte depletion. Structured prediction tools (e.g., PAWSS) formalize this; ASAM recommends explicit risk stratification on admission rather than reactive scoring alone. Her PAWSS-type profile mandates a **high-intensity, monitored pathway from hour zero** [1,2].

**Q2. Critique the CIWA-Ar in this patient. What are its validity conditions and alternatives?**
→ *Teaching point:* CIWA-Ar is **symptom-reported and attention-dependent**: it requires a communicative patient and is confounded by pain, opioids, delirium, and non-withdrawal anxiety — all present here. In ICU or communication-limited settings, objective scales (e.g., **MINDS/mMINDS**, or RASS-targeted sedation protocols) outperform it; a VA ward cohort moving to a MINDS-based protocol cut cumulative benzodiazepine exposure and DT incidence [3]. Real-world data also show symptom-triggered dosing is often misapplied in medical inpatients, with *higher* benzodiazepine exposure and more ICU transfers than fixed dosing when risk and monitoring aren't matched to the protocol [4]. The scale is a tool, not the treatment target [1,2].

**Q3. Mechanistically, why does benzodiazepine resistance emerge, and what defines it operationally?**
→ *Teaching point:* Chronic ethanol exposure **downregulates and reconfigures GABA-A receptor subunits** (reduced benzodiazepine-sensitive α1-containing populations) while **upregulating NMDA-receptor signaling**; benzodiazepines are positive allosteric modulators that need endogenous GABA tone and intact receptor sensitivity, so their ceiling falls exactly when glutamatergic excitotoxicity surges. Operationally, resistance is commonly flagged when escalating doses (e.g., >40 mg diazepam-equivalents in the first hour, or hundreds of mg per day) fail to control symptoms [2,5]. This rationale underlies barbiturates (direct, GABA-independent chloride-channel opening plus AMPA/kainate antagonism) and glutamate-modulating adjuncts [2,5,6].

**Q4. Where does phenobarbital fit in 2026 — what does the evidence actually support?**
→ *Teaching point:* Evidence has matured but stays nuanced. A 2026 multicenter target-trial emulation (~24,600 inpatients) found **no difference between phenobarbital and diazepam initiation in length of stay or mechanical ventilation**, suggesting near-equivalence against a long-acting benzodiazepine comparator [7]. A 2026 ICU meta-analysis found shorter ICU stay with phenobarbital pathways and hinted (very low certainty) that **front-loaded, protocolized phenobarbital-first pathways** do best [6]. ED meta-analytic data show no clear superiority over benzodiazepines [8]. Synthesis: phenobarbital is a **reasonable monotherapy alternative or rescue agent for benzodiazepine-resistant withdrawal, best deployed protocolized and front-loaded** (e.g., weight-based ~10 mg/kg IBW loading or 130–260 mg IV aliquots), with caution stacking it on large benzodiazepine debt — respiratory depression is synergistic and it has no reversal agent [5,6,7,8,9].

**Q5. Design the adjunct strategy for her DT: dexmedetomidine, ketamine, antipsychotics, anticonvulsants — what does each add, and what does none of them replace?**
→ *Teaching point:* **Dexmedetomidine** (α2-agonist) smooths autonomic storm and permits lighter sedation but **does not prevent seizures or treat the underlying GABA deficit** — never monotherapy. **Ketamine** (NMDA antagonist) targets the glutamatergic arm and has benzodiazepine-sparing signal in small studies. **Antipsychotics** (haloperidol) only for refractory hallucinosis/agitation — they lower seizure threshold and prolong QTc (hers is 465 ms: replete K/Mg first, monitor ECG). **Anticonvulsants** (gabapentin, carbamazepine/valproate) suit mild–moderate ambulatory withdrawal or as adjuncts, not DT. GABA-ergic agents remain the backbone; adjuncts are sculpting tools [1,2,5,6].

**Q6. She is febrile and delirious at 48 hours. Before attributing everything to DT, what must be excluded, and what supportive care is non-negotiable?**
→ *Teaching point:* DT in a pancreatitis patient is a diagnosis of **continuous re-exclusion**: necrotizing pancreatitis/abscess, aspiration pneumonia, meningitis, Wernicke encephalopathy, hepatic encephalopathy, hypoglycemia, and hyponatremia (careful correction — osmotic demyelination risk in malnourished drinkers). Non-negotiables: **high-dose parenteral thiamine before glucose** (treatment-dose dosing if Wernicke suspected — e.g., 500 mg IV TID), aggressive **Mg/K/Phos repletion** (watch refeeding), volume management balanced against pancreatitis, DVT prophylaxis, and a low threshold for airway protection with planned, not crash, intubation [1,2,10].

**Q7. Map the post-acute plan: what does kindling imply, and which relapse-prevention pharmacotherapy fits her?**
→ *Teaching point:* **Kindling** — each withdrawal episode sensitizes limbic/glutamatergic circuits, so subsequent episodes are more severe and seizure-prone; her trajectory (prior intubation → current DT) exemplifies it and must be documented prominently. Before discharge: initiate AUD pharmacotherapy — **naltrexone** (avoid if ongoing opioid analgesia is needed for pancreatitis; hepatic monitoring) vs **acamprosate** (renally cleared, fits hepatic disease) — plus addiction medicine referral and explicit warm handoff. An ICU DT admission without an AUD treatment plan is a system failure, not just a missed opportunity [1,2].

## Differential (ranked, with discriminators)

1. **Delirium tremens with emerging benzodiazepine resistance** — timeline, autonomic storm, fluctuating sensorium, escalating GABA-ergic requirements.
2. **Delirium from pancreatitis complications** (necrosis, sepsis, ARDS, abdominal compartment syndrome) — lipase trend, imaging, cultures, lactate; may coexist and co-drive.
3. **Wernicke encephalopathy** — ophthalmoplegia/nystagmus, ataxia, confusion; often partial; empiric treatment-dose thiamine is cheap insurance [10].
4. **Hepatic encephalopathy** — asterixis, ammonia (supportive only), stigmata of chronic liver disease; sedation-sensitive.
5. **Opioid-related delirium / polypharmacy** — hydromorphone burden, anticholinergics (hydroxyzine).
6. **Sedative-hypnotic withdrawal, hyponatremia/osmotic shifts, hypoglycemia, CNS infection** — history, BMP trend, glucose, LP if unexplained fever + meningismus.

## Workup & management (resident depth)

- **Monitoring & scales:** move from CIWA-Ar to mMINDS or RASS-targeted protocol in ICU; continuous telemetry, frequent neuro checks.
- **Labs/studies:** BMP q6–12h with Mg/Phos, LFTs, lipase trend, CBC, blood cultures + CXR for fever, ECG for QTc trend, ammonia only if hepatic encephalopathy genuinely suspected, CT head if focal signs/atypical seizure; consider CT abdomen for pancreatitis complications.
- **Pharmacologic sequence:** (1) front-loaded long-acting benzodiazepine (diazepam 10–20 mg IV q5–15 min to light sedation) with defined resistance threshold; (2) at resistance threshold, **protocolized phenobarbital** (130–260 mg IV aliquots q15–30 min or ~10 mg/kg IBW load) with airway readiness [5,6,7]; (3) **dexmedetomidine** for autonomic/agitation overlay, ± **ketamine** as benzodiazepine-sparing adjunct; (4) haloperidol only for refractory hallucinosis after QTc/electrolyte correction; (5) intubation + propofol (GABA-ergic — treats withdrawal) if airway or ventilation fails.
- **Supportive:** thiamine 500 mg IV TID ×3 days then de-escalate [10]; folate; Mg/K/Phos repletion; nutrition with refeeding precautions; quiet room, reorientation, family presence, sitter over restraints.
- **Documentation & hand-off:** label the chart with "history of complicated withdrawal — prior intubation, current DT"; kindling note for future admissions; AUD pharmacotherapy started before discharge with addiction referral.

## Facilitator notes (not for learner handout)

- **Timing:** Part 1 ~8 min (risk stratification, PAWSS logic, CIWA critique), Part 2 ~10 min (resistance mechanism, phenobarbital evidence — push residents to cite the 2026 target-trial emulation and ICU meta-analysis against each other), Part 3 ~10 min (adjunct design, exclusion discipline, disposition).
- **Common resident misconceptions:** (a) "phenobarbital is proven superior" — the 2026 JAMA IM emulation showed equivalence vs diazepam, not superiority [7]; (b) treating dexmedetomidine as withdrawal therapy (no seizure protection); (c) chasing a CIWA number in a delirious, opioid-treated patient; (d) thiamine at prophylactic dosing when Wernicke is on the differential; (e) stopping at withdrawal control without initiating AUD pharmacotherapy.
- **Evidence-appraisal thread:** have residents contrast observational target-trial emulation [7] vs conventional meta-analysis of nonrandomized studies [6,8] — confounding by indication, heterogeneity of "phenobarbital pathway" definitions, very-low-certainty subgroup signals. Good 5-minute journal-club moment.
- **Pivot questions:** "She's now on 300 mg phenobarbital + 120 mg diazepam-equivalents — the nurse asks for more lorazepam PRN. Response?" (stacking risk, switch strategy, airway plan). "Pancreatitis team wants NPO + fluids and objects to dexmedetomidine for bradycardia — negotiate." "If she had cirrhosis with ascites?" (lorazepam/oxazepam, acamprosate, avoid naltrexone).
- Keep dangerousness content framed around recognition, escalation, and safety planning.
