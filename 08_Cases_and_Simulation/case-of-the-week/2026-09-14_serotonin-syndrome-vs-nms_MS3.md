## Serotonin Syndrome vs. Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome: The Agitated, Febrile Patient on an Antidepressant

## PART 1 — LEARNER-FACING CASE

### Clinical stem

A 68-year-old is admitted to the medicine service for a diabetic foot infection. Home medications include **sertraline** (a *selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor*, or **SSRI** — an antidepressant that increases serotonin signaling), which was recently increased for depression. On hospital day 1 the team starts **linezolid**, an antibiotic chosen to cover a resistant organism. Linezolid is also a *weak monoamine oxidase inhibitor (MAOI)* — meaning it blocks the enzyme that breaks down serotonin.

On hospital day 3, nursing calls you: the patient is restless, sweating, and "won't stop moving their legs."

**Vitals:** T 38.7 °C (101.7 °F) · HR 122 · BP 162/92 · RR 22 · SpO₂ 97% on room air

**Exam:**
- **Mental status:** Anxious, restless, oriented to self and place but easily distracted.
- **Pupils:** Dilated (mydriasis) bilaterally.
- **Neuromuscular:** Coarse tremor. **Hyperreflexia** (overly brisk reflexes) and sustained **clonus** (rhythmic involuntary muscle jerks) at both ankles — *clearly more pronounced in the legs than the arms.* Muscle tone mildly increased in the legs but no "lead-pipe" stiffness.
- **Skin:** Flushed and diaphoretic (sweaty).
- **Abdomen:** Hyperactive bowel sounds; one episode of diarrhea this morning.

The patient has never taken an antipsychotic. There is no history of alcohol or illicit drug use, and the foot infection is improving on exam.

### Discussion questions

*Work through these as a group. Teaching points are in the separate Facilitator Guide (Part 2) — try to reason them out first.*

1. **What is the most likely diagnosis, and what classic triad of feature-clusters defines it?** Which two medications interacted to cause it?
2. **Which bedside neuromuscular findings point strongly toward this diagnosis** rather than its main mimic, neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS)?
3. **What validated bedside criteria support the diagnosis,** and which findings do they require?
4. **How would NMS look different?** Contrast the trigger drug class, speed of onset, reflexes, and muscle tone.
5. **What are your first management steps,** and which antidote can be considered if supportive care is not enough?
6. **When does this become an ICU-level emergency,** and what temperature threshold should trigger escalation?
7. **How could this episode have been prevented?** What should happen at every medication change?

## PART 2 — FACILITATOR GUIDE

*(Keep separate from the learner handout.)*

### Teaching points, question by question

**1. Diagnosis and triad.** This is **serotonin syndrome** (also called serotonin toxicity): the classic triad is **(1) altered mental status, (2) autonomic hyperactivity** (fever, tachycardia, hypertension, diaphoresis, mydriasis, hyperactive bowel sounds/diarrhea), and **(3) neuromuscular abnormalities** (tremor, hyperreflexia, clonus) in a patient with new or increased serotonergic drug exposure [1]. The trigger here is the **sertraline + linezolid** interaction — linezolid's MAO-inhibiting activity blocks serotonin breakdown while the SSRI blocks reuptake [3]. Onset is typically **rapid (within 24 hours of the provoking change)** [1] — though when an interacting drug such as linezolid is added, it can appear a day or more later, as here.

**2. The discriminating exam findings.** **Clonus and hyperreflexia, worse in the lower extremities**, are the most useful bedside pointers toward serotonin syndrome [1,2]. NMS produces the opposite neuromuscular picture: **diffuse "lead-pipe" rigidity with slowed or absent reflexes** [4]. Mydriasis and hyperactive bowel sounds also favor serotonin syndrome; NMS patients typically have normal pupils and normal or decreased bowel sounds [1].

**3. Hunter Serotonin Toxicity Criteria.** In a patient who has taken a serotonergic agent, the diagnosis is supported by any of: **spontaneous clonus**; **inducible clonus or ocular clonus PLUS agitation or diaphoresis**; **tremor plus hyperreflexia**; or **hypertonia with temperature > 38 °C plus clonus**. The Hunter rules were derived from >2,000 serotonergic overdose cases and are more sensitive (84%) and specific (97%) than the older Sternbach criteria [2]. This patient meets them several ways.

**4. Serotonin syndrome vs. NMS — the Step 2 CK table.**

| Feature | Serotonin syndrome | NMS |
|---|---|---|
| Trigger | Serotonergic agent added/increased (SSRI, SNRI, MAOI, linezolid, tramadol, triptans, ondansetron) [1,3] | Dopamine-blocking agent (antipsychotics, metoclopramide) or abrupt withdrawal of dopaminergic therapy [4] |
| Onset | Hours (usually < 24 h) [1] | Days (typically 1–2 weeks after starting/increasing) [4] |
| Neuromuscular | Hyperreflexia, clonus, tremor, myoclonus — legs > arms [1,2] | Lead-pipe rigidity, bradyreflexia [4] |
| Pupils | Dilated [1] | Normal |
| Bowel sounds | Hyperactive, diarrhea [1] | Normal or decreased |
| Resolution after stopping drug | Usually < 24–48 h [1] | Days to 2 weeks [4] |
| First treatment | Stop serotonergics; benzodiazepines, cooling; cyproheptadine if not responding [1] | Stop the dopamine blocker; ICU supportive care, cooling, benzodiazepines; dantrolene or bromocriptine considered in severe cases (case-series evidence only) |

**5. Management.** First: **stop all serotonergic agents** (here, hold linezolid — discuss an alternative antibiotic with ID — and hold sertraline). Then **supportive care**: IV fluids, continuous cardiac monitoring, external cooling for hyperthermia, and **benzodiazepines** for agitation and to blunt the neuromuscular hyperactivity [1]. For moderate-to-severe cases not responding to supportive care, **cyproheptadine** (an oral serotonin 5-HT2A antagonist) can be considered [1]. Antipyretics like acetaminophen do **not** help — the fever is from muscle activity, not a hypothalamic set-point change [1].

**6. Escalation and safety.** Severe serotonin toxicity — temperature **> 38.5 °C**, rising rigidity, or autonomic instability — warrants **ICU-level care with urgent active cooling and sedation, progressing to rapid-sequence intubation and neuromuscular paralysis** if hyperthermia cannot be controlled [1,5]. Complications of untreated severe cases include rhabdomyolysis, DIC, and multiorgan failure. Recognition and escalation, not memorizing rare complications, is the MS3-level skill.

**7. Prevention.** This was a **foreseeable drug–drug interaction**: linezolid carries a well-described serotonin syndrome risk in patients on SSRIs/SNRIs [3]. Prevention = **medication reconciliation at every transition**, interaction screening when any new agent is added (especially "hidden serotonergics": linezolid, tramadol, fentanyl, triptans, dextromethorphan, St. John's wort), and patient education about early symptoms [1,3].

### Ranked differential (top 5)

1. **Serotonin syndrome** — serotonergic exposure + triad + clonus/hyperreflexia (legs > arms), rapid onset. *Most likely.*
2. **Neuroleptic malignant syndrome** — argues against: no dopamine-blocking drug exposure, no lead-pipe rigidity, reflexes brisk rather than slowed [4].
3. **Sepsis / worsening infection with delirium** — plausible host, but improving wound, hyperreflexia and clonus are not explained by sepsis; still obtain cultures if any doubt.
4. **Anticholinergic toxicity** — mydriasis and fever overlap, but skin would be *dry* (not diaphoretic) and bowel sounds *absent* (not hyperactive).
5. **Alcohol or sedative withdrawal** — tremor, autonomic arousal overlap; no history, and clonus/hyperreflexia pattern fits serotonin toxicity better.

### Workup & management (summary)

- **Diagnosis is clinical** — no confirmatory lab test [1].
- Labs to assess severity/complications: BMP (AKI), **CK** (rhabdomyolysis), CBC, LFTs, coags if severe; ECG; glucose; consider cultures and tox screen to address the differential.
- **Stop the offending agents → benzodiazepines + IV fluids + cooling → cyproheptadine if moderate-severe → ICU for T > 38.5 °C or instability** [1,5].
- Document the reaction prominently in the chart and counsel the patient before any future serotonergic prescribing.
