# Case of the Week — Recurrent Crises, Rapid Shifts, and a "Treatment-Resistant Bipolar" Label (Resident Version)

## Borderline Personality Disorder: Traits Versus Diagnosis, Trauma First, and Evidence-Based Management

**Learner level:** Psychiatry resident · **Date:** 2026-11-23 · **Format:** 20–30 min guided discussion
**Literature:** Based on articles retrieved from PubMed; see the numbered reference list at the end.

---

## Stem (learner-facing)

**Part 1 — Consult question.** You are the psychiatry resident consulted in the emergency department for Mr. R, a 24-year-old graduate student, after superficial self-inflicted forearm injuries following his partner's threat to leave. The chart lists "bipolar disorder, treatment-resistant" and shows four emergency visits in 14 months, each after an interpersonal rupture, plus discontinued trials of lamotrigine and divalproex ("didn't work"), quetiapine ("too sedating"), and three antidepressants. Current medications: sertraline, aripiprazole, hydroxyzine, and trazodone, prescribed by three different clinicians.

**Part 2 — Interview.** Mr. R describes mood that shifts over hours — rage, despair, anxiety — nearly always triggered by perceived rejection, superimposed on chronic emptiness since his mid-teens. Relationships begin as idealized fusions and collapse within months into contempt and crisis. He reports impulsive spending and weekend binge drinking, identity diffusion ("I reinvent myself for whoever I'm with"), recurrent self-injury since age 15 that he describes as "the only thing that turns the volume down," and brief paranoid ideation under stress that clears within a day. He has never had a sustained period of decreased need for sleep with increased goal-directed activity; "elevated" periods last an afternoon and follow good news. Childhood history includes emotional neglect and physical intimidation at home, which he has never discussed with a clinician. He is in his second year of a demanding program, functioning well academically between crises.

**Part 3 — Risk data.** He voices a passive death wish ("I'd rather be dead than alone") but denies current intent or plan; he has no history of medically serious suicide attempts, though he minimizes the seriousness of past overdoses "to make the feelings stop" during two prior breakups. He is sober tonight, future-oriented ("I have a thesis meeting Friday"), engages readily, and asks for "something that actually works this time." His sister can stay with him this week.

## Guided discussion

**Q1. Make the case for and against the chart's bipolar diagnosis. What discriminates?**
→ *Teaching point:* Discriminators are time course, trigger, and polarity structure: borderline-pattern affective instability cycles within hours to a day, is interpersonally cued, and rides on chronic dysphoria/emptiness; bipolar episodes are sustained (days to weeks), often autonomous, and carry concurrent changes in sleep need, energy, and activity. Mood-stabilizer non-response and a "treatment-resistant" label across multiple classes should raise diagnostic suspicion rather than prompt a fourth agent. Note also that transient stress-related paranoid ideation or severe dissociation is itself part of the DSM-5-TR borderline criterion set — brief psychotic-spectrum symptoms do not force a primary psychotic or psychotic-depression diagnosis [1,2].

**Q2. You recognize the borderline pattern tonight. Do you diagnose borderline personality disorder tonight? What goes in the chart?**
→ *Teaching point:* Recognize the pattern; resist the single-encounter diagnosis. A personality disorder is a pervasive, inflexible, longitudinally stable construct, and a cross-sectional crisis presentation — however criterion-rich — cannot establish pervasiveness or stability, and systematically oversamples the patient at his worst. Three further reasons for restraint here: (1) an unexamined trauma history (emotional neglect, physical intimidation) that could drive much of the picture; (2) the demonstrated harm of premature labels in this very chart ("treatment-resistant bipolar" cost him five medication trials); (3) stigma that follows a personality diagnosis through every subsequent emergency encounter. Document **"borderline personality traits"** — listing the observed criteria verbatim — alongside the trauma history and the untreated differential, and reserve the formal diagnosis for the longitudinal treatment relationship. Our departmental convention: the official diagnosis belongs to a provider who has known the patient over an extended course, on the order of two years, with the pattern confirmed across contexts and collateral — not to a first-visit consultant. Semistructured instruments support, but do not replace, that longitudinal confirmation [1].

**Q3. He discloses childhood emotional neglect and physical intimidation. How does trauma reshape your differential and your sequencing?**
→ *Teaching point:* Trauma moves up the differential, not into a footnote. Posttraumatic stress disorder and the ICD-11 complex posttraumatic stress disorder construct share affect dysregulation, negative self-concept, self-injury, and dissociation with the borderline pattern; complex posttraumatic stress disorder is anchored to re-experiencing, avoidance, and threat-hyperarousal with a stable (if negative) self-image, whereas the borderline pattern shows abandonment panic, idealization–devaluation cycling, and identity diffusion. They can co-occur, and adverse childhood experience is a potent risk factor for both [1,2]. Practical sequencing: complete a structured trauma assessment before settling any personality diagnosis; treat trauma-related disorders on their own merits; and choose the initial psychotherapy frame (dysregulation-focused first, trauma-focused deliberately sequenced) rather than letting the first label in the chart decide. An honest formulation tonight: "borderline traits in the context of significant developmental trauma; trauma-related disorder to be formally evaluated."

**Q4. The attending asks whether this presentation is "atypical in a man." Is it?**
→ *Teaching point:* It is not. In Wave 2 of the National Epidemiologic Survey on Alcohol and Related Conditions (34,653 adults), lifetime prevalence showed no sex difference — 5.6% in men versus 6.2% in women [3]. The female predominance of clinical samples reflects help-seeking and ascertainment, not population reality: men's presentations are more often routed to substance-use or antisocial labels, or dismissed as anger, and they reach evidence-based psychotherapy less often. Keeping the traits-versus-diagnosis discipline is doubly important in men, where confirmation bias runs in both directions — under-recognition of the pattern and over-attribution of impulsivity to character or intoxication.

**Q5. How would you formulate his self-injury functionally, and what does that imply for risk stratification tonight?**
→ *Teaching point:* His self-injury is affect-regulatory ("turns the volume down") — negatively reinforced escape from aversive states — which is distinct from, though not protective against, suicidal intent; lifetime suicide mortality in this population is substantial, so chronic risk is never trivial. Stratify as chronic elevated baseline risk with identifiable acute-on-chronic escalators: comorbid major depression, substance intoxication, recent discharge, loss events, access to means. Tonight: no intent, future-orientation, engaged help-seeking, available support — favors structured outpatient disposition with an explicit, collaboratively written safety plan (warning signs → internal strategies → social contacts → professional contacts → means restriction) over admission. Document the reasoning, not just the conclusion. Prolonged or coercive hospitalization lacks evidence of benefit and can reinforce crisis-contingent care; brief admission is reserved for acute escalations that outpatient structure cannot hold [1,2].

**Q6. What is the comparative evidence for the specialized psychotherapies — and does treatment have to wait for a confirmed diagnosis?**
→ *Teaching point:* The 2020 Cochrane review (75 randomized trials, ~4,500 participants) found psychotherapy superior to treatment as usual for symptom severity (standardized mean difference −0.52, clinically meaningful on the Zanarini scale), with smaller effects on self-harm, suicide-related outcomes, and functioning; dialectical behavior therapy and mentalization-based treatment have the deepest trial bases, but subgroup analyses show no clear superiority of any brand-name therapy [4]. McMain's landmark trial found dialectical behavior therapy and general psychiatric management — a structured, guideline-informed generalist model — equivalent across suicidal and self-injury outcomes at one year [5]. Treatment targets the dysregulation and the self-injury, not the label: referral is appropriate now, at the traits stage, and access and fit should drive the choice of program. A well-structured generalist frame (consistent clinician, crisis plan, here-and-now focus) is a legitimate evidence-based offer where specialty programs have waitlists [1,4,5].

**Q7. The emergency physician asks you to "adjust his meds." What does the pharmacotherapy evidence actually support, and how do you rationalize the four-drug regimen?**
→ *Teaching point:* The 2022 Cochrane review of pharmacological interventions (46 randomized trials) found no medication class with convincing benefit on any primary outcome — symptom severity, self-harm, suicide-related outcomes, or functioning — with mostly very-low-certainty evidence; no agent carries regulatory approval for this indication [6]. Yet polypharmacy like Mr. R's is the norm in clinical practice [6]. Plan: target prescribing to verified comorbid syndromes only (screen formally for major depression, posttraumatic stress disorder, and alcohol use disorder); consolidate and taper redundant agents sequentially with consent; for acute crisis states, short courses of a low-potency antipsychotic such as quetiapine, or a sedative antihistamine, are preferred over benzodiazepines, which disinhibit and carry misuse liability here [1]. Frame deprescribing as treatment, not withdrawal of care.

**Q8. Design his discharge plan tonight — including exactly what your assessment says.**
→ *Teaching point:* (1) Written safety plan + lethal-means counseling with his sister present; (2) referral to a structured psychotherapy program (dialectical behavior therapy or equivalent) and, given waitlist reality, bridge with structured generalist follow-up with one named prescriber-clinician who can make the longitudinal diagnostic call; (3) medication reconciliation note recommending sequential taper of redundant agents and no new agents absent a verified comorbid syndrome; (4) formal screening for major depressive disorder, posttraumatic stress disorder, and alcohol use disorder at first outpatient visit; (5) psychoeducation that names the pattern honestly and hopefully — emotion dysregulation with high remission rates — without prematurely fixing the personality label; (6) follow-up scheduled before he leaves, within one week; (7) assessment documents "borderline personality traits" with observed criteria, the trauma history, the chronic-versus-acute risk formulation, and the plan for longitudinal diagnostic clarification.

## Differential (ranked, with discriminating features)

1. **Borderline personality traits (working impression; formal personality disorder diagnosis deferred to longitudinal assessment)** — pervasive adolescence-onset pattern: abandonment panic, idealization–devaluation cycles, identity diffusion, affect-regulatory self-injury, hour-scale reactive mood shifts, chronic emptiness, transient stress-related paranoia. Best cross-sectional fit by criterion count and course; pervasiveness and stability still require longitudinal and collateral confirmation.
2. **Complex posttraumatic stress disorder / posttraumatic stress disorder** — ranked immediately behind, and assessed first, given disclosed developmental trauma: overlapping dysregulation, self-injury, dissociation; discriminated by re-experiencing/avoidance/hyperarousal and stable negative self-concept rather than identity diffusion; frequently co-occurs [1,2].
3. **Bipolar II disorder** — requires sustained hypomania with decreased sleep need and increased activity; absent here. The most consequential misdiagnosis in this population — anchor to episode duration and autonomy [1].
4. **Major depressive disorder, recurrent (comorbid)** — present in a large majority lifetime; verify sustained neurovegetative episodes; treat on its own merits if confirmed [1].
5. **Substance-induced mood instability / alcohol use disorder** — binge pattern may be impulsivity criterion, independent disorder, or both; sobriety-phase reassessment clarifies — and in men this is often the only label recorded, hiding the underlying pattern [3].
6. **Cyclothymic disorder** — chronic subthreshold mood oscillation, but lacks the interpersonal reactivity, identity disturbance, and self-injury axis.

## Workup & management

- **Workup:** longitudinal timeline with collateral (the single highest-yield maneuver); structured trauma assessment before any personality diagnosis is settled; structured screens for mood and substance use disorders; medication history audit for diagnostic drift; toxicology in acute presentations; routine labs (including thyroid studies) only to exclude contributors; no biomarker confirms the impression. Semistructured diagnostic interviews support the eventual longitudinal diagnosis [1].
- **Acute:** wound care; structured risk assessment distinguishing chronic from acute-on-chronic drivers; collaborative safety plan + means restriction; crisis-only, time-limited prescribing if needed (low-potency antipsychotic or sedative antihistamine; avoid benzodiazepines) [1]; brief admission only for uncontainable acute risk.
- **Documentation discipline:** traits, observed criteria, trauma history, and open differential tonight; the formal personality diagnosis belongs to the longitudinal provider (departmental convention: an established relationship on the order of two years, pattern confirmed across contexts).
- **Longitudinal:** evidence-based psychotherapy referral (dialectical behavior therapy, mentalization-based treatment, transference-focused psychotherapy, or structured general psychiatric management — no brand demonstrably superior) [4,5]; deprescribing plan toward monotherapy-or-none for core symptoms [6]; treat verified comorbidity, including trauma-related disorders, on its own merits; early-intervention mindset in young patients [2]; expect and communicate high remission rates with reduced-but-persistent functional impairment, making vocational/functional recovery an explicit target [2].

## Facilitator notes (not for learner distribution)

- **Timing:** ~30 minutes. Open with Part 1 and ask, "What's your prior that this is bipolar disorder?" — let residents commit before Part 2 disconfirms.
- **The deliberate center of this version is Q2–Q4:** diagnostic restraint as an active clinical skill. Push residents to articulate why "criteria met tonight" ≠ "diagnosis made tonight": pervasiveness/stability cannot be observed cross-sectionally, trauma has not been assessed, premature labels already harmed this patient once, and the formal diagnosis belongs to the longitudinal relationship (our convention: ~two years with one provider). Also make them say the prevalence numbers out loud — 5.6% of men, 6.2% of women [3] — and discuss why their mental image of this diagnosis is probably female.
- **Push depth elsewhere:** demand the discriminators (duration, autonomy, sleep/energy signature); ask who can state the Cochrane effect size for psychotherapy and the null pharmacotherapy result from memory; probe whether anyone can articulate general psychiatric management's components, since most residents cite only dialectical behavior therapy [4,5].
- **Countertransference block:** name it explicitly — "manipulative" reframed as operant affect regulation; discuss team splitting in the emergency department and how a single documented formulation protects the patient from plan-by-countertransference.
- **Deprescribing skills:** role-play telling a patient a medication list will shrink; residents rarely practice this conversation.
- **Common misconceptions:** one encounter suffices for a personality diagnosis (traits now, diagnosis longitudinally); trauma assessment can wait (it reorders the differential); this is a women's disorder (community prevalence is equal [3]); admission is the default for any death wish (formulate chronic vs. acute-on-chronic); benzodiazepines for crisis (disinhibition/misuse risk); "borderline" as untreatable (remission is the expected outcome) [1,2].
- **Safety framing:** keep all self-harm content at recognition, escalation, and safety-planning level; no method or lethality detail in discussion or notes shared with learners.
