# Case of the Week — Recurrent Crises, Rapid Shifts, and a "Treatment-Resistant Bipolar" Label (Resident Version)

## Borderline Personality Disorder: Traits Versus Diagnosis, Trauma-Informed Assessment, and Evidence-Based Management

**Learner level:** Psychiatry resident · **Date:** 2026-11-23 · **Format:** 20–30 min guided discussion
**Literature:** Based on articles retrieved from PubMed; see the numbered reference list at the end.

---

## Stem (learner-facing)

**Part 1 — Consult question.** You are the psychiatry resident consulted in the emergency department for Mr. R, a 24-year-old graduate student, after superficial self-inflicted forearm injuries following his partner's threat to leave. The chart lists "bipolar disorder, treatment-resistant" and shows four emergency visits in 14 months, each after an interpersonal rupture, plus discontinued trials of lamotrigine and divalproex ("didn't work"), quetiapine ("too sedating"), and three antidepressants. Current medications: sertraline, aripiprazole, hydroxyzine, and trazodone, prescribed by three different clinicians.

**Part 2 — Interview.** Mr. R describes mood that shifts over hours — rage, despair, anxiety — nearly always triggered by perceived rejection, superimposed on chronic emptiness since his mid-teens. Relationships begin as idealized fusions and collapse within months into contempt and crisis. He reports impulsive spending and weekend binge drinking, identity diffusion ("I reinvent myself for whoever I'm with"), recurrent self-injury since age 15 that he describes as "the only thing that turns the volume down," and brief paranoid ideation under stress that clears within a day. He has never had a sustained period of decreased need for sleep with increased goal-directed activity; "elevated" periods last an afternoon and follow good news. Childhood history includes emotional neglect and physical intimidation at home, which he has never discussed with a clinician. He is in his second year of a demanding program, functioning well academically between crises.

**Part 3 — Risk data.** He voices a passive death wish ("I'd rather be dead than alone") but denies current intent or plan; he reports two prior potentially suicidal episodes during breakups; intent, medical severity and corroborating records remain unclear. He is sober tonight, future-oriented ("I have a thesis meeting Friday"), engages readily, and asks for "something that actually works this time." His sister can stay with him this week.

## Guided discussion

**Q1. Make the case for and against the chart's bipolar diagnosis. What discriminates?**
→ *Teaching point:* Discriminators are time course, trigger, and polarity structure: borderline-pattern affective instability cycles within hours to a day, is interpersonally cued, and rides on chronic dysphoria/emptiness; bipolar episodes are sustained (days to weeks), often autonomous, and carry concurrent changes in sleep need, energy, and activity. Prior medication response cannot confirm or exclude bipolar disorder. Reassess the history behind the “treatment-resistant” label before escalating treatment. Note also that transient stress-related paranoid ideation or severe dissociation is itself part of the DSM-5-TR borderline criterion set — brief psychotic-spectrum symptoms do not force a primary psychotic or psychotic-depression diagnosis [1,2].

**Q2. You recognize the borderline pattern tonight. Do you diagnose borderline personality disorder tonight? What goes in the chart?**
→ *Teaching point:* Record the pattern suggested by the history, why the bipolar formulation warrants re-evaluation, and the evidence that remains missing. Document observed and reported features and possible borderline personality traits as a working impression. Establish persistence, pervasiveness and impairment across settings using prior records, collateral and follow-up where available. Do not convert a crisis-only impression into a diagnosis or impose a fixed duration when an adequate evaluation already establishes the pattern [1].

**Q3. He discloses childhood emotional neglect and physical intimidation. How does trauma reshape your differential and your sequencing?**
→ *Teaching point:* Reported neglect and intimidation warrant a trauma-informed assessment, not a diagnosis in themselves. Assess trauma-related conditions alongside mood, substance use and personality patterns. Re-experiencing, avoidance and a persistent sense of threat help clarify PTSD; identity disturbance and relationship patterns inform the broader differential, not a categorical separator. The conditions can coexist. Clarify the history at a pace the patient can tolerate without requiring one diagnosis to be ruled out first [1,2].

**Q4. The attending asks whether this presentation is "atypical in a man." Is it?**
→ *Teaching point:* No. Grant et al. reported lifetime estimates of 5.6% in men and 6.2% in women, with no statistically significant sex difference in their DSM-IV survey of 34,653 US adults [3]. That survey does not establish causes of clinical presentation or referral differences. Assess the pattern across genders rather than leaning on a stereotype.

**Q5. How would you formulate his self-injury functionally, and what does that imply for risk stratification tonight?**
→ *Teaching point:* Explore the function of self-injury without treating it as evidence against suicidal intent. Clarify prior potentially suicidal episodes, current thoughts, intent, plans and access to means; reassess after acute changes and seek corroboration. Engagement, future orientation and offered support may favor discharge only if acute-on-chronic risk is assessed, support is verified, the safety plan is feasible and follow-up is arranged. Escalate when acute risk cannot be safely managed outside hospital. The label, apparent calm and denial of intent are not sufficient [1,2].

**Q6. What is the comparative evidence for the specialized psychotherapies — and does treatment have to wait for a confirmed diagnosis?**
→ *Teaching point:* The 2020 Cochrane review found psychotherapy may improve BPD symptom severity compared with usual care, with less certain evidence for other outcomes [4]. In McMain’s 180-participant trial, both DBT and structured general psychiatric management improved suicidal and self-injury outcomes; no statistically significant between-group difference was found at one year. This does not establish equivalence [5]. Offer structured help for the problems already identified while diagnostic clarification continues, weighing access, fit and patient preferences [1,4,5].

**Q7. The emergency physician asks you to "adjust his meds." What does the pharmacotherapy evidence actually support, and how do you rationalize the four-drug regimen?**
→ *Teaching point:* The 2022 Cochrane review found no convincing medication benefit on core BPD outcomes, with mostly low- or very-low-certainty evidence [6]. Reconcile prescriptions with the patient and prescribers; review indications, benefit, sedation, interactions and substances. With consent and follow-up, taper unnecessary agents one at a time when appropriate. Do not recommend a named sedative or antipsychotic as the default crisis response. Any time-limited medication should address a specific, measurable target after individualized risk–benefit discussion, with a stop or reassessment plan, and remain an adjunct to psychotherapy [1,6].

**Q8. Design his discharge plan tonight — including exactly what your assessment says.**
→ *Teaching point:* If reassessment supports discharge, confirm the sister’s availability with his permission. Co-write a practical safety plan, address lethal-means safety, arrange timely follow-up before departure and hand off to a named clinician. Give a fallback plan if support or follow-up falls through or acute risk changes. Document observed findings, reported trauma, diagnostic uncertainty, acute-versus-baseline risk reasoning, and who will reassess diagnosis and medication. If the plan is not feasible or risk not safely manageable, reassess disposition [1,2].

## Differential (ranked, with discriminating features)

1. **Borderline personality traits (working impression; formal personality disorder diagnosis deferred to longitudinal assessment)** — pervasive adolescence-onset pattern: abandonment panic, idealization–devaluation cycles, identity diffusion, affect-regulatory self-injury, hour-scale reactive mood shifts, chronic emptiness, transient stress-related paranoia. Best cross-sectional fit by criterion count and course; pervasiveness and stability still require longitudinal and collateral confirmation.
2. **Complex posttraumatic stress disorder / posttraumatic stress disorder** — evaluate alongside the longitudinal personality and mood differential given reported developmental adversity: overlapping dysregulation, self-injury, dissociation; assess re-experiencing, avoidance and persistent threat along with self-concept and relationship patterns; frequently co-occurs [1,2].
3. **Bipolar II disorder** — requires sustained hypomania with decreased sleep need and increased activity; absent here. The most consequential misdiagnosis in this population — anchor to episode duration and autonomy [1].
4. **Major depressive disorder, recurrent (comorbid)** — verify sustained depressive episodes and associated changes in sleep, energy and function; treat on its own merits if confirmed [1].
5. **Substance-induced mood instability / alcohol use disorder** — binge pattern may be impulsivity criterion, independent disorder, or both; sobriety-phase reassessment clarifies — and in men this is often the only label recorded, hiding the underlying pattern [3].
6. **Cyclothymic disorder** — chronic subthreshold mood oscillation, but lacks the interpersonal reactivity, identity disturbance, and self-injury axis.

## Workup & management

- **Workup:** longitudinal timeline with collateral (the single highest-yield maneuver); trauma-informed assessment alongside competing and co-occurring diagnoses; structured screens for mood and substance use disorders; medication history audit for diagnostic drift; toxicology in acute presentations; routine labs (including thyroid studies) only to exclude contributors; no biomarker confirms the impression. Semistructured diagnostic interviews support the eventual longitudinal diagnosis [1].
- **Acute:** wound care; structured risk assessment distinguishing chronic from acute-on-chronic drivers; collaborative safety plan + means restriction; verify available supports and choose the safest feasible level of care; no default crisis medication.
- **Documentation discipline:** traits, observed criteria, trauma history, and open differential tonight; confirm diagnosis only when the evidence is adequate, using available longitudinal records and collateral.
- **Longitudinal:** evidence-based psychotherapy referral (dialectical behavior therapy, mentalization-based treatment, transference-focused psychotherapy, or structured general psychiatric management; choose with the patient based on evidence, fit and access) [4,5]; review and simplify unnecessary medications collaboratively [6]; treat verified comorbidity, including trauma-related disorders, on its own merits; early-intervention mindset in young patients [2]; communicate that meaningful symptomatic improvement is possible while functional impairment may persist, making vocational/functional recovery an explicit target [2].
