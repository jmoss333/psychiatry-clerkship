# Case of the Week: The Shaky Hands and the Ibuprofen — MS3 Version

## Lithium — Monitoring, Toxicity, and Interactions: Case of the Week (2026-11-09)

*De-identified synthetic teaching case. No real patient details. Based on articles retrieved from PubMed.*

---

## Stem (learner-facing)

A 34-year-old woman with bipolar I disorder, stable for two years on lithium 900 mg nightly, is brought to the emergency department by her partner. Over the past four days she has had a diarrheal illness with poor oral intake. Three days ago she strained her back moving boxes and began taking ibuprofen 600 mg three times daily.

Today she seems "off": she is nauseated, has vomited twice, is unsteady on her feet, and her speech sounds slurred. Her partner noticed that her usual mild hand tremor has become a coarse, obvious shake.

**Vitals:** T 37.1°C, HR 104, BP 102/66, RR 16, SpO2 99% on room air.

**Exam:** Alert but slow to answer, oriented to person and place but not date. Coarse tremor of both hands, ataxic gait, hyperreflexia at the knees. No rigidity, no clonus, no fever.

**Initial labs:** Na 148, K 4.1, BUN 34, Cr 1.6 (baseline 0.8). Lithium level drawn on arrival: **2.4 mEq/L**.

---

## Guided discussion questions

**Q1. What is the most likely diagnosis, and what in the history put her at risk?**

→ *Teaching point:* This is lithium toxicity. Lithium has a narrow therapeutic index — the toxic level sits close to the therapeutic level (roughly 0.6–1.2 mEq/L for maintenance), so small changes in drug handling cause toxicity [1,2]. Lithium is renally cleared and handled by the kidney like sodium. Anything that causes volume depletion or reduced renal perfusion — here, diarrhea, poor intake, AND a new NSAID — makes the kidney reabsorb more sodium *and more lithium*, raising the level. Dehydration plus a new interacting drug is the classic setup [1,3].

**Q2. Which common medications raise lithium levels? Name at least three.**

→ *Teaching point:* The high-yield list: **NSAIDs** (ibuprofen, naproxen), **thiazide diuretics**, and **ACE inhibitors/ARBs**. Dehydration and low-sodium states act the same way. In primary care and the ED, always check a patient's medication list for lithium before adding these [1,3]. Aspirin and sulindac have less effect; acetaminophen is the safer analgesic choice here.

**Q3. How do the signs of lithium toxicity progress from mild to severe?**

→ *Teaching point:* Think "GI first, then neuro." Mild: nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, fine tremor becoming coarse. Moderate: ataxia, slurred speech, confusion, hyperreflexia. Severe: delirium, seizures, coma, cardiac conduction problems (bradycardia, QT changes) [2,3]. A *coarse* tremor in a lithium-treated patient is toxicity until proven otherwise — a *fine* tremor can be a normal side effect [1].

**Q4. Does the serum lithium level alone tell you how sick she is?**

→ *Teaching point:* No. The level supports the diagnosis, but severity is judged clinically — especially in **chronic** toxicity (toxicity on stable long-term dosing), where tissue stores are already loaded and serious neurotoxicity can occur at levels barely above therapeutic. In contrast, a large **acute** ingestion in a lithium-naive person can produce a high level with initially mild symptoms [2]. Treat the patient, not the number.

**Q5. What are the first management steps in the ED?**

→ *Teaching point:* (1) **Stop lithium** (and the NSAID). (2) **IV isotonic fluids** — restoring volume and sodium is the main "antidote," because it restores renal lithium clearance. (3) **Serial lithium levels** every 4–6 hours (levels can rise as the gut absorbs more or as fluids redistribute drug). (4) Check electrolytes, renal function, ECG. (5) Activated charcoal does **not** bind lithium [2].

**Q6. When is hemodialysis used?**

→ *Teaching point:* You don't need exact thresholds as an MS3 — know the concept: dialysis removes lithium efficiently and is considered when the level is very high, when there is severe neurotoxicity (depressed consciousness, seizures), or when kidneys can't clear the drug (renal failure) [2]. Call nephrology early for any severely symptomatic patient.

**Q7. She recovers. What routine monitoring does every patient on lithium need?**

→ *Teaching point:* Before and during treatment: **lithium level** (12-hour trough, ~5–7 days after any dose change, then roughly every 3–6 months once stable), **renal function**, **thyroid function (TSH)**, **calcium** (lithium can cause hyperparathyroidism), weight, and a **pregnancy test** when applicable [1,4]. Long-term lithium is associated with hypothyroidism and chronic kidney disease, which is why this monitoring is lifelong [4,5].

## Differential (ranked)

1. **Lithium toxicity (chronic, precipitated by volume depletion + NSAID)** — coarse tremor, ataxia, confusion, GI prodrome, level 2.4, rising creatinine. Best fit.
2. **Serotonin syndrome** — also tremor/hyperreflexia, but needs a serotonergic agent, and typically features clonus, fever, and rapid onset. No serotonergic drug here.
3. **Neuroleptic malignant syndrome** — rigidity ("lead pipe"), high fever, autonomic instability after dopamine antagonist exposure. Absent here.
4. **Sepsis/metabolic encephalopathy from the GI illness** — possible contributor; check glucose, infection workup — but doesn't explain coarse tremor and the level of 2.4.
5. **Hypernatremic dehydration alone** — Na 148 contributes to confusion but not to tremor/hyperreflexia of this pattern.

## Workup & management

- **Confirm:** serum lithium (repeat q4–6h), BMP, Cr/BUN, Ca, TSH, ECG, urinalysis; glucose and infection workup for the diarrheal illness.
- **Treat:** hold lithium and ibuprofen; isotonic IV fluids; correct electrolytes; telemetry if ECG changes or level high.
- **Escalate:** nephrology consult for possible hemodialysis if severe neurotoxicity, very high or rising levels, or renal failure [2].
- **Before discharge:** medication counseling — sick-day rules (hold lithium during vomiting/diarrhea/poor intake and call), avoid NSAIDs without checking, maintain steady salt and fluid intake; arrange outpatient level recheck and psychiatry follow-up.

## Facilitator notes

- **Timing:** 20–30 minutes. Reveal the stem in two stages (history/exam first, then labs) and ask learners to commit to a differential before showing the level.
- **Common misconceptions:** (a) "The level defines toxicity" — use Q4 to dismantle this; (b) "Charcoal helps" — it doesn't bind lithium; (c) confusing the fine tremor of therapeutic lithium with the coarse tremor of toxicity; (d) forgetting that OTC ibuprofen counts as a drug interaction.
- **Pivot questions:** "What would change if she were on lisinopril instead of ibuprofen?" (same mechanism class of interaction); "What if her level were 1.0 but she were obtunded?" (chronic toxicity — treat the patient).
- **Safety framing:** If learners raise intentional overdose, keep discussion at the level of recognition, medical stabilization, one-to-one observation, and psychiatric safety assessment — do not discuss ingestion amounts or methods.
