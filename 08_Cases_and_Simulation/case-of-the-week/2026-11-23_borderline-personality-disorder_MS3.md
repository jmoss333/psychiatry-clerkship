# Case of the Week — The Patient Who Keeps Coming Back (MS3 Version)

## Borderline Personality Disorder: Recognizing the Pattern Without Rushing the Label

**Learner level:** MS3 (psychiatry clerkship) · **Date:** 2026-11-23 · **Format:** 20–30 min guided discussion
**Literature:** Based on articles retrieved from PubMed; see the numbered reference list at the end.

---

## Stem (learner-facing)

**Part 1 — Initial presentation.** Mr. R is a 24-year-old graduate student brought to the emergency department by a roommate after the roommate found him with superficial self-inflicted injuries to his forearm following an argument with his partner, who had threatened to end the relationship. This is his fourth emergency visit in 14 months; each followed an interpersonal conflict. He tells the team, "Everyone leaves me eventually — I'd rather be dead than alone," but a few minutes later is calm, joking with staff, and asking to be discharged so he can "fix things" with his partner.

**Part 2 — Further history.** Mr. R describes his mood as "all over the place, every day" — intense anger, anxiety, or despair lasting hours and usually triggered by something interpersonal. He describes chronic feelings of emptiness, a long pattern of relationships that begin as "perfect" and collapse within months, impulsive spending and episodes of binge drinking, and uncertainty about his goals and identity ("I don't know who I am"). He mentions, without elaborating, that "home wasn't a safe place growing up." During a past breakup he briefly felt that people were plotting against him; this resolved within a day. He has previously been told he has "bipolar disorder," but trials of two mood stabilizers did not help. He reports no sustained week-long periods of elevated mood, decreased need for sleep, or increased goal-directed activity. He sleeps and eats normally between crises and enjoys his friends and schoolwork when things are calm.

**Part 3 — Exam and course.** Vital signs are normal. He is cooperative, well-groomed, with full-range affect that shifts rapidly during the interview. He denies current intent to end his life, identifies his roommate and sister as supports, and asks for help "not falling apart every time something goes wrong." Toxicology is negative. The wounds are dressed; they do not require sutures.

## Guided discussion

**Q1. What pattern in this presentation points away from a primary mood disorder and toward a personality-level pattern?**
→ *Teaching point:* The mood shifts here are rapid (hours, not weeks), reactive to interpersonal triggers, and occur on a background of chronic emptiness, unstable relationships, identity disturbance, and recurrent crisis behavior dating to adolescence. Bipolar disorder requires discrete, sustained episodes (mania ≥1 week or any duration if hospitalization is required; hypomania ≥4 days) with changes in sleep, energy, and activity. Affective instability that cycles within a single day around relationship events is far more typical of borderline pathology [1].

**Q2. Which features of borderline personality disorder can you identify in Mr. R's story — and what should you actually write in today's chart?**
→ *Teaching point:* Map the story to the diagnostic criteria: frantic efforts to avoid abandonment; unstable, intense relationships alternating between idealization and devaluation; identity disturbance; impulsivity in at least two areas (spending, alcohol); recurrent suicidal or self-injurious behavior; affective instability; chronic emptiness; inappropriate intense anger; and transient stress-related paranoid ideation. Five or more are required, and Mr. R shows nearly all nine — **but recognizing the pattern and making the official diagnosis are two different acts.** A personality disorder is by definition a pervasive, stable, longitudinal pattern, and a single crisis encounter cannot establish that. Document "borderline personality **traits**" with the specific behaviors you observed, and leave the formal diagnosis to a clinician who knows the patient longitudinally — in this clerkship we teach reserving the official diagnosis for a provider who has followed the patient over an extended relationship, on the order of two years, rather than making it in the emergency department [1]. Labels written in a chart tonight follow patients for years.

**Q3. Why does his comment that "home wasn't a safe place growing up" change how cautious you are with the label?**
→ *Teaching point:* Adverse childhood experiences are strongly associated with this presentation [1,2] — and trauma-related conditions (posttraumatic stress disorder and complex trauma presentations) can produce affect dysregulation, self-injury, dissociation, and interpersonal turmoil that look very similar in a single cross-section. Assess the trauma history first and work it up properly before anchoring on a personality disorder; the two can also coexist. Practically: a trauma screen belongs in tonight's assessment, and "borderline traits in the context of a significant trauma history; trauma-related disorder to be evaluated" is a more honest formulation than a definitive personality diagnosis.

**Q4. Is this presentation unusual in a man?**
→ *Teaching point:* No — and assuming otherwise is a diagnostic trap. In the largest United States epidemiologic survey, lifetime prevalence was essentially identical in men (5.6%) and women (6.2%) [3]. Men with this presentation are more often mislabeled — their impulsivity and anger get read as a substance problem or "anger issues" — so the pattern goes unrecognized and untreated. Expect this picture in patients of any gender.

**Q5. What is the first-line treatment, and does it require a settled diagnosis first?**
→ *Teaching point:* Psychotherapy — not medication — is the treatment of choice. Structured therapies such as dialectical behavior therapy and psychodynamic approaches (for example, mentalization-based treatment) reduce symptom severity with medium effect sizes compared with usual care [1,4]. No medication consistently improves the core symptoms, and no drug is approved for this indication; prescribing targets discrete comorbid disorders or short-term crisis management only [1]. Importantly, treatment does not wait for the official label: skills-based therapy targeting emotion dysregulation and self-injury is appropriate for the traits presentation now.

**Q6. Mr. R says, "I'd rather be dead than alone," but now asks to go home. How do you think about his safety?**
→ *Teaching point:* Distinguish chronic risk (recurrent self-injury and suicidal statements as part of the long-standing pattern) from acute-on-chronic risk (a new, specific plan or intent, worsening comorbid depression, intoxication, recent discharge, or loss of supports). Perform and document a structured suicide risk assessment, involve collateral, and build a safety plan: warning signs, internal coping strategies, people and settings that help, professional contacts, and reducing access to lethal means. Brief crisis admission is reserved for acute risk that cannot be managed with outpatient supports; long hospitalizations are generally not helpful and can reinforce crisis-driven care [1,2].

**Q7. The intern suggests starting an antidepressant "for the mood swings." How do you respond?**
→ *Teaching point:* Respectfully push back. Medication does not treat the core affective instability of this pattern, and patients often accumulate harmful polypharmacy. A selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor is reasonable only if he meets criteria for a comorbid disorder such as major depressive disorder. The high-yield move is referral to an evidence-based psychotherapy program and a validating explanation of what you see — a pattern of emotion dysregulation, often rooted in early adversity, that is very treatable, with remission the rule rather than the exception over time [1,2].

## Differential (ranked)

1. **Borderline personality traits (working impression; official personality disorder diagnosis deferred to longitudinal assessment)** — pervasive pattern since adolescence of interpersonal instability, abandonment fears, identity disturbance, self-injury, chronic emptiness, and hour-to-hour reactive mood shifts. Best cross-sectional fit, but confirmation requires time, not one emergency visit.
2. **Posttraumatic stress disorder / complex trauma presentation** — ranked high deliberately: he reports an unsafe childhood, overlapping affect dysregulation and self-injury; distinguished by re-experiencing, avoidance, and hyperarousal tied to index trauma; may coexist [2]. Must be formally assessed before a personality diagnosis is settled.
3. **Bipolar II / bipolar spectrum disorder** — argues against: no sustained hypomanic episodes, no change in sleep or goal-directed activity; mood shifts are reactive and brief. Misdiagnosis is common; prior mood-stabilizer failure is a clue.
4. **Major depressive disorder (comorbid or primary)** — screen for sustained neurovegetative symptoms; depression commonly co-occurs and is treatable.
5. **Substance use disorder** — binge drinking may be impulsivity, a comorbid disorder, or both; intoxication amplifies acute risk, and in men it often becomes the only diagnosis recorded [3].

## Workup & management

- **Workup:** careful longitudinal psychiatric history with collateral; trauma screen tonight; screen for mood and substance use disorders; toxicology when intoxication is possible; no laboratory test or imaging confirms the impression — labs serve to rule out contributors (thyroid studies are reasonable if a mood disorder is suspected).
- **Acute management:** treat wounds; structured suicide risk assessment; collaborative safety plan including means restriction; involve supports; avoid reflexive admission for chronic risk, but admit briefly for acute-on-chronic risk that outpatient structure cannot contain [1].
- **Documentation:** "borderline personality traits" with the observed behaviors listed, plus the trauma history and what still needs evaluation — not a definitive personality disorder diagnosis from one encounter.
- **Definitive management:** referral to structured, evidence-based psychotherapy (dialectical behavior therapy, mentalization-based treatment, or structured generalist care) [1,4]; name the pattern honestly and with hope — long-term symptomatic remission is common [2]; treat comorbid disorders on their own merits; avoid polypharmacy; schedule follow-up before discharge so a continuity provider can make the longitudinal call on diagnosis.

## Facilitator notes (not for learner distribution)

- **Timing:** ~25 minutes. Give Part 1 alone first and ask for initial reactions — surface countertransference early ("manipulative," "attention-seeking") and reframe: self-injury regulates unbearable affect; it is a symptom to treat, not a character verdict.
- **Facilitator's framing (make it explicit):** the deliberate center of this case is diagnostic humility. Students should leave able to recognize the pattern AND able to say why they are documenting traits rather than the disorder: personality diagnoses require longitudinal confirmation (our convention: an established provider relationship on the order of two years), trauma must be assessed first because it can mimic or coexist with the picture, and a premature label in the chart shapes every subsequent encounter.
- **Common misconceptions to correct:** (1) "This is bipolar until proven otherwise" — contrast episode duration and sleep/energy change; (2) "One emergency visit is enough to diagnose a personality disorder" — traits now, diagnosis longitudinally; (3) "He needs a mood stabilizer" — medication targets comorbidity, not core symptoms; (4) "Borderline personality disorder is a women's diagnosis" — community prevalence is equal in men and women [3]; (5) "He must be admitted because he mentioned death" — teach chronic vs. acute-on-chronic risk; (6) "Personality disorders never get better" — cite high remission rates to instill realistic optimism [2].
- **Pivot questions:** "What would make you admit him tonight?" · "What exactly would you write in the assessment, word for word?" · "How would you explain what you see to him without stigma?"
- **Safety framing:** keep all discussion of self-harm at the level of recognition, escalation, and safety planning; do not discuss methods or lethality specifics.
