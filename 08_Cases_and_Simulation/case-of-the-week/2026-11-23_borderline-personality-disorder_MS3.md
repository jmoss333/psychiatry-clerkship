# Case of the Week — When Crises Follow Interpersonal Rupture (MS3 Version)

## Borderline Personality Disorder: Recognizing the Pattern Without Rushing the Label

**Learner level:** MS3 (psychiatry clerkship) · **Date:** 2026-11-23 · **Format:** 20–30 min guided discussion
**Literature:** Based on articles retrieved from PubMed; see the numbered reference list at the end.

---

## Stem (learner-facing)

**Part 1 — Initial presentation.** Mr. R is a 24-year-old graduate student brought to the emergency department by a roommate after the roommate found him with superficial self-inflicted injuries to his forearm following an argument with his partner, who had threatened to end the relationship. This is his fourth emergency visit in 14 months; each followed an interpersonal conflict. He tells the team, "Everyone leaves me eventually — I'd rather be dead than alone," but a few minutes later is calm, joking with staff, and asking to be discharged so he can "fix things" with his partner.

**Part 2 — Further history.** Mr. R describes his mood as "all over the place, every day" — intense anger, anxiety, or despair lasting hours and usually triggered by something interpersonal. He describes chronic feelings of emptiness, a long pattern of relationships that begin as "perfect" and collapse within months, impulsive spending and episodes of binge drinking, and uncertainty about his goals and identity ("I don't know who I am"). He mentions, without elaborating, that "home wasn't a safe place growing up." During a past breakup he briefly felt that people were plotting against him; this resolved within a day. He has previously been told he has "bipolar disorder," but trials of two mood stabilizers did not help. He reports no sustained week-long periods of elevated mood, decreased need for sleep, or increased goal-directed activity. He sleeps and eats normally between crises and enjoys his friends and schoolwork when things are calm.

**Part 3 — Exam and course.** Vital signs are normal. He is cooperative, well-groomed, with full-range affect that shifts rapidly during the interview. He denies current intent to end his life, identifies his roommate and sister as supports, and asks for help "not falling apart every time something goes wrong." Toxicology is negative. The wounds are dressed; they do not require sutures.

## Guided discussion

**Q1. What pattern in this presentation points away from a primary mood disorder and toward a personality-level pattern?**
→ *Teaching point:* The mood shifts here are rapid (hours, not weeks), reactive to interpersonal triggers, and occur on a background of chronic emptiness, unstable relationships, identity disturbance, and recurrent crisis behavior dating to adolescence. Bipolar disorder requires discrete, sustained episodes (mania ≥1 week or any duration if hospitalization is required; hypomania ≥4 days) with changes in sleep, energy, and activity. Affective instability that cycles within a single day around relationship events is far more typical of borderline pathology [1].

**Q2. Which features of borderline personality disorder can you identify in Mr. R's story — and what should you actually write in today's chart?**
→ *Teaching point:* The history suggests a possible borderline personality pattern. Clarify the duration of the reported symptoms and their impact across settings, seek collateral and prior records, and document what is observed versus reported. Use ‘possible borderline personality traits’ as a working impression if the evidence is incomplete. A definitive diagnosis requires adequate longitudinal evidence, not a fixed two-year relationship with one clinician [1].

**Q3. Why does his comment that "home wasn't a safe place growing up" change how cautious you are with the label?**
→ *Teaching point:* His statement suggests possible childhood adversity, not a confirmed trauma history or diagnosis. With his permission, ask what feels relevant and assess trauma-related symptoms such as re-experiencing, avoidance, hyperarousal and dissociation. Trauma-related conditions may overlap or coexist with the borderline pattern. Document what he reports and what remains open [1,2].

**Q4. Is this presentation unusual in a man?**
→ *Teaching point:* No. One large US survey applying DSM-IV criteria reported lifetime estimates of 5.6% in men and 6.2% in women, with no statistically significant sex difference in that sample [3]. Assess symptoms and context rather than using gender to rule the pattern in or out.

**Q5. What is the first-line treatment, and does it require a settled diagnosis first?**
→ *Teaching point:* Offer structured psychotherapy adapted to his goals and the problems already identified, such as emotion dysregulation and recurring self-injury. DBT and mentalization-based treatment are examples; diagnostic uncertainty need not delay skill-building or referral. Medication is not the default for the core pattern; review verified co-occurring conditions and existing prescriptions before making changes [1,4].

**Q6. Mr. R says, "I'd rather be dead than alone," but now asks to go home. How do you think about his safety?**
→ *Teaching point:* Take his words seriously even when his affect and request change. Assess current suicidal thoughts, intent, plans and access to means; clarify previous episodes, stressors and substance use; and verify available supports. Distinguish baseline risk from changes today. Consider outpatient disposition only after reassessment and a feasible plan that includes verified supports, a collaborative safety plan and timely follow-up. If acute risk cannot be managed safely outside the hospital, escalate to an appropriate level of care. Neither the label nor denial of current intent determines disposition [1,2].

**Q7. The intern suggests starting an antidepressant "for the mood swings." How do you respond?**
→ *Teaching point:* Do not add an antidepressant simply for hour-to-hour mood shifts. Evaluate for a distinct treatable co-occurring disorder, review current prescriptions, and use shared decision-making about any treatment. Prioritize a psychotherapy referral and a non-stigmatizing explanation of the working formulation [1,2].

## Differential (ranked)

1. **Borderline personality traits (working impression; official personality disorder diagnosis deferred to longitudinal assessment)** — pervasive pattern since adolescence of interpersonal instability, abandonment fears, identity disturbance, self-injury, chronic emptiness, and hour-to-hour reactive mood shifts. Best cross-sectional fit, but confirmation requires time, not one emergency visit.
2. **Posttraumatic stress disorder / complex trauma presentation** — ranked high deliberately: he reports an unsafe childhood, overlapping affect dysregulation and self-injury; distinguished by re-experiencing, avoidance, and hyperarousal tied to index trauma; may coexist [2]. Assess alongside the personality, mood and substance-use differential.
3. **Bipolar II / bipolar spectrum disorder** — argues against: no sustained hypomanic episodes, no change in sleep or goal-directed activity; mood shifts are reactive and brief. Misdiagnosis is common; prior medication response does not establish or exclude the diagnosis.
4. **Major depressive disorder (comorbid or primary)** — screen for sustained neurovegetative symptoms; depression commonly co-occurs and is treatable.
5. **Substance use disorder** — binge drinking may be impulsivity, a comorbid disorder, or both; intoxication amplifies acute risk, and in men it often becomes the only diagnosis recorded [3].

## Workup & management

- **Workup:** careful longitudinal psychiatric history with collateral; assess possible childhood adversity and trauma-related symptoms when appropriate; screen for mood and substance use disorders; toxicology when intoxication is possible; no laboratory test or imaging confirms the impression — labs serve to rule out contributors (thyroid studies are reasonable if a mood disorder is suspected).
- **Acute management:** treat wounds; structured suicide risk assessment; collaborative safety plan including means restriction; involve supports; verify supports and reassess the safest feasible level of care; arrange follow-up before discharge if appropriate [1].
- **Documentation:** "borderline personality traits" with observed behaviors, the reported childhood-safety concern and what still needs evaluation — not a definitive personality disorder diagnosis from one encounter.
- **Definitive management:** referral to structured, evidence-based psychotherapy (dialectical behavior therapy, mentalization-based treatment, or structured generalist care) [1,4]; name the pattern honestly and with hope — long-term symptomatic remission is common [2]; treat comorbid disorders on their own merits; avoid polypharmacy; schedule follow-up before discharge so a continuity provider can make the longitudinal call on diagnosis.
