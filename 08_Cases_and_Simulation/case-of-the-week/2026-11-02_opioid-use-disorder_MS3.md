# Case of the Week: "He Woke Up Angry" — MS3 Version

## Opioid Use Disorder: Intoxication, Withdrawal, and Medications for OUD (MOUD)

**Week of November 2, 2026 · Learner level: MS3 (psychiatry clerkship) · Discussion time: ~20–30 min**

*Based on articles retrieved from PubMed. All patient details are synthetic and de-identified — this is a teaching composite, not a real patient.*

---

## Stem (learner-facing)

**Part 1 — Arrival.** A 28-year-old man is brought to the emergency department by EMS after being found unresponsive in a park restroom. Bystanders gave two doses of intranasal naloxone (an opioid antagonist that rapidly reverses opioid effects) before EMS arrived. On arrival he is awake, irritable, and asking to leave. Vital signs: HR 104, BP 142/88, RR 18, SpO2 98% on room air, T 37.1°C.

On exam he is diaphoretic (sweating), yawning repeatedly, with dilated pupils, piloerection ("gooseflesh"), rhinorrhea, and diffuse muscle aches. He vomits once during the interview. Track marks are visible on both forearms.

**Part 2 — History.** Once he settles, he tells you he has used "blues" (counterfeit pills that in the current drug supply almost always contain illicitly manufactured fentanyl) daily for about three years, escalating doses, with multiple failed attempts to cut back, cravings, and loss of his job and apartment. He has overdosed twice before. He says: "I'm done. I've tried to stop on my own and I can't. But last time a hospital gave me something that made me instantly, violently sick, and I'm not doing that again."

**Part 3 — Course.** Two hours later his Clinical Opiate Withdrawal Scale (COWS — an 11-item scored exam quantifying withdrawal severity) score is 14 (moderate withdrawal). He is requesting help but is also threatening to leave if he keeps feeling this bad.

## Guided discussion questions

**Q1. Right after naloxone reversal, what two clinical states is this patient cycling between, and what features on his exam are classic for opioid withdrawal?**

→ *Teaching point:* Opioid **intoxication** = sedation, miosis (pinpoint pupils), respiratory depression — the killer is hypoventilation. Opioid **withdrawal** = the opposite autonomic picture: mydriasis, diaphoresis, piloerection, yawning, lacrimation/rhinorrhea, myalgias, vomiting/diarrhea, anxiety. Naloxone converts one into the other within minutes. Withdrawal is miserable but — unlike alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal — rarely lethal by itself in healthy adults; dehydration and treatment dropout (with loss of tolerance and later overdose) are the real dangers [1].

**Q2. Does this patient meet criteria for opioid use disorder? How would you characterize severity?**

→ *Teaching point:* DSM-5-TR OUD requires ≥2 of 11 criteria in 12 months. He has tolerance, escalating use, failed quit attempts, craving, major role failure, and continued use despite harm — ≥6 criteria = **severe** OUD. Tolerance and withdrawal alone do NOT count toward the diagnosis when opioids are taken as prescribed; here they clearly count.

**Q3. Why was he "instantly, violently sick" after a prior hospital gave him a medication — what likely happened?**

→ *Teaching point:* **Precipitated withdrawal.** Buprenorphine is a partial agonist with very high mu-receptor affinity: given too early, it displaces the full agonist (fentanyl) from receptors and abruptly drops agonist signaling. Fentanyl's lipophilicity makes it linger in tissue for days after heavy use, so even a patient who "looks ready" can be precipitated — a key reason standard inductions now wait for at least moderate objective withdrawal (COWS ≥8–12 is a commonly used threshold) before the first dose [1,3].

**Q4. He's in moderate withdrawal and wants treatment. What are your options right now in the ED, and which carry a mortality benefit?**

→ *Teaching point:* Three FDA-approved MOUD: **methadone** (full agonist; for outpatients, dispensed only through federally regulated opioid treatment programs), **buprenorphine** (partial agonist; office-based prescribing — any prescriber, no special waiver since 2023), and **naltrexone** (antagonist; requires full detox first). **Methadone and buprenorphine reduce overdose and all-cause mortality**; starting MOUD rather than "detox alone" roughly halves mortality (adjusted HR 0.52 for MOUD vs none) [1]. Withdrawal treated without transition to MOUD is a missed, potentially fatal, opportunity — tolerance falls and the next use can kill.

**Q5. Walk through a standard ED buprenorphine start for this patient. What do you tell him about what to expect?**

→ *Teaching point:* With COWS ≥ ~8–12 and last use several hours ago: buprenorphine 2–8 mg SL, reassess in 1–2 h, repeat to typically 8–16 mg day 1, target ~16–24 mg daily. Counsel: "We wait until your body is already in withdrawal so the medicine makes you feel *better*, not worse." Discharge (if appropriate) with naloxone kit, warm handoff to follow-up. Adjuncts for symptoms: ondansetron (nausea), NSAIDs (myalgias), alpha-2 agonists such as clonidine or lofexidine (autonomic symptoms) [1].

**Q6. His friend asks, "You gave him naloxone — isn't he safe now?" What's the safety counseling before any discharge?**

→ *Teaching point:* Naloxone's duration (~30–90 min) is shorter than fentanyl's, so re-sedation can occur; observe after reversal. Teach overdose response (call emergency services, give naloxone, rescue breathing — ventilation is the cornerstone) and dispense take-home naloxone: community naloxone distribution is associated with 25–46% lower community overdose death rates [1]. Never use alone; assume the supply contains fentanyl and possibly adulterants.

## Differential (ranked)

1. **Opioid withdrawal (naloxone-precipitated) in severe OUD** — classic autonomic signature plus history; leading diagnosis.
2. **Sedative-hypnotic (alcohol/benzodiazepine) withdrawal** — overlapping autonomics, but expect tremor, possible seizures/DTs; these withdrawals are life-threatening, so always screen for co-use.
3. **Sympathomimetic intoxication (stimulants)** — mydriasis and agitation overlap; look for recent use history, absence of yawning/piloerection.
4. **Sepsis or other medical illness in a person who injects drugs** — fever, tachycardia; always examine for cellulitis, abscess, endocarditis stigmata.
5. **Anxiety/agitation of another primary psychiatric cause** — a diagnosis of exclusion while the toxidrome is active.

## Workup & management

- **Immediate:** post-naloxone observation and respiratory monitoring; COWS scoring serially; point-of-care glucose; ECG if methadone is contemplated (QTc) or electrolyte concern from vomiting.
- **Labs:** CBC, BMP, LFTs, hepatitis B/C and HIV screening (offer), pregnancy test when applicable, urine drug screen (remember: routine immunoassays often miss fentanyl — a negative "opiates" screen does not rule out fentanyl use).
- **Skin/infectious survey:** examine injection sites; low threshold for imaging/blood cultures if febrile.
- **Treatment:** symptom-triggered buprenorphine induction as above; ondansetron, NSAIDs, clonidine as adjuncts; begin MOUD *before* discharge whenever possible [1]. Methadone and buprenorphine are first-line; both cut mortality [1,2]. Naltrexone (extended-release) is an option only after ~7–10 days opioid-free — the "induction hurdle" that drove higher early relapse in the X:BOT trial's intention-to-treat analysis [4].
- **Safety:** take-home naloxone, overdose education, never-use-alone counseling, warm handoff to addiction follow-up or bridge clinic.

## Facilitator notes (not for learner distribution)

- **Timing:** Part 1 ~5 min (toxidromes), Part 2 ~5 min (diagnosis), Part 3 + Q4–Q6 ~15 min (management). If short on time, prioritize Q3–Q5 — they carry the clerkship-level money points.
- **Common misconceptions to surface:** (a) "Withdrawal is dangerous, so taper slowly and send home" — opioid withdrawal is rarely lethal, but *untreated OUD after detox* is; (b) "Buprenorphine is just replacing one addiction with another" — address stigma head-on with the mortality data [1]; (c) "The urine screen was negative for opiates, so it wasn't an opioid overdose" — fentanyl requires a specific assay.
- **Pivot questions:** "What would change if he were 34 weeks pregnant?" (MOUD still standard of care; do not detox). "What if he refuses all medication?" (naloxone kit, harm-reduction counseling, door left open — autonomy without abandonment.)
- **Safety framing:** keep all dangerousness content oriented to recognition, escalation, and safety planning. No dosing "recipes" for non-prescribed use, no method detail beyond standard clinical teaching.
- **Board hook (Step 2 CK):** naloxone for overdose with ventilation first; COWS-gated buprenorphine start; methadone/buprenorphine mortality benefit; precipitated withdrawal mechanism.
