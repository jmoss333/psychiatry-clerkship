## Acute Agitation & Delirium in the ED: The Combative Older Adult Who "Wasn't Like This Yesterday"

*De-identified synthetic teaching case. No real patient details. Based on articles retrieved from PubMed; DOI links appear in the reference list.*

## Learner-Facing Case Stem

**Setting:** Community hospital emergency department (ED), 22:30 on a weeknight.

Mr. R is a 74-year-old man brought in by his daughter for "confusion and acting out of character" over the past two days. He has a history of hypertension, benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH — an enlarged prostate that can obstruct urine flow), and mild hearing loss. His daughter says he is normally independent, sharp, and manages his own finances.

Tonight he is pulling at his IV line, shouting that the staff are "holding him prisoner," and trying to climb off the stretcher. Moments later he is drowsy and mumbling. His daughter is tearful: "He wasn't like this yesterday. Yesterday he just seemed tired and a little off."

**Vital signs:** T 38.1 °C, HR 104, BP 148/86, RR 18, SpO2 96% on room air.

**On exam:** He is inattentive — he cannot recite the months backward and drifts off mid-sentence. He does not know the date or that he is in a hospital. There are no focal neurologic deficits. Mucous membranes are dry. A suprapubic region is mildly tender. He becomes agitated when approached quickly but calms somewhat when the lights are lowered and one staff member speaks slowly and simply.

**Medications (per daughter):** amlodipine, tamsulosin, and — started five days ago by an outside provider for "bladder spasms" — oxybutynin (an anticholinergic).

## Guided Discussion Questions (with Teaching Points)

**Q1. What is the single most important distinction to make first: is this a psychiatric illness, or is it delirium?**

*Teaching point:* This presentation is delirium until proven otherwise — an acute (hours to days) disturbance of attention and awareness that fluctuates, with an underlying medical cause. New-onset "psychosis" in a 74-year-old with fever, inattention, and fluctuating arousal is a medical emergency, not primary psychiatric disease. Primary psychotic disorders rarely first present in the eighth decade, and they do not impair attention and level of consciousness the way delirium does. ED clinicians miss delirium in a large share of cases when they do not screen for it [3].

**Q2. What bedside features distinguish delirium from dementia and from primary psychosis?**

*Teaching point:* Three anchors — onset, attention, and course. Delirium: acute onset, impaired attention, fluctuating arousal, often reversible. Dementia: months-to-years course, attention relatively preserved early, stable arousal. Primary psychosis: usually younger onset, clear sensorium (alert, attentive), hallucinations more often auditory than visual. Visual hallucinations and a waxing-waning sensorium point strongly to delirium. A patient can have both — dementia is the biggest risk factor for delirium.

**Q3. What is your ranked differential for the cause of this patient's delirium?**

*Teaching point:* Delirium is a syndrome; the differential is for its cause. See the ranked differential below. In older adults, think infection (especially urinary tract infection [UTI] with retention in a man with BPH), medications (anticholinergics like oxybutynin are classic precipitants), metabolic derangements, and — always — hypoglycemia, hypoxia, alcohol or sedative-hypnotic (e.g., benzodiazepine) withdrawal (ask the daughter directly about drinking and sleeping pills), CNS infection (meningitis/encephalitis — fever plus confusion), and intracranial catastrophe as can't-miss items.

**Q4. The patient is escalating — shouting and swinging at staff. What do you do first?**

*Teaching point:* First, make the scene safe: step back to a safe distance with a clear exit, call for help (nurse, security, your supervising resident/attending), and let one experienced team member lead — you participate under supervision. Then: verbal de-escalation before medication, and medication before physical restraint. The Project BETA consensus emphasizes engaging the patient verbally, offering choices ("Can I get you a blanket? Would you take a medicine to help you feel calmer?"), minimizing stimulation, and reserving restraint for imminent danger [2]. Restraints in a delirious older adult can worsen agitation and cause injury; if used, they demand documentation, frequent reassessment, and removal as soon as safe.

**Q5. If medication is needed for dangerous agitation in suspected delirium, what is a reasonable choice — and what should you avoid?**

*Teaching point:* Treat the cause first; medicate the behavior only when it endangers the patient or staff. Project BETA recommends matching the drug to the suspected etiology: for delirium not due to alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal, a low-dose antipsychotic (e.g., haloperidol or an atypical) is preferred, and benzodiazepines should generally be AVOIDED because they can deepen confusion and worsen delirium in older adults [1]. (The exception: sedative-hypnotic or alcohol withdrawal, where benzodiazepines are first-line.) Avoid haloperidol (and use any antipsychotic only with great caution) in known Parkinson disease or suspected Lewy body dementia — look for parkinsonism or hallucinations/fluctuations that predate this illness — because of severe antipsychotic sensitivity; ask your team. Start low, go slow, and reassess.

**Q6. What is your initial workup?**

*Teaching point:* Fingerstick glucose immediately; then targeted evaluation: CBC, BMP, urinalysis with culture, bladder scan (urinary retention is both a cause and a consequence here — he is on an anticholinergic with BPH), ECG (baseline QTc before antipsychotics), and review/discontinuation of deliriogenic medications (stop the oxybutynin). Chest imaging and blood cultures if infection suspected; head CT if trauma, anticoagulation, focal deficit, or no cause found. Avoid the reflex "head CT for everyone" — the highest-yield tests target infection, metabolic causes, and medications.

**Q7. The daughter asks, "Is he going to be like this forever?" How do you counsel her?**

*Teaching point:* Delirium is usually reversible when the cause is treated, but it can take days to weeks to clear, and it is a marker of vulnerability — associated with longer hospital stays and increased risk of subsequent cognitive decline. Family presence, reorientation, glasses/hearing aids, sleep protection, and early mobilization all help. Naming the syndrome for families ("this is delirium — confusion caused by a medical problem, not a new mental illness") reduces fear and enlists them in care.

## Ranked Differential (Top 5)

1. **Delirium due to UTI/urosepsis with urinary retention** — fever, suprapubic tenderness, BPH, tachycardia.
2. **Medication-induced (anticholinergic) delirium** — oxybutynin started five days ago; dry mucous membranes; urinary retention compounding.
3. **Delirium of other medical cause (metabolic/electrolyte, dehydration)** — dry membranes; needs BMP.
4. **Delirium superimposed on early, unrecognized dementia** — must reassess cognition after recovery; do not diagnose dementia during delirium.
5. **Primary psychiatric illness (late-onset psychosis/mania)** — diagnosis of exclusion; inattention, fever, and fluctuation argue strongly against it.

## Workup & Management (Learner Summary)

- **Immediate:** glucose, vitals, SpO2; ensure safety of patient and staff; one calm communicator; reduce stimulation.
- **De-escalate verbally first** [2]; involve the daughter as a reorienting presence.
- **Labs/studies:** CBC, BMP, UA + culture, bladder scan/post-void residual, ECG, consider CXR, blood cultures; head CT only with trauma/anticoagulation/focal signs/unexplained course.
- **Treat the cause:** antibiotics if UTI/urosepsis; relieve retention (catheterize, hold anticholinergic); **stop oxybutynin**; hydrate.
- **If dangerous agitation persists:** low-dose antipsychotic per Project BETA guidance; avoid benzodiazepines outside withdrawal syndromes [1].
- **Prevent harm:** fall precautions, avoid tethers where possible, glasses/hearing aids, early reassessment; document decision-making for any restraint.
- **Disposition:** admit; delirium workup and treatment rarely complete in the ED; communicate the diagnosis explicitly to the admitting team.

## Facilitator Notes (Not for Learner Distribution)

- **Flow (20–30 min):** 5 min stem read-aloud; Q1–Q3 (10 min) — push students to commit to "delirium until proven otherwise" and to rank their differential; Q4–Q5 (8 min) — role-play a 60-second verbal de-escalation; Q6–Q7 (7 min).
- **Common student errors to draw out:** (1) anchoring on "psych patient" and calling psychiatry before medical workup; (2) reaching for lorazepam first in a non-withdrawal delirium; (3) ordering head CT reflexively while missing the medication list; (4) forgetting the bladder scan in a man on tamsulosin + new anticholinergic.
- **If time permits:** demonstrate a 30-second attention screen (months of the year backward; days of week backward) and mention that formal ED tools exist (Delirium Triage Screen → brief Confusion Assessment Method) with strong test characteristics [3] — students need only know that brief validated screens exist and that attention is the core deficit.
- **Safety framing:** keep all dangerousness content on recognition, team safety, escalation to security/medication protocols, and least-restrictive interventions. No restraint technique detail.
- **Step 2 CK hooks:** delirium vs dementia vs psychosis table; anticholinergic precipitants; benzodiazepines contraindicated in non-withdrawal delirium in the elderly; UTI/retention in older men; QTc before antipsychotics.
