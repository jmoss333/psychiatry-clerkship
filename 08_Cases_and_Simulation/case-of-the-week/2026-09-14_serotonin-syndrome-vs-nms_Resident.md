## Serotonin Syndrome vs. Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome: Rigidity, Fever, and a Confounded Medication List

## PART 1 — LEARNER-FACING CASE

### Clinical stem

A 29-year-old with bipolar I disorder is admitted to the inpatient psychiatric unit in a mixed episode. Home regimen: **venlafaxine XR** (increased two weeks ago by an outside prescriber) and **quetiapine** at bedtime. In the ED the night of admission, the patient received **haloperidol 10 mg IM twice** plus lorazepam for severe agitation. A scheduled **risperidone** titration was started on admission and advanced over four days.

On hospital day 5, nursing reports the patient is "stiff and not really talking." Over 48 hours the patient has become progressively rigid, diaphoretic, intermittently confused, then hypoactive and near-mute.

**Vitals:** T 39.4 °C · HR 131 · BP 168/98 → 121/74 within minutes (labile) · RR 24 · SpO₂ 96% RA

**Exam:**
- **Mental status:** Fluctuating stupor; minimally verbal; no sustained eye contact; intermittent mutism.
- **Neuromuscular:** Severe generalized **"lead-pipe" rigidity** in all four limbs, symmetric. **Reflexes reduced.** No spontaneous, inducible, or ocular **clonus**. No tremor-predominant picture.
- **Pupils:** Normal size and reactive.
- **Skin:** Diaphoretic, pale.
- **Abdomen:** Hypoactive bowel sounds.

**Labs:** CK 28,400 U/L and rising · WBC 15.8 ×10⁹/L without left shift · AST/ALT mildly elevated · serum iron low · Cr 1.4 (baseline 0.8) · urine dark, myoglobin-positive. Lithium level: none on board. Head CT unremarkable; infectious workup (blood cultures, UA, CXR) unrevealing so far; LP being discussed.

### Discussion questions

*Teaching points are in the Facilitator Guide (Part 2).*

1. **Rank your differential.** What specifically argues for NMS over serotonin syndrome in this patient, given that both a serotonergic agent (venlafaxine) and multiple dopamine antagonists are on board?
2. **Contrast the pathophysiology and time course** of the two syndromes. Why does the D2-blockade mechanism predict the exam and lab findings you see here?
3. **Which laboratory pattern supports NMS,** and what are the limits of labs in separating these syndromes?
4. **Build the management ladder for NMS by severity** — what is the evidence base (and its weakness) for benzodiazepines, bromocriptine, amantadine, dantrolene, and ECT?
5. **Where does malignant catatonia fit?** How would a lorazepam challenge inform you here, and what is the argument that NMS is a drug-induced form of malignant catatonia?
6. **The patient recovers. Psychosis and mood instability persist and clearly require an antipsychotic.** How do you approach rechallenge — timing, agent selection, titration, monitoring, and consent?
7. **What would you have done differently prospectively?** Identify the modifiable risk factors in this admission (drug choice, route, titration speed, cumulative D2 load, dehydration).

## PART 2 — FACILITATOR GUIDE

*(Keep separate from the learner handout.)*

### Teaching points, question by question

**1. Differential and discrimination.** The picture is **NMS**: subacute evolution over days in the setting of escalating cumulative D2 blockade (parenteral haloperidol + risperidone titration + quetiapine), **lead-pipe rigidity with hyporeflexia and no clonus**, normal pupils, hypoactive bowel sounds, and the supporting lab pattern [1,4]. Serotonin syndrome from venlafaxine remains on the differential — but it develops over **hours**, produces **hyperreflexia, clonus (legs > arms), mydriasis, and GI hyperactivity**, and usually clears within 24–48 h of drug cessation [2,3]. Perry & Wilborn's comparative review emphasizes exactly this contrast when polypharmacy muddies attribution [4]. Also actively exclude: malignant catatonia (see Q5), CNS infection (LP if any doubt), status epilepticus, anticholinergic toxicity, heat stroke, and thyroid storm [5].

**2. Mechanism and tempo.** NMS is driven by acute **D2 receptor blockade** in the nigrostriatal (rigidity, rhabdomyolysis), hypothalamic (thermal dysregulation), and mesocortical/limbic (altered mental status) pathways, with sympathetic dysregulation producing the autonomic storm [1]. Typical onset is **1–2 weeks after initiation or escalation**; parenteral administration and rapid titration accelerate risk [1,6]. Serotonin toxicity is a **dose-related spectrum of excess agonism** (5-HT2A-predominant for severe toxicity), which is why it appears within hours of the provoking dose and why neuromuscular *excitation* rather than parkinsonian rigidity dominates [2,3]. The mechanistic difference predicts the exam: dopamine withdrawal → extrapyramidal rigidity and bradyreflexia; serotonergic excess → spinal hyperexcitability, clonus, hyperreflexia.

**3. Laboratory pattern.** The triad of **marked CK elevation** (often >1,000, here >25,000 with myoglobinuria), **leukocytosis**, and **low serum iron** — with transaminase elevation — supports NMS over serotonin syndrome in patients on both drug classes [4]. Caveats residents should own: CK elevation is neither necessary nor specific (IM injections, restraints, and agitation all raise it); severe serotonin toxicity can also cause rhabdomyolysis; and **no laboratory finding is diagnostic — both are clinical diagnoses** [2,4,5]. Low serum iron is a sensitivity-limited but useful discriminator in the right context [4]. Track CK, creatinine, potassium, and urine output for rhabdomyolysis-associated AKI.

**4. Management ladder for NMS.** Evidence is observational — case series and retrospective reviews, no RCTs [1,6]:
- **All severities:** stop all dopamine antagonists immediately (and restart any withdrawn dopaminergic therapy if that was the trigger); aggressive IV fluids (rhabdomyolysis/AKI prophylaxis), active cooling, electrolyte correction, DVT prophylaxis, and continuous monitoring — supportive care is the backbone and mortality driver [1,6].
- **Moderate:** add **lorazepam** (also treats the catatonic overlap) and consider **bromocriptine** (2.5 mg q8h, titrated) or **amantadine**; continue dopamine agonists ≥10 days after resolution to avoid rebound, given depot/long half-life exposures [1,6,7].
- **Severe (T ≥ 40 °C, CK markedly elevated, autonomic instability):** ICU; **dantrolene** for severe rigidity/hyperthermia, often with a dopamine agonist [1,4,6]. Intubation and paralysis if refractory hyperthermia.
- **Refractory or catatonia-predominant:** **ECT** — effective in case series for NMS unresponsive to 48–72 h of medical therapy and first-line territory for malignant catatonia [1,5,6].

**5. Malignant catatonia and the continuum argument.** This stem includes mutism, negativism-adjacent findings, and stupor — catatonic features. Malignant catatonia can be clinically indistinguishable from NMS (fever, rigidity, autonomic instability), and several authors treat NMS as a **drug-induced variant of malignant catatonia** [1,5]. Practical implications: a **lorazepam challenge** (1–2 mg IV with structured re-examination, e.g., Bush-Francis) is both diagnostic and therapeutic; benzodiazepines are low-risk in either syndrome; and failure to improve with adequate benzodiazepines pushes toward ECT [5]. A prior psychiatric history of catatonia raises future NMS risk — worth documenting.

**6. Rechallenge.** Continued antipsychotic need is the rule, not the exception, and recurrence can occur at any point after resolution [6]. Principles from Pileggi & Cook and the general literature [1,6]:
- **Wait ≥2 weeks** after full resolution (longer after depot exposure) before rechallenge.
- **Change agents:** choose a **lower-potency or lower-D2-affinity agent** than the culprit (e.g., quetiapine, or clozapine when indicated); avoid the offending drug and avoid depot formulations during rechallenge.
- **Start low, titrate slowly**, with daily exam for rigidity, temperature, autonomic signs, and periodic CK early on.
- **Document and consent:** the NMS episode goes in the allergy/adverse-reaction field; the patient (and family, where appropriate) should understand the recurrence risk and early warning signs.

**7. Prospective risk modification.** Modifiable contributors in this stem: **parenteral high-potency haloperidol** in the ED, **rapid risperidone titration on top of residual haloperidol**, cumulative multi-agent D2 load, agitation-related dehydration and exertion. Alternatives: benzodiazepine-forward agitation management, slower titration with overlap accounting, early vitals/rigidity checks after any parenteral high-potency exposure, and explicit hand-off of the venlafaxine increase (which independently demands serotonin toxicity vigilance) [1,2,6].

### Ranked differential

1. **Neuroleptic malignant syndrome** — cumulative/escalating D2 blockade, days-long prodrome, lead-pipe rigidity + hyporeflexia, CK/leukocytosis/low-iron pattern [1,4].
2. **Malignant catatonia** — overlapping and possibly continuous with NMS; catatonic features present; lorazepam challenge ± ECT pathway [5].
3. **Serotonin syndrome** — venlafaxine on board, but tempo, absent clonus/hyperreflexia, normal pupils, and GI hypoactivity argue against [2,3].
4. **CNS infection (meningoencephalitis)** — fever + AMS mandates active exclusion; low threshold for LP once stable [1,5].
5. **Anticholinergic toxicity / heat stroke / thyroid storm / status epilepticus** — address with exam (dry vs. diaphoretic skin, pupil exam), core temp context, TSH, and EEG if fluctuating arousal is unexplained [5].

### Workup & management (summary)

- **Clinical diagnosis; labs assess severity and exclude mimics:** CK (serial), CMP, CBC, iron studies, LFTs, coags, blood cultures, UA/urine myoglobin, tox screen, ECG; CT head; LP if infectious doubt; EEG if nonconvulsive status is plausible [1,4,5].
- **Stop all D2 antagonists → ICU-level supportive care → lorazepam → bromocriptine/amantadine → dantrolene (severe) → ECT (refractory/catatonic)** [1,5,6].
- Monitor for rhabdomyolysis-associated AKI, hyperkalemia, DIC, aspiration, and VTE [1].
- Structured rechallenge plan and adverse-reaction documentation before discharge [6].
