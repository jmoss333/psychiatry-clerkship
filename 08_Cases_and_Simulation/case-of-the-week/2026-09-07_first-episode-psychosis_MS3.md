# Case of the Week — MS3 Version (Week of September 7, 2026)

## First-Episode Psychosis: Workup & Initial Management

**Learner level:** MS3 (psychiatry clerkship) · **Format:** ~20–30 min small-group discussion · **This is a fully synthetic, de-identified teaching case. No real patient is depicted.**

---

## Case Stem (learner-facing)

Jordan is a 19-year-old college sophomore with no prior psychiatric history, brought to the emergency department by his roommate. Over the past three months Jordan has become increasingly withdrawn, stopped attending classes, and covered his dorm window with foil "so the transmissions can't get through." For the past two weeks he has heard two voices commenting on his actions ("he's opening the door now... he knows we're watching"). He believes campus security has implanted a tracking device in his phone charger.

He is calm and cooperative but guarded. He denies any thoughts of hurting himself or anyone else. He reports smoking cannabis "a few times a month" but says he stopped six weeks ago because "it made the voices louder." He takes no medications. His roommate says Jordan was "totally normal" freshman year.

**Vital signs:** T 37.0°C, HR 84, BP 118/72, RR 14, SpO₂ 99%. Exam: alert, oriented ×4, no focal neurologic deficits. Mental status: guarded, flat affect, linear but over-inclusive thought process, auditory hallucinations and persecutory delusions endorsed, insight poor, judgment fair.

---

## Guided Discussion Questions

**Q1. What features of this presentation point toward a primary psychotic disorder, and what would make you worry about a medical ("secondary") cause instead?**

*Teaching point:* A gradual prodrome (social withdrawal, functional decline) over months in a young adult, with intact orientation and normal vital signs, is typical of primary psychosis. Red flags for secondary psychosis — abrupt onset, fluctuating consciousness (suggests delirium — an acute confusional state with impaired attention), abnormal vitals, focal neurologic signs, visual hallucinations predominating, or onset after age 40 — are absent here. Always assess sensorium first: psychosis with a clouded sensorium is delirium until proven otherwise [3].

**Q2. Build a ranked differential diagnosis for this patient.**

*Teaching point:* See ranked differential below. DSM-5-TR duration criteria organize the primary psychotic disorders: brief psychotic disorder (<1 month), schizophreniform disorder (1–6 months), schizophrenia (≥6 months including prodrome). Three months of symptoms with functional decline currently fits schizophreniform disorder — the label may evolve, and students should say so rather than prematurely anchoring on schizophrenia.

**Q3. What is the essential initial workup before starting treatment?**

*Teaching point:* The goal is to exclude medical mimics and establish a treatment baseline — not to order everything. Core panel: CBC, CMP (electrolytes, glucose, renal and hepatic function), TSH, urine drug screen, urine hCG when applicable, HIV and syphilis screening, and vitamin B12. Baseline metabolic parameters (weight/BMI, lipids, A1c) matter because antipsychotics cause metabolic side effects. Neuroimaging and EEG are reserved for presentations with neurologic red flags rather than ordered routinely [3].

**Q4. Does his cannabis use explain the presentation?**

*Teaching point:* Substance-induced psychotic disorder requires symptoms to remit with sustained abstinence — his symptoms have persisted and worsened six weeks after stopping, which argues against a purely substance-induced picture. However, cannabis use is a genuine risk factor for primary psychosis and worsens its course, so counseling continued abstinence is part of treatment, not an afterthought.

**Q5. How would you start an antipsychotic, and how do first-episode patients differ from patients with chronic illness?**

*Teaching point:* First-episode patients typically respond to lower doses and are more sensitive to side effects than multi-episode patients. Start a second-generation antipsychotic (e.g., risperidone or aripiprazole) at the low end of the dose range and titrate slowly, monitoring for extrapyramidal symptoms (drug-induced movement problems such as stiffness, tremor, and restlessness) and metabolic effects [2]. Discuss side effects up front — early adverse experiences drive later nonadherence.

**Q6. Beyond medication, what should treatment include — and why does the system of care matter?**

*Teaching point:* The NIMH RAISE-ETP trial showed that coordinated specialty care — a team-based package of low-dose antipsychotic treatment, individual therapy, family psychoeducation, and supported education/employment — produced better quality of life and symptom outcomes than usual care, with the greatest benefit in patients treated earlier in illness [1]. Referral to an early-psychosis program is a concrete, evidence-based discharge action, and shorter duration of untreated psychosis predicts better long-term outcome.

**Q7. What safety assessment is essential before disposition?**

*Teaching point:* Ask directly about thoughts of self-harm and about whether the voices ever give commands, and assess whether delusions involve specific people the patient might feel driven to confront. Early psychosis carries elevated suicide risk, highest in the first years of illness. If any risk indicator emerges, escalate: involve the attending, use a structured risk assessment, and err toward observation and safety planning with the patient and family. Recognition and escalation — not prediction — are the student's job.

## Ranked Differential Diagnosis

1. **Schizophreniform disorder / evolving schizophrenia** — 3-month prodrome, functional decline, hallucinations + delusions, age and demographic typical.
2. **Substance-induced (cannabis) psychotic disorder** — use predates symptoms, but persistence ≥6 weeks after abstinence argues against.
3. **Psychotic disorder due to another medical condition** (thyroid disease, autoimmune encephalitis, HIV, neurosyphilis, B12 deficiency) — must be screened; low pretest probability given normal exam and vitals.
4. **Mood disorder with psychotic features** — no current manic or depressive syndrome reported, but screen carefully; longitudinal course will clarify.
5. **Brief psychotic disorder** — duration already exceeds one month; listed to teach the duration framework.

## Workup & Initial Management (consolidated)

- **Labs:** CBC, CMP, TSH, urine drug screen, HIV, syphilis (RPR/treponemal), B12, ±hCG; baseline weight/BMI, lipids, A1c before antipsychotic [3].
- **Imaging/EEG:** only for neurologic red flags (focal deficits, seizure features, atypical age, rapid cognitive change) [3].
- **Medication:** low-dose second-generation antipsychotic; slow titration; side-effect counseling; document baseline AIMS (Abnormal Involuntary Movement Scale) [2].
- **Psychosocial:** refer to coordinated specialty care / early-psychosis program; family psychoeducation with consent; supported return to school [1].
- **Safety:** direct suicide and command-hallucination inquiry; escalate any positive finding; safety planning before discharge.
- **Follow-up:** close interval follow-up (days–weeks, not months); engage family to support adherence.

## Facilitator Notes (do not distribute to learners)

- **Timing guide:** stem + Q1–Q2 (10 min), workup Q3–Q4 (8 min), management Q5–Q6 (8 min), safety Q7 (4 min).
- **Common student errors to draw out:** anchoring on "schizophrenia" at first contact; attributing everything to cannabis; ordering head CT/MRI reflexively; starting high-dose antipsychotics; treating referral to specialty care as optional.
- **Board-style pearl (Step 2 CK):** psychosis + fluctuating attention/orientation = delirium workup, not antipsychotic monotherapy; duration criteria (brief <1 mo, schizophreniform 1–6 mo, schizophrenia ≥6 mo) are frequently tested.
- **Escalation framing:** keep all dangerousness content at the level of recognition, escalation, and safety planning; do not role-play method-specific content.
- **If asked about prognosis:** shorter duration of untreated psychosis is associated with better symptomatic and functional outcomes — use it to motivate urgency of engagement, not fatalism.
