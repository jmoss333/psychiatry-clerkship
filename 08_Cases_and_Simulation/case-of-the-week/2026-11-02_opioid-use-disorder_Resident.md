# Case of the Week: "Day 2, COWS 11, and a Hard No" — Resident Version

## Opioid Use Disorder: Withdrawal Management and MOUD Initiation in the Fentanyl/Xylazine Era

**Week of November 2, 2026 · Learner level: Psychiatry residents · Discussion time: ~25–30 min**

*Based on articles retrieved from PubMed. All patient details are synthetic and de-identified — this is a teaching composite, not a real patient.*

---

## Stem (learner-facing)

**Part 1 — The consult.** You are the C-L resident. Medicine admits a 41-year-old woman with severe OUD (daily intranasal/IV use of illicit fentanyl, roughly 3 g of "powder" weekly) for left forearm cellulitis with a necrotic-appearing ulcer requiring IV antibiotics and wound care. Consult question: "Manage withdrawal; patient threatening to leave AMA."

At bedside, 26 hours after last use: COWS 11 (restlessness, HR 96, diaphoresis, mydriasis, rhinorrhea, myalgias, one episode of diarrhea). She is also notably sedated in waves despite objective withdrawal, and nursing reports BP 92/58 earlier. She tells you: "Last year someone started me on 'subs' in a detox and I went into the worst withdrawal of my life within an hour. I will never touch buprenorphine again. And methadone clinics don't work with my job."

**Part 2 — Collateral and records.** Records confirm a buprenorphine-precipitated withdrawal event 14 months ago during a standard induction at COWS 9, about 12 hours after last fentanyl use. She has never had a trial of methadone. Two prior non-fatal overdoses; she carries naloxone. PHQ-9 is 16; she describes depressed mood and insomnia for months, worse during repeated withdrawal cycles. Urine immunoassay: positive fentanyl (specific assay), negative "opiates," positive for xylazine on expanded panel. Pregnancy test negative. QTc 438 ms.

**Part 3 — The decision.** She consents to "anything that keeps me out of withdrawal and lets me finish the antibiotics," but maintains her refusal of a standard buprenorphine induction. The hospitalist asks you to pick the regimen and write parameters.

---

## Guided discussion questions

**Q1. Interpret the discordant picture — COWS 11 with waves of sedation and relative hypotension. What in the tox screen explains it, and how does it change overdose and withdrawal management?**

→ *Teaching point:* **Xylazine co-exposure.** Xylazine, an α2-adrenergic agonist veterinary sedative now a common adulterant in the illicit fentanyl supply, produces sedation, bradycardia, and hypotension that naloxone does not reverse (it is not an opioid), and it is strongly associated with necrotic skin ulcers — including at non-injection sites. Overdose management remains ventilation plus naloxone for the opioid component; xylazine withdrawal (dysphoria, hypertension, anxiety) may need α2 agonists (clonidine, guanfacine, dexmedetomidine inpatient) layered onto opioid-withdrawal treatment. Her wound should prompt xylazine suspicion independent of the panel [5].

**Q2. Her precipitated-withdrawal story at COWS 9 / 12 hours is typical for the fentanyl era. Why does fentanyl break the old induction rules, and what are the current evidence-based workarounds?**

→ *Teaching point:* Fentanyl's high lipophilicity creates a deep tissue depot with a long terminal half-life after chronic use — time-since-last-use and even moderate COWS underestimate residual receptor occupancy, so standard inductions precipitate withdrawal more often than in the heroin era [1,3]. Options: (a) **standard induction with a higher withdrawal threshold** (COWS ≥ 12, longer abstinence window); (b) **low-dose ("microdose"/Bernese) initiation** — start 0.25–0.5 mg SL (or transdermal patch protocols) and up-titrate over 4–7+ days while the full agonist continues, exploiting buprenorphine's slow receptor accumulation to avoid an abrupt agonist drop [3]; (c) **high-dose/macro induction** in monitored settings; (d) **full-agonist bridge** — inpatient methadone, with transition decisions deferred. Case series support low-dose initiation specifically in fentanyl-using patients, including transitions off methadone, though RCT-level evidence is still thin — model honest appraisal of that evidence gap [3].

**Q3. She's admitted on IV antibiotics — what can you legally do with methadone here, and how does the "72-hour rule" extend this in other settings?**

→ *Teaching point:* **Hospitalized patients are exempt from the OTP restriction**: any provider may order methadone for OUD during an admission for another condition. Start 20–30 mg day 1 (lower with sedation/xylazine concerns), reassess q2–4h for withdrawal vs sedation, titrate cautiously given methadone's long, variable half-life and QTc liability (hers is acceptable at 438 ms; recheck after titration). Outside the hospital, the **72-hour rule** permits up to 3 consecutive days of methadone dispensing for withdrawal while arranging linkage — bridge clinics have used it to enable ED throughput, hospital admission tolerance, and even cover during low-dose buprenorphine inductions [1,6].

**Q4. Design her actual regimen. Defend a concrete plan with parameters.**

→ *Teaching point:* A defensible plan: **methadone 20 mg now** with holding parameters (hold for RR < 12, sedation score, SBP < 90 given xylazine), additional 5–10 mg for COWS ≥ 8 after 3–4 h (day-1 max ~30–40 mg inpatient), plus ondansetron, NSAIDs/acetaminophen, and clonidine 0.1 mg q6–8h PRN doubling as xylazine-withdrawal coverage [1,5]. Because she refuses standard buprenorphine induction but is not opposed to buprenorphine *if it doesn't hurt*, offer a **low-dose buprenorphine cross-titration starting while methadone continues** (e.g., 0.5 mg SL day 1 → doubling steps to 12–16 mg by day ~5–7, then stop methadone) [3,6] — the inpatient stay is the ideal protected window. If she declines, methadone with OTP linkage (her job concern: discuss take-home phases, telehealth counseling) or discharge planning around her stated constraints. Key systems point: *the antibiotics course is the engagement opportunity; untreated withdrawal is the #1 driver of AMA discharge in this population.*

**Q5. Where does extended-release buprenorphine or XR-naltrexone fit for her, and what does X:BOT actually show (ITT vs per-protocol)?**

→ *Teaching point:* **X:BOT** (n=570): XR-naltrexone was harder to initiate (72% vs 94% induction success); ITT relapse favored buprenorphine-naloxone (HR 1.36 against XR-NTX), driven almost entirely by induction failures, while per-protocol outcomes were equivalent [4]. Translation: for a fentanyl-using patient who cannot tolerate a 7–10 day opioid-free window, XR-NTX is usually impractical; agonist therapy is the default. **XR-buprenorphine (monthly SC)** is attractive for adherence and has novel rapid-initiation pathways (24–48 h transdermal-bridged protocols in case reports) but requires establishing tolerability first [8]. Choice is shared decision-making anchored on the mortality data: agonist MOUD vs no MOUD, adjusted HR for all-cause mortality ≈ 0.52 [1].

**Q6. The medicine team wants to "treat the withdrawal and let the addiction clinic sort out meds after discharge." How do you push back, and what are the discharge non-negotiables?**

→ *Teaching point:* Withdrawal management without MOUD transition is associated with loss of tolerance, early relapse, and elevated overdose death — framing MOUD initiation as *part of* withdrawal treatment, not an aftercare question, is the consult psychiatrist's job [1,2]. Non-negotiables: MOUD started (or active linkage with first dose arranged), take-home naloxone in hand (community distribution: 25–46% reductions in overdose mortality) [1], wound care follow-up, hepatitis/HIV screening sent, depression follow-up (her PHQ-9 of 16 warrants reassessment once withdrawal resolves — withdrawal inflates depressive symptom scores), and documentation of capacity/AMA risk-mitigation planning if she leaves early. Harm reduction if she resumes use: never alone, test doses, assume xylazine.

**Q7. (If time) She asks about pain control for wound debridement while on methadone. Principles?**

→ *Teaching point:* Continue the maintenance opioid (it treats OUD, not procedural pain), use multimodal analgesia, and add short-acting full agonists at adequate doses when needed — opioid tolerance means *higher*, not lower, requirements. Same principle applies on buprenorphine: modern perioperative consensus favors **continuing buprenorphine** through most procedures rather than stopping it [7].

## Differential (ranked)

1. **Opioid (fentanyl) withdrawal with xylazine co-exposure** in severe OUD — explains the mixed autonomic picture, wound, and panel.
2. **Xylazine withdrawal/intoxication cycling** — contributes sedation and hypotension; underrecognized without expanded testing [5].
3. **Sedative-hypnotic co-withdrawal** (alcohol/benzodiazepines) — must be excluded; seizure/DT risk changes the regimen.
4. **Sepsis from the soft-tissue infection** — hypotension and tachycardia overlap; trend fever curve, lactate, cultures.
5. **Major depressive disorder vs substance-induced depressive episode** — PHQ-9 16; defer definitive diagnosis until post-acute phase, but do not defer safety screening.
6. **Stimulant co-use** — common in the current supply ("speedballing" patterns); affects monitoring and discharge planning.

## Workup & management

- **Monitoring:** serial COWS plus a sedation scale (the xylazine problem is bidirectional); orthostatics; telemetry/QTc follow-up with methadone titration.
- **Labs/studies:** CBC, BMP, LFTs, blood cultures if febrile, CK if prolonged immobilization, hepatitis B/C, HIV, syphilis screening, pregnancy testing when applicable; expanded tox panel including fentanyl and xylazine immunoassays (routine opiate screens miss both).
- **Wound:** surgical/wound-care consult; xylazine-associated ulcers need aggressive local care; image for osteomyelitis if deep.
- **Pharmacotherapy:** inpatient methadone bridge ± low-dose buprenorphine cross-titration as in Q4; adjuncts (ondansetron, NSAIDs, clonidine — the latter covering both opioid-autonomic and xylazine-withdrawal symptoms) [1,5]; avoid XR-NTX given induction barrier [4].
- **Psychiatric:** reassess depression after withdrawal stabilizes; suicide screening now (framed around recognition and safety planning); document capacity assessment approach for threatened AMA.
- **Discharge:** naloxone kit + overdose education; confirmed MOUD continuation appointment (OTP intake or buprenorphine prescriber) *before* the last antibiotic dose; written taper-free plan — no "detox and discharge."

## Facilitator notes (not for learner distribution)

- **Timing:** Part 1 ~8 min (Q1–Q2: the fentanyl/xylazine era mechanics), Part 2 ~10 min (Q3–Q4: regimen design — make residents commit to numbers and parameters), Part 3 ~10 min (Q5–Q7: evidence and systems).
- **Push residents past "consult reflexes":** the interesting failure mode is writing a COWS protocol and signing off. Force the AMA-prevention framing and the MOUD-before-discharge framing.
- **Evidence honesty:** low-dose induction is supported by case series/cohorts, not RCTs [3]; X:BOT per-protocol equivalence is often misquoted as "naltrexone equals buprenorphine" — ITT tells the implementation story [4]. The xylazine evidence base is consensus/meeting-report level [5]. Model stating certainty levels out loud.
- **Pivot questions:** "Same consult but she's 26 weeks pregnant" (methadone or buprenorphine standard of care; low-dose induction case-report level in pregnancy; coordinate with OB). "Same consult but QTc 505" (buprenorphine preference, cardiology input, telemetry). "She leaves AMA on day 2" (72-hour-rule methadone at a bridge clinic, naloxone, explicit return-anytime message [6]).
- **Safety framing:** all suicide/dangerousness content stays at recognition → escalation → safety planning; no method detail. No street-dosing specifics beyond what clinical assessment requires.
