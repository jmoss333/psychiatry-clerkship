## Acute Agitation & Delirium in the ED: Etiology-Driven Pharmacotherapy, the Droperidol Renaissance, and What We Still Miss

*De-identified synthetic teaching case. No real patient details. Based on articles retrieved from PubMed; DOI links appear in the reference list.*

## Learner-Facing Case Stem

**Setting:** Tertiary-care ED, 23:00. You are the psychiatry resident on consult-liaison call, paged simultaneously about two patients.

**Patient A** is a 74-year-old man with hypertension, BPH, and no psychiatric history, brought in for two days of fluctuating confusion, now combative — pulling lines, striking at staff, intermittently somnolent. T 38.1 °C, HR 104. Started on oxybutynin five days ago. Inattentive on exam (cannot sustain months backward); dry mucous membranes; suprapubic tenderness. The ED has already drawn labs and asks you to "give us something to calm him down — we were going to use lorazepam 2 mg IV."

**Patient B** is a 28-year-old man brought by police after an altercation, agitated and diaphoretic, pacing, hypervigilant, with pressured hostile speech and dilated pupils. HR 126, BP 162/94, T 37.6 °C. He refuses oral medication, and attempts at verbal engagement are failing. Security is at the bedside. No collateral yet; unknown ingestion vs. primary psychiatric illness.

The ED attending asks you to advise on immediate pharmacologic management for both, and — for Patient A — whether this is "a psych admission."

---

## Guided Discussion Questions (with Teaching Points)

**Q1. Frame the two patients: what distinct agitation syndromes do they represent, and why does etiology drive drug choice?**

*Teaching point:* Project BETA's central axiom is that agitation is a symptom, and pharmacotherapy should target the most likely cause: delirium (treat the underlying condition; low-dose antipsychotic if needed; avoid benzodiazepines except in sedative-hypnotic/alcohol withdrawal), intoxication with CNS stimulants (benzodiazepines favored), intoxication with CNS depressants like alcohol (avoid stacking benzodiazepines; antipsychotic preferred if drug needed), and agitation from primary psychiatric illness (antipsychotic ± benzodiazepine, PO before IM) [2]. Patient A is hypoactive-hyperactive mixed delirium; Patient B is undifferentiated agitation with a sympathomimetic toxidrome high on the differential.

**Q2. The ED plans lorazepam 2 mg IV for Patient A. Talk them out of it — or into something better.**

*Teaching point:* In non-withdrawal delirium, particularly in older adults, benzodiazepines deepen and prolong the confusional state and increase fall and respiratory risk [2]. If nonpharmacologic measures fail and behavior threatens safety, BETA supports low-dose antipsychotic (e.g., haloperidol 0.5–1 mg, or comparable low-dose SGA), with ECG/QTc awareness, EPS monitoring, and the explicit understanding that the definitive treatment is source control: stop the anticholinergic, relieve retention, treat the presumed UTI. Also model consult-liaison technique: give the ED a concrete order set and contingencies rather than a veto.

**Q3. For Patient B, refusing PO and failing de-escalation: compare the IM options and their evidence.**

*Teaching point:* Head-to-head ED data are unusually good here. Klein et al. (prospective, n=737) found IM midazolam 5 mg sedated more patients at 15 minutes than haloperidol (5 or 10 mg) or ziprasidone 20 mg, with olanzapine 10 mg close behind; adverse events were uncommon across arms [3]. Taylor et al.'s double-blind RCT found IV midazolam-droperidol combination faster than droperidol or olanzapine alone [4]. Cole et al.'s drug-shortage natural experiment (n=1,257) found IM droperidol 5 mg and olanzapine 10 mg comparably effective, with droperidol requiring fewer additional doses and no torsades [5]. Synthesis: benzodiazepine or midazolam-based regimens buy speed at the cost of respiratory depression and no antipsychotic effect; droperidol and olanzapine are fast, effective single agents; match choice to suspected etiology and monitoring capacity.

**Q4. The pharmacy pages: "droperidol — isn't that black-boxed?" What is the actual state of the QT question?**

*Teaching point:* The 2001 FDA boxed warning was based largely on case reports at high/off-label doses; subsequent ED literature and the drug's return to availability have driven re-adoption. Reviews of the reintegration of droperidol conclude that at ED agitation doses (2.5–10 mg) torsades is rare, and large ED cohorts (including Cole et al.) observed no torsades events [5–7]. Reasonable practice: know that the boxed warning is still on the label (pre-dose 12-lead ECG, ECG monitoring for 2–3 h) and that agitation is an off-label use; follow an institutional ED droperidol protocol, screen for known long-QT/recent QT-prolonging drug load when feasible, obtain ECG when the clinical situation allows, monitor after dosing, and avoid routine "QTc paralysis" that delays treatment of dangerous agitation. Residents should be able to articulate both the warning's origin and the evidence limits.

**Q5. Where does ketamine fit — and what are its liabilities?**

*Teaching point:* Meta-analysis of ED/prehospital data shows dissociative-dose ketamine (typically 4–5 mg/kg IM) achieves adequate sedation in roughly 5 minutes — faster than antipsychotic/benzodiazepine comparators — but with meaningful rates of intubation, emergence phenomena, hypersalivation, and vomiting; intubation risk appears dose- and setting-dependent [6]. Position ketamine as a rescue/severe-end option ("when the patient is a danger within minutes"), used with airway-capable staffing, not a first-line agent for routine agitation. Note the ethical scrutiny of prehospital ketamine use and the importance of protocolized indications.

**Q6. Patient A's delirium: what does the evidence say about ED recognition, screening, and why we miss it?**

*Teaching point:* ED clinicians historically miss delirium in up to ~75% of cases [8]. Two-step screening — the highly sensitive Delirium Triage Screen followed by the highly specific brief Confusion Assessment Method (bCAM: altered mental status/fluctuation + inattention + either altered LOC or disorganized thinking) — has strong test characteristics in older ED patients (bCAM specificity ~96%, +LR ~20) [8]. Systematic review supports feasible ED screening instruments but no single gold-standard workflow [9]; qualitative work shows misses cluster around hypoactive presentations, attribution to dementia or "baseline," and absence of collateral [10]. Narrative reviews emphasize prevention bundles (orientation, sensory aids, sleep, mobility, medication hygiene) over pharmacologic prophylaxis, for which evidence remains poor [11].

**Q7. Design the disposition and documentation for both patients — including restraint governance and the consult note's medicolegal spine.**

*Teaching point:* Patient A: medical admission (urosepsis/anticholinergic delirium workup), not psychiatry; document capacity assessment, least-restrictive-alternatives sequence, specific behavioral indications for each PRN, and deliriogenic-medication reconciliation. Patient B: reassess post-sedation with serial exams and collateral; undifferentiated agitation is a medical evaluation until toxidrome/metabolic causes are excluded; document the de-escalation attempts, indication, agent, dose, response, and monitoring (vitals, SpO2, ECG when obtainable). For any physical restraint for violent behavior (CMS 42 CFR 482.13(e); state law and hospital policy may be stricter): a non-PRN, time-limited order (≤4 h for adults, renewable only after reassessment, up to 24 h before a new in-person physician/LIP assessment), a face-to-face evaluation within 1 hour of initiation, monitoring at the interval hospital policy sets (continuous if restraint and seclusion are combined; never prone; airway, circulation, temperature), release at the earliest safe time, and a post-event debrief as best practice. Frame all dangerousness documentation around risk recognition, escalation pathways, and safety planning [1,2,12].

## Ranked Differential

**Patient A (cause of delirium):**
1. UTI/urosepsis with urinary retention (BPH + new anticholinergic).
2. Anticholinergic (oxybutynin) delirium — peripheral signs present.
3. Metabolic derangement/dehydration (hypernatremia, AKI, hypercalcemia).
4. Structural/intracranial (subdural, stroke) — lower probability without focal signs or anticoagulation; threshold to image if course unexplained.
5. Delirium superimposed on unrecognized major neurocognitive disorder.
6. Nonconvulsive status epilepticus — consider if fluctuation persists despite source control.
7. Alcohol or sedative-hypnotic withdrawal — obtain substance history/collateral before endorsing 'avoid benzodiazepines'.
8. CNS infection (meningitis/encephalitis) — LP if the source is uncertain, meningismus is present, or he fails to improve with source control.
9. Late-onset primary psychiatric illness — diagnosis of exclusion.

**Patient B (undifferentiated agitation):**
1. Sympathomimetic intoxication (methamphetamine, cocaine, synthetic cathinones).
2. Stimulant-induced or primary psychotic disorder exacerbation.
3. Alcohol/sedative withdrawal (agitation + autonomic signs) — history pending.
4. Hypoglycemia or hypoxia — check immediately.
5. Thyroid storm, thermodysregulation, CNS infection — keep on the list if fever climbs or sensorium clouds.
6. Hyperactive delirium of medical origin (metabolic, post-ictal).
7. Traumatic brain injury/intracranial hemorrhage — brought in after an altercation; examine for head trauma before and after sedation.
8. Excited catatonia or serotonin toxicity — check catatonic signs and clonus/hyperreflexia before a dopamine antagonist.

## Workup & Management (Resident Summary)

**Both:** immediate glucose and SpO2; environment control; one-voice de-escalation per the 10 domains [1]; explicit team roles before any hands-on intervention.

**Patient A:** CBC, BMP, Ca/Mg/Phos, UA + culture, blood cultures, bladder scan → catheterize if retention; stop oxybutynin (and audit total anticholinergic burden); ECG for QTc baseline; CXR; head CT only for trauma/anticoagulation/focal signs/unexplained course; consider EEG if fluctuating unresponsiveness persists. If dangerous agitation despite the above: haloperidol 0.5–1 mg IV/IM (or low-dose SGA), reassess q30–60 min, cumulative-dose cap and EPS/QTc vigilance [2] (IV haloperidol is off-label — ECG monitoring); avoid haloperidol if Parkinson disease or Lewy body dementia is known or suspected (baseline parkinsonism or pre-existing hallucinations/fluctuations) — very-low-dose oral quetiapine if an antipsychotic is unavoidable. Avoid benzodiazepines. Delirium prevention bundle on admission [11].

**Patient B:** attempt PO olanzapine or SL/PO SGA first if any engagement possible [2]; if IM required and a sympathomimetic toxidrome is likely: a benzodiazepine first (e.g., midazolam 5 mg IM, with airway monitoring) per BETA [2,3], adding or substituting an antipsychotic if psychotic features predominate; droperidol 5 mg or olanzapine 10 mg IM are effective alternatives (note Cole's cohort was 86% alcohol-intoxicated; do not stack IM olanzapine on a parenteral benzodiazepine) [3,5]; combination midazolam-droperidol shortens time-to-sedation in IV-access settings [4]; dissociative-dose ketamine reserved for immediately life-threatening agitation with airway-capable staffing [6]. Post-sedation: vitals q15 min including temperature, SpO2/capnography where available, ECG when feasible, CK and toxicologic/metabolic workup (hyperthermia and rhabdomyolysis risk, especially if he struggled against restraint; avoid prone positioning), serial reassessment; avoid combining IM olanzapine with a parenteral benzodiazepine (not recommended per FDA label; cardiorespiratory depression), especially in alcohol-intoxicated patients [2].

## Facilitator Notes (Not for Learner Distribution)

- **Flow (25–30 min):** brief dual-stem read (3 min); Q1–Q2 (8 min) — force an etiology-first framework and a concrete counter-proposal to the lorazepam order; Q3–Q5 (12 min) — evidence appraisal: have residents rank the Klein/Taylor/Cole designs by internal validity (observational block design vs. double-blind RCT vs. natural experiment) and defend agent selection; Q6–Q7 (7 min).
- **Push residents on:** (1) naming the *evidence architecture*, not just drug names; (2) numbers needed to articulate — midazolam's 15-minute advantage, bCAM's +LR ~20, ketamine's ~5-minute onset vs. intubation signal; (3) the difference between "QTc caution" and "QTc paralysis"; (4) hypoactive delirium as the miss-prone phenotype [10].
- **PGY-level differentiation:** PGY-1/2 — safe single-agent selection and delirium workup; PGY-3/4 — restraint governance, protocol design, debriefing after restraint events, and systems fixes for delirium screening uptake [9].
- **Safety framing:** all dangerousness content stays on recognition, team safety, escalation protocols, least-restrictive interventions, and post-event debriefing. No restraint-technique or self-harm method detail.
- **Optional stretch:** assign one resident to present the droperidol boxed-warning history [5,7] and one to present ED delirium screening implementation barriers [9,10] at a future journal club.
