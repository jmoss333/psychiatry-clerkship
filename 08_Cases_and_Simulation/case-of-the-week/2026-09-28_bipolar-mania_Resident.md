# Case of the Week — "Electric" (Resident Version)

## Bipolar Mania: Recognition & Acute Management

## Case Stem (Learner-Facing)

Jordan is a 21-year-old college junior admitted from the ED at 3 a.m. after 10 days of 2–3 hours of sleep nightly with subjectively *increased* energy, a 40-page unsolicited "restructuring proposal" emailed to the university provost, $4,800 in impulsive purchases, and escalating irritability when redirected. Speech is pressured and difficult to interrupt; thought process shows flight of ideas with intact but rapid associations. Jordan describes mood as "electric," endorses "God has bigger plans for me," is vague when pressed (mood-congruent grandiosity vs. emerging delusion — not yet fixed), and denies perceptual disturbance, suicidal ideation, and homicidal ideation. Insight is absent; Jordan requests discharge to "file paperwork with the provost's office."

Collateral (parent, with consent): an untreated ~3-month period of low mood, hypersomnia, and academic withdrawal two years ago; a maternal uncle with a bipolar-pattern illness maintained on lithium. Roommates report isolated non-prescribed stimulant use during finals last spring; none known in the past month. Alcohol: social. No known medical illness; no current prescriptions.

In the ED, Jordan received lorazepam 2 mg PO once for escalating agitation with partial effect. Vitals now: HR 98, BP 134/84, T 37.0°C. Neurologic exam nonfocal. Labs: CBC, CMP, TSH pending; urine toxicology pending; urine pregnancy negative. On the unit, Jordan is intrusive with peers, sexually disinhibited in comments to staff, and has slept 90 minutes.

## Guided Discussion Questions

**Q1. Apply DSM-5-TR criteria precisely: what distinguishes this manic episode from hypomania, a mixed-features episode, and schizoaffective disorder? What would "with mixed features" require, and why does it matter therapeutically?**

*Teaching point:* Mania = ≥1 week (or any duration if hospitalization required) of elevated/expansive/irritable mood **plus increased energy/activity**, ≥3 associated symptoms (≥4 if only irritable), with marked impairment, hospitalization, or psychosis. The energy/activity requirement was elevated to a Criterion A element in DSM-5 and retained in DSM-5-TR — mood change alone is insufficient. "With mixed features" requires ≥3 concurrent depressive symptoms during mania; it predicts worse course, higher suicide risk, poorer lithium response, and steers toward valproate or SGAs [1,2]. Schizoaffective disorder requires ≥2 weeks of psychosis in the *absence* of prominent mood symptoms across the illness course — a longitudinal, not cross-sectional, judgment. Jordan's grandiosity is currently mood-congruent and not clearly fixed; document mental status serially rather than committing early to "with psychotic features."

**Q2. The urine tox returns positive for amphetamines. Does this end the diagnostic discussion? Structure the reasoning for substance-induced vs. primary mania with co-occurring use.**

*Teaching point:* No. Favoring primary bipolar I: symptom duration (10 days) far exceeding a stimulant intoxication window, symptoms antedating any known recent use per collateral, prior spontaneous depressive episode, first-degree-adjacent family history, and persistence during observed abstinence on the unit. DSM-5-TR attributes the episode to a substance only when symptoms are *etiologically* tied to intoxication/withdrawal and do not persist beyond it (~1 month benchmark). Practical stance: treat the mania in front of you, re-verify the diagnosis longitudinally, and never let a positive tox screen delay mood stabilization when the syndrome is sustained [1,2]. Also consider the reverse causality: prodromal/manic disinhibition drives substance use.

**Q3. Rank the pharmacologic options for acute mania using the comparative-efficacy evidence, then commit to a regimen for Jordan with doses and monitoring.**

*Teaching point:* The Cipriani multiple-treatments meta-analysis (68 RCTs, n=16,073) found antipsychotics overall superior to mood stabilizers for speed/magnitude of antimanic effect — haloperidol, risperidone, and olanzapine ranked highest for efficacy; gabapentin, lamotrigine, and topiramate were no better than placebo [4]. Network meta-analytic data also support **combination (Li/VPA + SGA) over monotherapy** for response in severe mania, at the cost of more adverse effects [5]. CANMAT/ISBD 2018 first-line: lithium, quetiapine, divalproex, asenapine, aripiprazole, paliperidone, risperidone, cariprazine, mono or in combination [1]. A defensible plan for Jordan: **lithium + risperidone** — lithium for the classic euphoric phenotype, family lithium response signal, and unique anti-suicide/maintenance evidence; risperidone for rapid antimanic control. Start lithium 300 mg BID–TID (young, normal renal function), trough level 5 days after initiation/dose change, acute target ~0.8–1.2 mEq/L; risperidone 1–2 mg nightly titrated to 2–4 mg. Baselines: creatinine/eGFR, TSH, calcium, weight/BMI, A1c, lipids, ECG as indicated, and pregnancy testing already done. Discuss lamotrigine's *absence* of antimanic efficacy — a common resident error imported from maintenance/depression indications [1,4].

**Q4. Defend or reject: "Start valproate instead — it loads faster." Include pharmacokinetics, teratogenicity, and the patient-specific counterargument.**

*Teaching point:* Valproate can be orally loaded (20–30 mg/kg/day) reaching therapeutic levels within days, is preferred in mixed features and possibly in irritable/dysphoric phenotypes, and requires LFTs, CBC (thrombocytopenia), and ammonia if mental status worsens. Against it here: classic euphoric mania and family lithium response predict lithium benefit; valproate is a major teratogen (neural tube defects, neurodevelopmental harm) requiring explicit counseling and contraception planning in any patient who can become pregnant — several guidelines now discourage it in this population when alternatives exist [1,2,3]. The speed argument is largely neutralized by pairing lithium with an SGA. There is no single right answer; the exercise is articulating *why*.

**Q5. Jordan remains intrusive and sleeps 90 minutes despite day 3 of treatment. Discuss the management of inadequate early response, the role of PRNs, and when ECT enters the algorithm.**

*Teaching point:* Expected antimanic response begins within 1–2 weeks; early moves are dose optimization (verify lithium level; risperidone to upper range), scheduled sleep protection (benzodiazepine or SGA at night — sleep restoration is both marker and mechanism of recovery), and elimination of covert antidepressants/stimulants/caffeine. If a first-line combination fails at adequate dose/level/duration, switch the SGA or the mood stabilizer rather than stacking a third agent reflexively [1,3]. **ECT** is effective for treatment-refractory mania, delirious/exhaustive mania, high-lethality states, catatonic features, and pregnancy where medication risk is problematic; RANZCP endorses it across all acute phases [3]. Avoid premature "treatment resistance" labels before confirming adherence, level, and duration.

**Q6. Outline the transition from acute to maintenance treatment. What does the maintenance network meta-analysis actually show, and how do you counsel Jordan — a 21-year-old facing possible lifelong illness after one episode?**

*Teaching point:* Kishi et al. (41 RCTs, n=9,821) found most active treatments outperform placebo for any-mood-episode recurrence; lithium and quetiapine show broad-spectrum prevention, lamotrigine is preferentially antidepressive-prophylactic, and SGA+Li/VPA combinations outperform Li/VPA alone for relapse prevention [6]. Continue what worked acutely (with exceptions — e.g., taper antipsychotics used purely for agitation if maintenance risk-benefit is unfavorable), keep lithium maintenance levels ~0.6–0.8 mEq/L, and monitor renal/thyroid function every 6–12 months [1,6]. Counseling a first-episode young adult: frame maintenance as relapse-risk reduction with shared decision-making, name the high recurrence risk after a first manic episode, emphasize sleep/circadian regularity and substance avoidance as modifiable triggers, and pre-agree on early-warning signs with family. The Lancet 2025 seminar emphasizes patient-centered goals and psychosocial interventions (psychoeducation, IPSRT, family-focused therapy) as evidence-based adjuncts, not afterthoughts [2,7].

**Q7. Suicide risk in bipolar disorder concentrates outside the manic phase. Build the risk-management plan for this admission and the first 90 days post-discharge.**

*Teaching point:* Approximately 15–20% of people with bipolar disorder die by suicide, with risk concentrated in depressive and mixed states, early illness, and the immediate post-discharge window [2]. For Jordan: structured risk screening now and serially (not only at intake), explicit reassessment as the mania breaks — the switch into post-manic depression is a predictable danger point; collaborative safety planning before discharge (warning signs, internal coping, social contacts, professional contacts, means safety addressed as a category with family involvement — no method-level detail in teaching or documentation beyond what care requires); lithium's specific anti-suicide evidence as a maintenance consideration [1,2]; a booked (not "recommended") follow-up within 7 days; and family psychoeducation naming the post-discharge period as high-risk. Escalation rule for the team: any emergent suicidal ideation triggers immediate attending-level review and level-of-care reassessment.

## Ranked Differential Diagnosis

1. **Bipolar I disorder, current episode manic, moderate–severe** — duration, DIGFAST density, impairment, prior probable major depressive episode, family history; mood-congruent grandiosity not yet clearly delusional.
2. **Primary mania with co-occurring stimulant use** (dual diagnosis) — the tox-positive variant of #1; most likely synthesis if symptoms persist in verified abstinence.
3. **Stimulant-induced bipolar-type episode** — requires symptom resolution within the intoxication/withdrawal window; 10-day sustained course argues against as sole etiology.
4. **Mania secondary to another medical condition** — hyperthyroidism, autoimmune/limbic encephalitis, CNS lesion, corticosteroid exposure; screen with TSH, CMP, exam; MRI/EEG only for atypical features (age >40 at onset, focality, fluctuating consciousness).
5. **Schizoaffective disorder, bipolar type** — cannot be established cross-sectionally; requires ≥2 weeks psychosis without prominent mood symptoms longitudinally.
6. **First-episode psychosis with agitation** — grandiose content could herald a primary psychotic illness; the mood-syndrome dominance and episodicity favor bipolar I.
7. **Cluster B–pattern impulsivity / ADHD** — trait-level, non-episodic; does not produce decreased *need* for sleep; include to teach the episodic-vs-trait discrimination.

## Workup & Management

**Admission workup:** CBC, CMP, TSH, urine toxicology (done, amphetamine-positive), urine hCG (negative), consider vitamin B12, blood alcohol if indicated; ECG for QTc and pre-lithium baseline; weight/BMI, A1c, lipids before SGA continuation; no routine neuroimaging for a classic first manic episode in a young adult absent atypical features [1,2].

**Acute management algorithm:**

1. Discontinue any pro-manic agents (antidepressants, stimulants, corticosteroids where possible); protect sleep pharmacologically.
2. Severe mania with risk behaviors → **combination therapy: lithium or divalproex + first-line SGA** [1,5]; monotherapy acceptable for less severe presentations.
3. Agitation: staged approach — de-escalation, oral SGA ± lorazepam, IM only on refusal with imminent risk; avoid stacking QTc-prolonging IM agents; document least-restrictive rationale [3].
4. Reassess at adequate level/dose/duration before declaring failure; then switch within first-line, then ECT for refractory/urgent cases [1,3].
5. Transition planning: maintenance agent selection per Kishi NMA and CANMAT hierarchy; metabolic and renal/thyroid surveillance calendar; relapse signature + early-warning plan documented [1,6].

## Facilitator Notes (Do Not Distribute to Learners)

- **Flow (25–30 min):** Stem 4 min; Q1–Q2 diagnostic block 8 min; Q3–Q5 pharmacology block 10 min; Q6–Q7 longitudinal/safety block 8 min. For a PGY-1-heavy group, spend longer on Q3; for senior residents, push Q4's defend-or-reject format and Q6 counseling role-play.
- **Anticipated sticking points:** (a) lamotrigine reflex — no antimanic efficacy [4]; (b) treating the tox screen instead of the syndrome; (c) forgetting pregnancy counseling with valproate; (d) "with psychotic features" over-calling on mood-congruent grandiosity; (e) omitting the antidepressant-discontinuation step because no antidepressant is prescribed — ask "what if Jordan had been started on an SSRI for the earlier depression?" to surface switch-risk teaching [1,2].
- **Evidence-appraisal micro-teach (optional, 5 min):** contrast Cipriani 2011 [4] (acute mania, antipsychotics fastest) with Kishi 2020 [6] (maintenance, lithium/quetiapine broad-spectrum) to show why "best drug" is phase-dependent; note NMA assumptions (transitivity, trial-population differences) as limitations.
- **Safety framing:** keep all suicide-related content at recognition, structured screening, escalation pathways, and safety-plan architecture. No method-level discussion in the session or in learner materials.
- **Documentation pearl:** serial mental status exams that separately track mood, sleep quantity, speech, and delusion fixity make response (or non-response) legible for the next team — model one entry live if time allows.
