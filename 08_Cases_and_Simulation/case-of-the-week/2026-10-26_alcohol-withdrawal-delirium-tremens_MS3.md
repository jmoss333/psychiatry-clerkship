# Case of the Week — The Shaky Post-Op Patient (MS3 Version)

## Alcohol Withdrawal & Delirium Tremens: Recognition, Risk Stratification, and First-Line Management

*De-identified synthetic teaching case. All details are fictional composites — no real patient information. Based in part on articles retrieved from PubMed; see the numbered references with DOI links at the end.*

**Learner level:** MS3 (psychiatry clerkship) · **Discussion time:** ~20–30 minutes · **Format:** facilitated small group

---

## Stem (learner-facing)

**Part 1 — Initial presentation.** A 52-year-old man is admitted to the orthopedic service after open reduction and internal fixation of an ankle fracture sustained in a fall. On hospital day 2, the primary team calls psychiatry because he is anxious, asking repeatedly to go home, and "shaking." On your exam he is tremulous, diaphoretic, and irritable but fully oriented. Vital signs: HR 112, BP 158/94, T 37.4°C, RR 18, SpO2 98%. He describes nausea and a pounding headache. He did not disclose alcohol use on admission; when asked privately and nonjudgmentally, he reports drinking "six to eight beers most nights, more on weekends" for many years. His last drink was the evening before admission, roughly 36 hours ago.

**Part 2 — Additional data.** Labs: AST 98 U/L, ALT 44 U/L (AST:ALT > 2:1), GGT elevated, MCV 102 fL, platelets 118 ×10⁹/L, magnesium 1.4 mg/dL, potassium 3.3 mEq/L, glucose 92 mg/dL. Blood alcohol level on admission was zero. He has no prior documented withdrawal seizures or delirium tremens, but says he once got "real sick, shaking bad" when he stopped drinking a few years ago.

**Part 3 — Course.** Overnight, before a treatment plan is in place, he has a single generalized tonic–clonic seizure lasting about 90 seconds. By hospital day 3 he is disoriented to place and time, picking at the bedsheets, reporting that insects are crawling on the walls, with HR 134 and T 38.1°C.

## Guided discussion

**Q1. What features of Part 1 point toward alcohol withdrawal rather than ordinary post-operative anxiety or pain?**
→ *Teaching point:* Alcohol withdrawal is a syndrome of **autonomic hyperactivity** (tachycardia, hypertension, fever, diaphoresis) plus tremor, nausea, anxiety, and insomnia, beginning roughly **6–36 hours after the last drink**. Hospitalized patients often under-report alcohol use at admission, so unexplained autonomic signs on hospital day 1–3 should always prompt a focused, nonjudgmental alcohol history and a validated screen (e.g., AUDIT-C) [1,2].

**Q2. What is the expected timeline of alcohol withdrawal phenomena, and where is this patient on it?**
→ *Teaching point:* Classic sequence after the last drink: **minor withdrawal symptoms 6–36 h; withdrawal seizures 6–48 h; alcoholic hallucinosis 12–48 h (with a clear sensorium); delirium tremens 48–96 h**, sometimes later. At 36 hours with intact orientation, this patient is in minor-to-moderate withdrawal — the window where adequate treatment prevents progression [1,2].

**Q3. How would you risk-stratify him — what factors predict severe or complicated withdrawal?**
→ *Teaching point:* Red flags for complicated withdrawal include **prior withdrawal seizures or delirium tremens**, heavy prolonged daily use, presentation while still symptomatic despite a blood alcohol level of zero, marked autonomic signs, older age, acute medical/surgical illness, and electrolyte derangements (hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia). His possible prior severe withdrawal episode, daily heavy use, surgical stress, and low Mg/K make him **high risk**, warranting proactive (not wait-and-see) treatment [1,2].

**Q4. What is first-line pharmacotherapy, and how is it dosed and monitored?**
→ *Teaching point:* **Benzodiazepines are first-line** — they are cross-tolerant with alcohol at the GABA-A receptor and are the only drug class proven to prevent both withdrawal seizures and delirium tremens. Long-acting agents (diazepam, chlordiazepoxide) self-taper; **lorazepam or oxazepam are preferred in advanced liver disease**. Dosing is usually **symptom-triggered** using a validated scale such as the **CIWA-Ar** (treat when score ≥ ~8–10), which lowers total benzodiazepine exposure versus fixed schedules in monitored settings — though fixed or front-loaded dosing is appropriate for high-risk patients or where frequent scoring is impractical [1,2,3].

**Q5. Besides benzodiazepines, what supportive measures does every patient like this need?**
→ *Teaching point:* **Thiamine before (or with) glucose** — parenteral thiamine to prevent Wernicke encephalopathy (confusion, ataxia, ophthalmoplegia; most patients do NOT show the full triad) — plus folate, multivitamins, and repletion of **magnesium and potassium**. Treat in a quiet, well-lit room with frequent reorientation, fall and aspiration precautions, and scheduled reassessment [1,2,4].

**Q6. In Part 3 the patient becomes disoriented, febrile, and hallucinating. What is this, and how does it differ from alcoholic hallucinosis?**
→ *Teaching point:* This is **delirium tremens (withdrawal delirium)**: fluctuating **disturbed sensorium** plus severe autonomic instability, typically 48–96 h after the last drink, with mortality of roughly 1–4% when treated — historically far higher untreated. **Alcoholic hallucinosis**, by contrast, features hallucinations with a **clear sensorium** and stable vitals. DT is a medical emergency: escalate to a monitored setting, give rapidly titrated IV benzodiazepines, and search for precipitants (infection, trauma, metabolic derangement) [1,2,3].

**Q7. Once he stabilizes, what must the discharge plan include?**
→ *Teaching point:* Withdrawal management alone is not treatment of **alcohol use disorder**. Before discharge: brief intervention, offer of **naltrexone or acamprosate**, referral to addiction treatment and mutual-help resources, and clear documentation of this withdrawal episode (it predicts worse future withdrawal — kindling). A withdrawal admission is a high-yield engagement moment [1,2].

## Differential (ranked, with discriminators)

1. **Alcohol withdrawal syndrome → delirium tremens** — timeline from last drink, autonomic signs, tremor, risk factors, response to benzodiazepines.
2. **Post-operative delirium of other cause** (infection, hypoxia, PE, medications, pain) — can coexist; look for focal signs, hypoxia, leukocytosis/cultures; delirium here is hypoactive as often as hyperactive and lacks the tremor–diaphoresis–hypertension cluster.
3. **Sedative-hypnotic (benzodiazepine/barbiturate) withdrawal** — nearly identical physiology; distinguish by medication history.
4. **Alcoholic hallucinosis** — hallucinations with clear sensorium and stable vitals, earlier onset (12–48 h).
5. **Thyroid storm / sympathomimetic intoxication / serotonin syndrome** — autonomic overlap; check TSH, tox screen, medication list, reflexes/clonus.

## Workup & management (MS3 essentials)

- **Workup:** CIWA-Ar scoring; BMP + Mg/Phos, LFTs, CBC, glucose, blood alcohol level, urine tox; ECG (QTc before antipsychotics); lipase if abdominal pain; head CT if focal deficit, head trauma, or atypical/first seizure outside the 6–48 h window; infectious workup if febrile beyond low-grade.
- **Management ladder:** (1) symptom-triggered or front-loaded **long-acting benzodiazepine**; (2) IV escalation and monitored bed for DT; (3) **thiamine before glucose**, folate, Mg/K repletion; (4) treat the whole patient — pain control, fracture care, precipitant search; (5) safety: fall/aspiration precautions, 1:1 sitter if needed, avoid restraints when possible; (6) discharge-ready AUD treatment plan (naltrexone/acamprosate + referral).
- **Safety note:** adjunctive antipsychotics (e.g., haloperidol) may calm agitation or hallucinosis but **lower the seizure threshold and do not treat withdrawal** — never use them as monotherapy [1,2].

## Facilitator notes (not for learner handout)

- **Timing:** Part 1 ~8 min (recognition + history-taking skills), Part 2 ~7 min (risk stratification + labs), Part 3 ~10 min (DT emergency + disposition). Reveal in stages.
- **Common MS3 misconceptions to probe:** (a) "wait until CIWA is high to start treatment" in a high-risk patient; (b) confusing alcoholic hallucinosis with DT — the discriminator is **sensorium**; (c) forgetting thiamine-before-glucose; (d) treating agitation with antipsychotic monotherapy; (e) assuming a zero blood alcohol level excludes alcohol-related illness — it raises withdrawal risk when symptoms are already present.
- **Pivot questions:** "What would change if his last drink were 2 hours ago?" (intoxication vs withdrawal timeline); "What if his LFTs showed cirrhosis?" (lorazepam/oxazepam); "The nurse asks for PRN haloperidol instead of 'more benzos' — how do you respond?" (seizure threshold, treats symptom not syndrome).
- **Board pearl to land:** USMLE loves the 48–96 h DT window, thiamine-before-glucose, and long-acting benzodiazepines as first-line.
- Keep all dangerousness content framed around recognition, escalation, and safety planning.
