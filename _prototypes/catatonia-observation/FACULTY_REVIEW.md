## What the faculty is reviewing

The educational aim is to practice describing what is **Seen**, **Stated** in the narration, or **Reported** by another person, while leaving unsupported conclusions **Unknown**. Each scene is a compressed, schematic illustration with text narration. Its exercise is not a clinical examination or BFCRS score. The categories below are the prototype's current teaching key, submitted for faculty judgment.

Questions for the whole set:

- Is each scene plausible and useful for the intended learner audience without resembling an identifiable patient or staff member?
- Does any illustration, caption, choice, or explanation imply a diagnosis, rule-out, intent, communication capacity, or usual baseline that the scene cannot establish?
- Are the four time windows and the distinction between directly seen, narrated, and collateral information clear?
- Is the interaction usable by keyboard, screen reader, and on a narrow screen? Does its visual pacing support learning without suggesting that these are standardized examination videos?
- Should pressing **Check my description** with no choices selected reveal every answer and explanation immediately, or should the learner make a choice first?
- Is the link to official training prominent enough for supervised, formal assessment?

The [2023 British Association for Psychopharmacology consensus guideline](https://www.bap.org.uk/pdfs/BAP_Guidelines-Catatonia.pdf) supports seeking collateral history to clarify time course, careful observation and examination, and avoiding an automatic inference of wilful non-engagement from limited behavior. It does **not** validate this fictional exercise or its wording. [URMC's official BFCRS resources](https://www.urmc.rochester.edu/psychiatry/divisions/collaborative-care-and-wellness/bush-francis-catatonia-rating-scale) provide the formal training materials and calculator. The repository's `instrument_rights.json` marks BFCRS **restricted** and **link-only**; [URMC's Web Terms of Use](https://www.urmc.rochester.edu/privacy/disclaimer) require prior written consent for redistribution or reproduction outside the stated exception. This prototype must retain original artwork and text, with no BFCRS items, anchors, scoring, or copied training visuals.

## 1. A brief greeting

### Exact moment captions

1. **Arrival** — A clinician approaches a seated person. We do not know the person’s usual behavior or why they are here.
2. **Greeting** — The clinician says, “Good morning. I’m checking in.” The person remains seated and looks toward their lap.
3. **Pause** — In this fictional scene, the person does not speak during the short pause. The scene does not establish intent, cause, or how long this has lasted.
4. **Describe** — Record only what the brief scene shows. A real assessment needs more observation, context, and supervision.

| Seen | The person’s gaze appears directed downward in this illustration. | The downcast eyes and text narration show a gaze toward the lap; do not infer its cause. |

## 4. Two witnesses

### Exact moment captions

1. **Earlier report** — Two sources will be compared: a nurse’s earlier account and a bedside look now. The account is reported history, not something we witnessed.
2. **Nurse speaks** — The nurse reports seeing the person walk to the doorway earlier. Keep the source attached to that account.
3. **Bedside look** — At the bedside now, the clinician sees the person seated.
4. **Keep sources clear** — The report and bedside observation describe different times. Write down who saw what and when; ask about the intervening period.
