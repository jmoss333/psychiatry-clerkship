# Eating Disorders on the Inpatient Unit

> **Source:** standard inpatient teaching aligned with APA, the Society for Adolescent Health and Medicine (SAHM), and MARSIPAN medical-instability guidance. Educational; fictional composites only, no PHI. Reviewed and attested by Joshua Moss, MD (2026-07-01).

**Why this page exists** — Anorexia nervosa (AN), bulimia nervosa (BN), and binge-eating disorder (BED) are reliably tested on the shelf/COMAT and are genuinely encountered on adult inpatient units — often *co-morbid* with the mood, anxiety, OCD, substance, or trauma presentation that prompted admission. AN carries one of the **highest mortality rates of any psychiatric illness** (medical complications *and* suicide), so recognition and medical triage are the student-level priorities. Frame the illness as a life-threatening condition the patient is struggling *with*, not a choice — the voice on the unit should be warm and non-shaming.

**Recognize the three you must know**
- **Anorexia nervosa** — energy restriction → significantly low weight, intense fear of weight gain (or persistent behavior preventing it), and disturbed body image. Two subtypes: *restricting* and *binge-eating/purging*. Weight can be normalized by height as %median BMI; don't require a specific BMI cutoff to take it seriously.
- **Bulimia nervosa** — recurrent binges with compensatory behavior (vomiting, laxatives, fasting, over-exercise), self-evaluation over-tied to shape/weight. **Weight is often normal or high — a normal weight does not rule it out.** Look for Russell's sign (knuckle calluses), parotid enlargement, and dental enamel erosion.
- **Binge-eating disorder** — recurrent binges with distress but *no* regular compensatory behavior; the most common eating disorder. (Keep **ARFID** — avoidant/restrictive intake without body-image disturbance — on the differential, especially in neurodevelopmental presentations.)

**Initial workup** — Weight and vitals including **orthostatics and temperature**; a focused cardiac and volume exam. Labs: CMP with **phosphate, magnesium, and potassium**, glucose, CBC, and an **ECG for QTc and bradyarrhythmia**; add LFTs, amylase/lipase, and pregnancy testing as indicated. Get collateral and (in younger patients) the growth chart. The electrolyte pattern is a tell: **purging → hypokalemic, hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis;** laxative misuse can instead give a metabolic acidosis; dehydration and hypoglycemia are common. Establish the psychiatric picture too — screen for depression, anxiety, OCD, substance use, trauma, and **suicide risk**, which is elevated across eating disorders.

**Medical instability — the admission question** — The high-yield student skill is recognizing who needs a *medical* bed. Criteria that commonly trigger medical admission (confirm against your institution's protocol) include **bradycardia (often <40–50 bpm), hypotension, orthostatic vital-sign changes, hypothermia, hypoglycemia, electrolyte derangement (K⁺, phosphate, Mg²⁺), QTc prolongation or arrhythmia, marked or rapid weight loss, syncope, dehydration, and failure of outpatient/PHP care.** Bradycardia is one of the most common reasons for medical admission in AN. Match the *level of care* to acuity: medical floor for instability, psychiatric/behavioral eating-disorder unit or residential/PHP for weight and behavioral work once medically stable.

**Refeeding syndrome — the complication that kills** — When nutrition restarts, the shift from catabolic to anabolic metabolism drives phosphate, potassium, and magnesium *into* cells; **hypophosphatemia is the hallmark**, and the danger is cardiac failure, arrhythmia, respiratory failure, and delirium. Risk is highest in the **most malnourished** and after the longest starvation. The safe posture is **"start low, go slow"**: begin conservative caloric refeeding, **check phosphate/potassium/magnesium at least daily early**, **supplement phosphate and give thiamine before or with carbohydrate**, and advance calories per protocol as electrolytes stay stable. Defer exact caloric targets and repletion doses to your institution's refeeding order set — the student job is to *anticipate and monitor*, not to prescribe.

**Acute inpatient management** — Medical stabilization first, then structured nutritional rehabilitation with a **multidisciplinary team** (medicine, nutrition/dietetics, nursing, psychiatry, and often social work). Use **supervised meals and a post-meal observation period**, monitor for covert purging and **water-loading** (which falsely raises weight — a "too-fast" weight jump is a flag), and weigh on a consistent schedule/gown. Psychotherapy is the backbone: **Family-Based Treatment (Maudsley/FBT)** for adolescents with AN, and **CBT-E** for BN and BED. On medications: **no drug is first-line for AN** — weight restoration is; **olanzapine** may modestly help weight and obsessionality in AN; **fluoxetine (target 60 mg/day)** is the classic pharmacotherapy for BN; **lisdexamfetamine** is approved for moderate–severe BED. **Avoid bupropion in patients who purge or are underweight (seizure risk)**, and be cautious with QT-prolonging agents given the baseline cardiac risk.

**What the student does**
- Take and record **orthostatic vitals and temperature**; flag bradycardia or a prolonged QTc to the team.
- Track **phosphate, potassium, and magnesium** during refeeding and know why a falling phosphate matters.
- Attend or provide **meal support** and observe the post-meal interval; document behaviors non-judgmentally.
- Screen for and track **suicidality** and the comorbidities that often drive admission (mood, OCD, substance use, trauma).
- Gather collateral and, in younger patients, the growth chart; compute %median BMI rather than eyeballing weight.
- Practice warm, **non-shaming language** — externalize the disorder ("the eating disorder is telling you…") and avoid comments on appearance or specific numbers.

**Disposition & discharge essentials** — Confirm medical stability and a safe electrolyte/weight trajectory, then secure the right step-down: a specialized eating-disorder program (inpatient behavioral, residential, or PHP/IOP), a named outpatient team (therapist trained in FBT or CBT-E, dietitian, and medical follow-up), a relapse/warning-signs plan, and family engagement with consent. Address means restriction and the suicide-safety plan when risk is present. Communicate the refeeding and monitoring plan explicitly to the receiving team.

**High-yield pearls**
- **Refeeding syndrome = watch the phosphate** — start low, go slow, replete phosphate, give thiamine; the most malnourished are the highest risk.
- **Bradycardia** is a leading reason for medical admission in AN; orthostatic changes, hypothermia, and QTc prolongation also drive the medical-bed decision.
- **Hypokalemic, hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis** = self-induced vomiting until proven otherwise.
- A **normal weight does not rule out bulimia** — look for Russell's sign, parotid swelling, and dental erosion.
- **Fluoxetine 60 mg** is the go-to for BN; **avoid bupropion** when purging or underweight (seizures); **lisdexamfetamine** for BED.
- **AN has among the highest mortality of any psychiatric illness** (medical + suicide) — take medical instability seriously and screen suicide risk.
- Watch for **water-loading**: a weight that jumps too fast can be dilutional, not recovery.
