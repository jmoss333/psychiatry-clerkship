# Anxiety in Clinic: GAD, Social Anxiety and Phobias

> **Review status:** Pending faculty review. Drafted with AI assistance on 2026-11-26; every citation was checked against its PubMed record that day. Educational; fictional composites only, no PHI.
> Learner level: **MS3 core**, with labeled **Resident extension** blocks (shown on both sites).

---

*A 34-year-old teacher sees her primary care clinician for headaches and poor sleep. For a year she has worried most days about her students, her parents' health and money. She has never had a panic attack. She drinks four cups of coffee a day.*

---

## Objectives

1. Diagnose generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) and tell it apart from panic disorder, social anxiety disorder, specific phobia and agoraphobia.
2. Screen an adult for anxiety, and clear the medical and substance mimics.
3. Offer first-line treatment (psychotherapy, an SSRI or SNRI, or both) by preference and access.
4. Explain why benzodiazepines are not first-line, and why stopping a working antidepressant early invites relapse.

## In 30 seconds

- **Common, early and outpatient.** In the US National Comorbidity Survey Replication, lifetime prevalence of any anxiety disorder was 28.8%, with a median age of onset of 11 years[^kessler-2005-ncsr-lifetime-prevalence]. Most are treated in clinics, not on a unit.
- **GAD** is excessive worry about a number of events or activities, more days than not for at least 6 months, that the person finds hard to control, with at least 3 of 6 symptoms: restlessness, easy fatigue, poor concentration, irritability, muscle tension and disturbed sleep[^dsm-5-tr].
- **Screen.** The USPSTF recommends screening adults for anxiety disorders, including pregnant and postpartum persons (grade B); the evidence is insufficient in adults 65 and older (I statement)[^uspstf-2023-anxiety-screening-adults]. A GAD-7 of 10 or more is the usual cutpoint[^kroenke-2010-phq-gad-review]. A positive screen starts a diagnostic interview; it is not a diagnosis.
- **Clear the mimics first:** thyroid, cardiopulmonary, caffeine, stimulants, alcohol or sedative withdrawal, akathisia.
- **Treat.** Psychotherapy and medication both work. For GAD, duloxetine, venlafaxine and escitalopram beat placebo with relatively good acceptability; benzodiazepines were effective but poorly tolerated[^slee-2019-gad-pharmacotherapy-nma].
- **Social anxiety:** individual cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) first, an SSRI if therapy is declined[^mayo-wilson-2014-social-anxiety-nma]. **Specific phobia:** exposure[^wolitzky-taylor-2008-specific-phobia].
- **Don't stop too soon.** Responders switched to placebo relapsed more often within a year than those who stayed on their antidepressant (36.4% vs 16.4%)[^batelaan-2017-antidepressant-discontinuation-anxiety].

## 1 · Which anxiety disorder is it?

Ask what the fear is *about*[^dsm-5-tr]:

| Disorder | The fear is about | Duration | Discriminating question |
|---|---|---|---|
| GAD | Many everyday things: work, health, family, money | At least 6 months | "What do you worry about?" The answer is a list. |
| Panic disorder | The attacks, which come out of the blue | At least 1 month of worry or avoidance after an attack | "Have you had a sudden surge of fear for no reason?" |
| Social anxiety disorder | Being watched, judged or embarrassed | Typically 6 months or more | Note the performance-only specifier. |
| Specific phobia | One object or situation | Typically 6 months or more | Fear is immediate and out of proportion. |
| Agoraphobia | Places where escape or help would be hard | Typically 6 months or more | Occurs with or without panic disorder. |

Ask about depression in every anxious patient: in the primary care study that built the GAD-7, GAD and depressive symptoms frequently co-occurred, yet behaved as distinct dimensions[^spitzer-2006-gad7].

## 2 · Screening and workup in clinic

- **Give the GAD-7 well.** Practise with the official form on the <a href="tools/screeners.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Screeners tool</a>, and treat a score of 10 or more as a reason to interview[^kroenke-2010-phq-gad-review].
- **Take a substance and medication history first:** caffeine, nicotine, stimulants, decongestants, corticosteroids, thyroid hormone, and recent changes in alcohol or benzodiazepine use.
- **Look for medical causes** when the history points there: new anxiety in later life, or with weight loss, chest pain or neurological signs. The workup is on [Anxiety/Trauma/OCD](?page=t_anxiety.md) and [Medical Workup & Mimics](?page=medical_workup.md).

## 3 · Treating GAD

**Offer both.** In a meta-analysis of 79 randomized trials, psychotherapy had a medium-to-large effect on GAD (g = 0.76) and medication a small one (g = 0.38); the authors warn these cannot be compared directly, because medication trials more often used placebo controls[^carl-2020-gad-psychotherapy-medication].

**Medication: an SSRI or SNRI first.** The largest network meta-analysis (89 trials, 25,441 patients) found duloxetine, pregabalin, venlafaxine and escitalopram more effective than placebo with relatively good acceptability[^slee-2019-gad-pharmacotherapy-nma]. Benefit builds over weeks; early jitteriness usually settles. Monitor patients under 25 closely early on: in FDA-held adult trials, the odds of suicidal behavior on an antidepressant were raised under age 25 (odds ratio 2.30) but not at older ages[^stone-2009-antidepressant-suicidality-age].

**Benzodiazepines are not first-line.** They act quickly, but in the network meta-analysis they were poorly tolerated[^slee-2019-gad-pharmacotherapy-nma]; they also carry dependence, falls and sedation. Never stop one abruptly in a patient likely to be physically dependent; plan a taper[^brunner-2025-benzodiazepine-taper].

**Continue what works.** Across anxiety disorders, OCD and PTSD, stopping an antidepressant in responders raised the odds of relapse within a year (odds ratio 3.11); the review says the lack of data beyond a year is not advice to stop at a year[^batelaan-2017-antidepressant-discontinuation-anxiety].

> **Resident extension: reading the GAD network meta-analysis.** Quetiapine had the largest effect on the Hamilton Anxiety Scale but was poorly tolerated (odds ratio 1.44), as were paroxetine and benzodiazepines. Mirtazapine, sertraline, fluoxetine, buspirone and agomelatine looked efficacious, but on small samples. Failure of a first medicine "might not be a reason to abandon a pharmacological treatment strategy"[^slee-2019-gad-pharmacotherapy-nma]. Before switching, check adherence, dose, duration and whether psychotherapy was offered.

> **Resident extension: what the GAD-7 can and cannot do.** In its primary care validation, the chosen cutpoint gave 89% sensitivity and 82% specificity[^spitzer-2006-gad7]. Its sensitivity to change is emerging, not definitive, so track response by interview as well as score[^kroenke-2010-phq-gad-review].

## 4 · Social anxiety, phobias and agoraphobia

- **Social anxiety disorder.** In a network meta-analysis, only individual cognitive behavioral therapy and SSRIs/SNRIs beat an appropriate placebo. The authors call it the best initial treatment, with SSRIs for those who decline therapy[^mayo-wilson-2014-social-anxiety-nma]. For performance-only anxiety, see [Anxiety/Trauma/OCD](?page=t_anxiety.md).
- **Specific phobia.** Exposure-based therapy outperformed placebo and other psychotherapies; in vivo exposure beat imagined or virtual exposure at the end of treatment, though not at follow-up[^wolitzky-taylor-2008-specific-phobia].
- **Panic disorder and agoraphobia.** Cognitive behavioral therapy with interoceptive exposure plus an SSRI or SNRI; see the Panic Disorder Case of the Week (Aug 10).

## What the student does in clinic

- Open with one question ("What's on your mind most days?") before any checklist, then interview for duration, control, the six symptoms and impairment.
- Ask about depression, alcohol, cannabis, sedatives and caffeine every time.

## Self-check

<details>
<summary>1. The teacher has a GAD-7 of 13. What next?</summary>

A diagnostic interview, not a prescription: confirm the criteria, ask about depression and substances, and account for the coffee.
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. She wants "something that works today." What do you offer?</summary>

An SSRI or SNRI, counseling that benefit builds over weeks, plus psychotherapy. A benzodiazepine is quick but poorly tolerated and dependence-forming.
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Eight months later she feels well and wants to stop. What do you say?</summary>

Stopping raises her relapse risk; weigh it against side effects and her preference. If she stops, taper and follow up.
</details>
