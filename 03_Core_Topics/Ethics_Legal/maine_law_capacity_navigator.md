# Maine Law & Capacity: A Statute Navigator for the Inpatient Unit

> **Review status:** Pending faculty review (legal content, added 2026-10-27). Educational; no PHI.

> **Educational summary — not legal advice — hospital policy and counsel govern.** Verify the current statute and consult counsel or institutional policy before acting. Statute text below was read on the Maine Revisor of Statutes site on 2026-10-26 (the Revisor's "data extracted" date on each page is 2026-10-05).

**In one line** — On a Maine inpatient unit, four questions recur: *which admission pathway is this patient in*, *what is the clock*, *who may decide when the patient cannot*, and *when may or must I disclose* — and each has a specific section of Maine statute behind it that you should be able to name before you act.

**How to use this page** — It is a map, not an authority. Each row names the statute, links the official text, and gives a one-line teaching gloss (the gloss is ours; the quoted text is the Legislature's). The interactive version is the [Maine Law Navigator](?tool=maine-law-navigator.html). For the general (all-states) principles, read [Ethics & the Law](?page=ethics_legal.md) first; for the bedside capacity interview and note, use the [Decisional Capacity tool](?tool=capacity.html).

## Admission pathways (Title 34-B, chapter 3, subchapter 4)

| Pathway | Statute | Who starts it | What the statute requires | Teaching gloss |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Informal (voluntary) admission | 34-B §3831; freedom to leave §3832 | The person "desiring admission" (or a guardian, for an adult ward who does not object) | A person under 18 "must have the consent of the person's parent or guardian" (§3831(3)) | A voluntary patient may leave "within 16 hours of the patient's request" unless an emergency application under §3863 is initiated in that window (§3832(1)) |
| Protective custody | 34-B §3862 | A law enforcement officer with probable cause | Officer "shall deliver the person immediately for examination by a medical practitioner" (§3862(1)(B)) | If a certificate is executed, the officer may detain up to **18 hours** to obtain the judicial endorsement (§3862(3)) |
| Extreme risk protection (weapons) | 34-B §3862-A | Law enforcement, after protective custody | A medical practitioner assesses "likelihood of foreseeable harm" | A weapons-restriction pathway that runs alongside, not instead of, admission |
| Emergency involuntary admission ("blue paper") | 34-B §3863 | Any health officer, law enforcement officer "or other person" (application) + a medical practitioner (certificate) | Application + dated certificate + judicial endorsement | Detail and clocks below |
| Judicial commitment | 34-B §3864 | Hospital chief administrative officer (state institute) or the commissioner (designated nonstate hospital) | District Court hearing; clear and convincing evidence | Commitment, not emergency admission, is what a court orders |
| Court-ordered involuntary treatment | 34-B §3864(7-A) | Request in the commitment application (§3864(1-A)) | Four findings, below | Commitment does not by itself authorize treatment over objection |
| Progressive treatment program (PTP) | 34-B §3873-A | Hospital, commissioner, ACT director, medical practitioner, law enforcement officer, or guardian | Severe and persistent mental illness + likelihood of serious harm + a treatment plan and community providers | Court-ordered outpatient plan; order "for a period of up to 12 months" (§3873-A) |

### The emergency procedure ("blue paper"), 34-B §3863, step by step

1. **Application (sub-§1).** States the applicant's belief that the person "is mentally ill and, because of the person's illness, poses a likelihood of serious harm" and the grounds for it.
2. **Certifying examination (sub-§2).** A dated certificate signed by a *medical practitioner* — defined in §3801(4-B) as "a licensed physician, licensed physician associate, certified psychiatric clinical nurse specialist, certified nurse practitioner or licensed clinical psychologist" — stating the person was examined on the date of the certificate, is mentally ill and poses a likelihood of serious harm (with the grounds), and that "adequate community resources are unavailable". The opinion may rest on history from reliable sources, "including, but not limited to, family members".
3. **Judicial review (sub-§3).** A Superior Court justice, District Court judge, Judge of Probate or justice of the peace endorses the papers. Pending endorsement, a person may be held "not to exceed 24 hours" if the endorsement is sought immediately (sub-§3(B)); sub-§3(D)–(E) allow up to 48 more hours, and one further 48-hour period, when no inpatient bed can be found and the department has been notified.
4. **Transport (sub-§4).** After endorsement, transport must use "the least restrictive form of transportation available that meets the clinical needs of that individual".
5. **Notice (sub-§6).** After consulting the person, the hospital notifies a guardian, spouse, parent, adult child, or next of kin/friend — unless notice "would pose risk of harm to the person admitted".
6. **Second opinion (sub-§7).** "If findings required for admission under subsection 2 are not certified in a 2nd opinion by a staff medical practitioner within 24 hours after admission, the person must be immediately discharged."
7. **Continue or convert (sub-§5-A).** If more hospitalization is needed, the hospital "shall first determine if the person may be informally admitted under section 3831." Otherwise an application to the District Court must be "filed within 3 days from the date of admission" (next business day if the third day is a weekend or holiday); "If no application to the District Court is timely filed, the person must be promptly discharged."
8. **Limitation (sub-§9).** "Admission to a psychiatric hospital on an emergency basis under the provisions of this section is not commitment to a psychiatric hospital."

### Definitions that decide the pathway (34-B §3801)

- **Likelihood of serious harm (§3801(4-A)):** (A) "A substantial risk of physical harm to the person as manifested by recent threats of, or attempts at, suicide or serious self-inflicted harm"; (B) substantial risk of physical harm to others shown by recent violent behavior or conduct placing others in reasonable fear; (C) "A reasonable certainty that the person will suffer severe physical or mental harm as manifested by recent behavior demonstrating an inability to avoid risk or to protect the person adequately from impairment or injury"; (D) a deterioration standard that applies only to the PTP.
- **Mentally ill person (§3801(5)):** includes "persons suffering effects from the use of drugs, narcotics, hallucinogens or intoxicants, including alcohol"; a developmental disability or a sociopathy diagnosis is "not for those reasons alone" mental illness.
- **Inability to make an informed decision (§3801(10)):** "being unable to make a responsible decision whether to accept or refuse a recommended treatment as a result of lack of mental capacity to understand sufficiently the benefits and risks of the treatment after a thorough and informative explanation has been given by a qualified mental health professional."

## Capacity, competence and who decides

- **Capacity is a clinical, decision-specific finding; competence is a court's.** Maine's health-care decisions statute defines **capacity** as "the ability to have a basic understanding of the diagnosed condition and to understand the significant benefits, risks and alternatives to the proposed health care and the consequences of forgoing the proposed treatment, the ability to make and communicate a health care decision and the ability to understand the consequences of designating an agent or surrogate to make health care decisions" (18-C §5-802(3)). Teach and document it with the four abilities in the [Decisional Capacity tool](?tool=capacity.html).
- **Involuntary treatment needs a court order with four findings (34-B §3864(7-A)(A)):** "(1) That the person lacks the capacity to make an informed decision regarding treatment; (2) That the person is unable or unwilling to comply with recommended treatment; (3) That the need for the treatment outweighs the risks and side effects; and (4) That the recommended treatment is the least intrusive appropriate treatment option." The court may instead appoint a surrogate for the duration of the commitment.
- **Surrogates (18-C §5-806).** For an adult or emancipated minor whom the primary physician has determined lacks capacity, with no agent or guardian, a reasonably available family member may act as surrogate in the statute's priority order (spouse; a spouse-equivalent adult; adult child; parent; adult sibling; adult grandchild; adult niece or nephew; adult aunt or uncle; another familiar adult relative). A surrogate "may not deny surgery, procedures or other interventions that are lifesaving and medically necessary."
- **Advance directives for mental health treatment (18-C §5-803).** "An advance health care directive is valid for purposes of directing mental health treatment"; the directive is construed with 34-B §§3831 and 3862 (§5-803(9)). For a directive that authorizes mental health treatment, the capacity determination may be made "by a person qualified to conduct an examination pursuant to Title 34‑B, section 3863" (§5-803(4)). An adult with such a directive may be admitted informally for no more than 5 working days under §3831(6).
- **Pitfall:** §3831 lists the person, a guardian (for a non-objecting adult ward) and an advance directive as routes to informal admission; it does not list a §5-806 family surrogate. Do not treat a relative's agreement as a voluntary admission — ask your attending and the hospital's counsel.

## Confidentiality and family (34-B §1207; 22 §1711-C)

- **The default:** mental health records and the facts in them "shall be kept confidential and may not be disclosed by any person," except as the section allows (34-B §1207(1)). Maine's general rule for health information is the same: "An individual's health care information is confidential" (22 §1711-C(2)).
- **Family and caretakers (34-B §1207(5-A)):** a licensed mental health professional caring for an adult client may share information "directly relevant to the person's involvement with the client's care" when a client with capacity consents, does not object when given the chance, or the professional can reasonably infer no objection; or, if the client lacks capacity or an emergency precludes participation, when the professional judges disclosure to be in the client's best interests.
- **Collateral history in is not disclosure out.** Listening to a family member does not breach confidentiality; telling them about the patient is what §1207(5-A) governs.

## Disclosure of danger (the Maine "duty to warn" question)

- **What the statute says (34-B §1207(6-A)):** "A licensed mental health professional shall disclose protected health information that the professional believes is necessary to avert a serious and imminent threat to health or safety when the disclosure is made in good faith to any person, including a target of the threat, who is reasonably able to prevent or minimize the threat."
- **Related provisions:** disclosure to law enforcement for a law-enforcement purpose under the conditions of 45 CFR 164.512(f) (§1207(7)); notifying law enforcement of a belief that a person committed to a state mental health institute has access to firearms (§1207(8)); and Maine's general permission for any treating practitioner to disclose "to avert a serious threat to health or safety" under the conditions of 45 CFR 164.512(j) (22 §1711-C(6)(D)).
- **What this page does not claim.** The text of §1207(6-A) uses "shall," but it is phrased as "shall disclose … when the disclosure is made in good faith", the same construction §1207(7) uses for disclosures that federal law only permits. Whether Maine law imposes an affirmative, enforceable *Tarasoff*-type duty to warn — and whether §1207 reaches every inpatient setting — is a question this page does not answer; we have not verified Maine case law on it. Treat a serious, imminent threat to an identifiable person as an immediate escalation to your attending, and follow hospital policy and counsel on what to disclose and to whom.

## Minors

- **Consent to treatment (22 §1502):** "a minor may consent to treatment for substance use disorder or for emotional or psychological problems." (The Revisor page carries two versions of the cross-references — text effective until 1 January 2027 and text effective 1 January 2027; this sentence is the same in both.)
- **Informal admission is different (34-B §3831(3)):** "Any person under 18 years of age must have the consent of the person's parent or guardian" for informal admission; admission of a minor to a state mental health institute also needs the commissioner's consent (§3831(4)).
- **Pitfall:** §1502 does not make a 16-year-old able to admit themselves voluntarily. Where a minor is placed, and under whose consent, is also hospital policy — a minor on an adult unit is a question for your attending and the hospital, not this page.

## Mandated reporting

- **Child abuse or neglect (22 §4011-A(1)):** health care practitioners and licensed mental health professionals, among others, acting in a professional capacity "shall immediately, within no more than 24 hours, report or cause a report to be made to the department when the person knows or has reasonable cause to suspect that a child has been or is likely to be abused or neglected".
- **Incapacitated or dependent adults (22 §3477(1)):** physicians, medical residents and interns, physician associates and mental health professionals, among others, "immediately shall report to the department when the person knows or has reasonable cause to suspect that an incapacitated or dependent adult has been or is likely to be abused, neglected or exploited". The trigger is incapacity or dependency, not age alone.

## Common pitfalls

- Calling a blue paper a "commitment." It is an emergency admission; §3863(9) says so in terms.
- Letting the 24-hour second-opinion clock or the 3-day filing clock run without a decision. Each ends in mandatory discharge if missed.
- Forgetting §3863(5-A): before seeking commitment, the hospital must first consider informal admission.
- Treating commitment as consent to treat. Involuntary treatment is a separate court finding under §3864(7-A).
- Treating §1502 (a minor's consent to treatment) as authority for a minor's voluntary *admission* (§3831(3)).
- Assuming a family member is a surrogate for psychiatric admission because they are one for medical decisions.
- Citing a "duty to warn" more confidently than the Maine text supports — read §1207(6-A) and escalate.
