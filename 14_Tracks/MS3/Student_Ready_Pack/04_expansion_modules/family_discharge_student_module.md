## Core Idea

Inpatient psychiatry does not end at symptom reduction. A patient leaves into a
system: family, housing, medications, transportation, work/school, finances,
follow-up, stigma, and safety. If the system cannot hold the plan, the plan is
not done.

## Why It Matters — The Numbers

- **The window is highest right after discharge.** Suicide risk in the first week post-discharge is on the order of ~2,950 per 100,000 person-years, roughly 200× the general-population rate (Chung et al., *BMJ Open* 2019; the overall post-discharge rate of 484 per 100,000 person-years is from Chung et al., *JAMA Psychiatry* 2017). The realism of the plan matters *most* in exactly the days after the patient leaves.
- **Follow-up is often the weak link.** Roughly one in eight psychiatric inpatients is readmitted within 30 days (more with schizophrenia), and in many samples only about half attend a mental-health visit within 7 days (e.g., Haselden 2019, NY Medicaid: 53%). In a Medicaid cohort of youths aged 10–18, attending an outpatient mental-health visit within 7 days of discharge was associated with lower suicide risk over the following six months (adjusted RR 0.44, 95% CI 0.23–0.83; Fontanella 2020). Booking a dated appointment before discharge (not "they'll call to arrange one") is a reasonable way to make that visit happen.
- **Means safety is a lethality intervention, not a formality.** Firearms are involved in more than half (~55%) of US suicides and are lethal in 85–90% of attempts; structured lethal-means counseling matters: in a large quasi-experimental cohort (Boggs et al. 2020), patients with documented lethal-means assessment had fewer suicide attempts and deaths at 180 days (0.83% vs 3.3%) — an association, not a randomized effect. This is why the "who can confirm items are secured?" phrase below is a clinical act.
- **Bringing the family in changes outcomes.** Structured family psychoeducation lowers 12-month relapse odds in schizophrenia versus treatment as usual (OR 0.18, 95% CI 0.12–0.27; Rodolico 2022). That evidence comes from multi-session programs: two sessions or fewer did not significantly reduce relapse, and the review excluded trials in which all patients were acutely ill, so it is not evidence about a single inpatient meeting. In 179 New York Medicaid inpatients, any family–staff involvement was associated with higher odds of a 7-day outpatient visit (adjusted OR 2.79, 95% CI 1.28–6.08). This is an association from chart review, not proof of cause (Haselden 2019).

*Numbers are evidence-anchored and reviewed and attested by Joshua Moss, MD (2026-07-09); use them to explain the "why," not to quote at families.* · **Pending re-attestation:** family psychoeducation, family-involvement and 7-day follow-up evidence changed 2026-11-22 (Rodolico 2022; Haselden 2019; Fontanella 2020; Chung 2019)

## What Students Should Notice

Family/support context:

- Who noticed the change?
- Who brought the patient in?
- Who is trusted?
- Who is feared?
- Who minimizes risk?
- Who escalates conflict?
- Who can help with medication, sleep, appointments, transportation, means safety?

Discharge context:

- Where will the patient sleep?
- Who will be there?
- What follow-up is scheduled?
- What medication access exists?
- What transportation exists?
- What warning signs should trigger help?
- What crisis plan is realistic?

## Family Meeting Agenda Template

1. Purpose of meeting.
2. Patient voice: what the patient wants discussed.
3. Current clinical status in plain language.
4. Safety and warning signs.
5. Medication and treatment plan.
6. Family/support roles and limits.
7. Discharge barriers.
8. Follow-up and crisis plan.
9. What remains uncertain.
10. Next steps and who owns each task.

## Student Role In A Family Meeting

Before:

- Ask the team what your role is.
- Review the patient consent/permission plan.
- Write 2-3 questions you may ask.
- Know what not to promise.

During:

- Listen for emotion and mismatch.
- Track concrete tasks.
- Use plain language.
- Do not take sides.
- Ask permission before sensitive topics.

After:

- Summarize what changed.
- Identify unresolved barriers.
- Document only according to local policy.
- Debrief with supervisor.

## Useful Student Phrases

Validation:

> "It makes sense that everyone is exhausted and scared."

Clarifying role:

> "Our goal is not to make the family responsible for treatment. Our goal is to
> understand what support is realistic and what would be unsafe to assume."

Warning signs:

> "What did you notice before hospitalization that told you something was changing?"

Means safety:

> "Who can confirm that medications/firearms/other high-risk items are secured
> before discharge?"

Discharge realism:

> "What part of the plan feels hardest to actually do at home?"

## Family Education Handout Use

Before giving a handout:

- Ask what the patient/family already understands.
- Ask permission to share written material.
- Choose one relevant handout, not a packet dump.

When giving it:

- Say why this handout fits.
- Highlight one section.
- Invite disagreement or questions.

After:

- Ask what felt useful or not.
- Document education only according to local policy.
- Do not substitute a handout for a conversation.

## Common Failure Modes

- Family is supportive but overwhelmed.
- Family agrees in meeting but cannot do the plan.
- Patient consents to collateral but later feels exposed.
- Team assumes housing is stable because an address exists.
- Discharge plan depends on a follow-up appointment that is not actually scheduled.
- Safety plan exists but means safety is unconfirmed.
- Patient improves on unit but sleep plan at home is unrealistic.
