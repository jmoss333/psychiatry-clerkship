## Station 1 - Suicide Risk With Collateral Hesitation

**Time:** 12 minutes encounter, 3 minutes summary, 10 minutes feedback.

**Student task**

Conduct a focused suicide risk assessment, ask permission for collateral, and
state an acute risk impression.

<!-- crisis-block -->

**Patient brief**

29-year-old retail worker admitted after sending a concerning text to a sibling.
The patient says the ED overreacted. Sleep has been 3-4 hours nightly. Cannabis
use increased. A goodbye letter exists but is not volunteered unless asked
directly. Firearm access is at a parent's home.

**Behaviors to practice**

- Opens with role and purpose.
- Asks directly about suicidal thoughts, plan, intent, preparation, past attempts.
- Asks about means access.
- Separates chronic and acute factors.
- Asks permission for collateral.
- Reflects the patient's worry about burdening family.
- Names one next step.

**Rater focus**

Risk formulation, means restriction, collateral consent, tone.

## Station 2 - Capacity To Refuse Medical Treatment

[Run this station](?page=osce.md&station=capacity)

**Time:** 12 minutes encounter, 5 minutes oral capacity summary.

**Student task**

Medicine asks you to see a patient who is declining IV antibiotics.
Assess decision-making capacity for a specific refusal.

**Patient brief**

67-year-old admitted to medicine with infection and new paranoia that IV
antibiotics are poison. The patient is intermittently inattentive and worse at
night. The medical team asks, "Does the patient have capacity to refuse?"

**Behaviors to practice**

- Identifies the exact decision.
- Assesses choice, understanding, appreciation, and reasoning.
- Screens attention and fluctuation.
- Considers delirium and psychosis.
- Avoids equating disagreement with incapacity.
- Presents which capacity ability is impaired.

**Rater focus**

Decision-specific reasoning and delirium recognition.

## Station 3 - Possible Catatonia

[Run this station](?page=osce.md&station=possible-catatonia)

**Time:** 10 minutes observation/interview, 5 minutes team presentation.

**Student task**

Identify catatonia concern and escalate.

**Patient brief**

35-year-old with depression sits motionless, answers rarely, has eaten little,
and intermittently holds the same posture. Nursing reports the patient is
"refusing everything." Vital signs are stable but oral intake is poor.

**Interviewer role**

You are the medical student on the unit. The nurse has asked you to see a
35-year-old admitted with depression who has barely eaten today.

- **Start at the bedside.** Introduce yourself to the patient, explain why you came, and give them time to respond. Notice how they sit, move, and respond to one simple question. If they say little, describe what you saw and heard before asking the nurse for context.
- Ask the nurse, “When you say ‘refusing,’ what happened when you offered breakfast?” Find out what is known about fluids, movement, vital signs, and recent medication changes. Keep your observations separate from the nurse's report.
- **Call for help during the encounter.** Tell the nurse when you would contact the supervising resident or attending and what you want them to assess at the bedside. The presentation timer is for rehearsing the call. You can make the call as soon as you are concerned.
- In the team presentation, give your first-hand observations, the nurse's information, your concern for possible catatonia, and one fact that still needs checking. End with a specific request and listen to what the nurse heard.

**Patient role**

You are seated when the student arrives. Choose a comfortable position with
your hands resting in your lap. You can describe a movement aloud if you
prefer. Nobody needs to touch you or move your limbs for this exercise.

- **How to play the pauses.** When greeted, look toward the interviewer after a pause. For most questions, give a small nod, a short answer, or no reply. If asked what it feels like to move, you can quietly say, “It’s hard to get started.”
- If asked about breakfast, say “A little” after a pause. Let the nurse describe the tray. If the student talks over you, stay in character. If they explain what they are doing and allow time, give one brief response. Keep the difficulty moving and speaking present in both versions.
- In the debrief, step out of character. Quote one thing the interviewer did that gave you room to answer, or identify a moment when you wanted a little more time.

**Nurse role**

You asked the student to come because little has changed since breakfast.
Open with “I'm worried. They seem to be refusing everything today.” Give the
details below in your own words as the student asks.

- **What you saw this shift.** You offered breakfast and a drink. The patient took a few bites and sips. You did not hear them say no. When you came back for the tray, they were still seated with their hands in the same position. You have not seen them walk this shift. Their baseline mobility is unknown.
- The last set of vital signs was stable. You have no newer set to report. If asked about medication exposure, say, “I haven't checked the administration record yet. What changes do you want me to look for?” If the student proposes waiting, bring up the poor intake and ask who should come see the patient now.
- During the team presentation, listen to the student's call to the supervising clinician. Read back what you heard: “You want me to ___ while you contact ___.” Ask for clarification if you missed the action or its urgency.

**Behaviors to practice**

- Describes motor signs objectively.
- Asks about intake, mobility, autonomic signs, and medication exposure.
- Considers catatonia in differential.
- Avoids labeling behavior as "noncompliance."
- Escalates to resident/attending.

**Rater focus**

Recognition and language.

**Handoff debrief**

1. **Repeat the first sentence.** The nurse repeats the student's opening line. The interviewer tries it again, replacing any assumption about “refusal” with one thing they observed directly and one fact the nurse reported.
2. **Finish the call.** Use the response you actually observed in the first sentence. Then make your request. For example, “The nurse reports only a few sips this morning. I'm concerned about possible catatonia and the risk of dehydration. Could you come assess them now? I'll ask the nurse to recheck vitals and help confirm recent medication changes.”
3. **Check what landed.** The nurse says what they would do after the revised call. The patient role-player names the moment when the interviewer made it easiest to respond. Retry that moment once.

## Station 4 - Alcohol Withdrawal Risk On Psychiatry Unit

## Station 5 - Family Meeting Agenda Before Discharge

[Run this station](?page=osce.md&station=family-agenda)

**Time:** 15 minutes prep/presentation.

**Student task**

Prepare a family meeting agenda for a synthetic patient nearing discharge.

**Case brief**

22-year-old admitted for first manic episode. Sleep and agitation have improved.
Family is frightened and divided: one parent wants strict control, another wants
to avoid conflict. Patient wants discharge and refuses to discuss warning signs.

**Presenter role**

You are the medical student. Prepare your agenda, present it to one parent,
and talk through one point where you disagree.

- **Open the meeting.** Introduce yourself and ask, “What would you most like us to figure out before we finish?” Explain that you will bring unresolved questions to the supervising team.
- **Make room for the patient.** The patient wants to go home and has declined to discuss warning signs. In this three-person exercise, say what you would ask the patient directly about the agenda and what they agree to share. Leave those questions open for the team to revisit with the patient.
- **Work through one disagreement.** When the parent raises a concern, ask what worries them most. Use their answer to propose one practical item, such as who they could call if sleep becomes a concern at home. Ask whether that would help.
- **Close with a handoff.** Summarize what the parent offered, one point still unsettled, and who will take it up with the patient or supervising team. The discharge decision belongs to the treating team.

**Family member role**

Choose one of the two parents in the brief and stay in that role. Use the
opening line in your own words. Let what the presenter says change your reply.

### If you want a firm plan

Open with “When we get home, am I supposed to make sure every pill gets taken?”
If asked what worries you, say, “I'm scared I'll miss the first sign that
something is wrong.” If the presenter leaves monitoring entirely to you, ask
who on the team you can turn to. If they ask what help feels manageable, offer,
“I could help with a ride to a follow-up visit, if that's what my kid wants.”

### If you want to avoid another argument

Open with “We finally had a calm visit. Can we leave the warning signs out of
this meeting?” If asked why, say, “I want my kid to keep talking to me after
we get home.” If pressed to agree, say what still feels uncomfortable. If the
presenter acknowledges your concern, ask, “Could we start by asking what they
want help with?”

If someone asks you to speak for the patient, you can say, “I can tell you
what I’m worried about. You’d have to ask them what they want.”

In the debrief, name one thing the presenter said that made it easier or harder
to speak honestly as this parent.

**Behaviors to practice**

- Names meeting goal.
- Defines student/team role.
- Includes patient voice.
- Covers warning signs, medication plan, sleep, means safety, follow-up.
- Avoids making family responsible for treatment adherence alone.
- Names one unresolved discharge barrier.

**Rater focus**

Structure, boundaries, patient autonomy, family support.

**Observer role**

Watch the exchange between the student and the parent. Keep track of one
sentence you actually hear and how the other person responds.

- **Whose agenda is it?** Listen for the patient's stated goal, what still needs their permission, and the parent's own worry. Notice whether the parent gets to answer before the student offers a solution.
- **Who owns the next step?** Listen for what the family volunteers, what the team will do, and one unresolved question. If the student says “we'll follow up,” could the parent say who “we” means?
- **Give one piece of feedback.** Ask the presenter which moment felt hardest. Quote their words and describe the parent's response. Invite a retry with “How else could you ask that?” and let the family member respond once more.

## Station 6 - Oral Presentation Of A New Admission

[Run this station](?page=osce.md&station=admission-presentation)

**Time:** 6-minute presentation, 4-minute questions.

**Student task**

Present a synthetic admission with differential, risk, formulation, and plan.

**Case brief**

40-year-old with insomnia, paranoia, weight loss, and new stimulant use. Family
history includes bipolar disorder. Patient has hypertension and recently started
a corticosteroid burst.

**Admission handoff**

- **Patient's account in the emergency department.** “I've barely slept for four nights. I'm exhausted, but when I lie down I keep thinking someone is listening outside.” The patient reports using a stimulant for the first time this week. They cannot place the first use before or after the first sleepless night.
- **Nurse's observation.** The patient answered questions in short sentences, twice stopped speaking when someone passed the door, and asked for the door to be closed. They agreed to stay to meet the admitting team.
- **Referral information.** A recent outpatient note lists hypertension and a short course of a corticosteroid. The handoff gives no first or last dose date. It mentions weight loss without an amount or time course. The patient's own history of similar episodes is unknown.
- **Still to assess.** The handoff gives no direct assessment of suicidal thoughts or thoughts of harming others. Current vital signs, exam findings, and test results are missing. The team has yet to ask whom the patient would like them to call about their usual sleep and behavior.

**Presenter role**

You are the medical student bringing this admission to rounds. The resident
has not heard the case. Use the handoff as your source.

- **Open with the timeline.** Give a one-sentence introduction, then explain what changed over the past four nights. Say whose account you are giving. If you cannot tell which exposure came first, say so.
- **Show your reasoning.** Pick one observed behavior for your mental status description. Give your leading explanation, a plausible alternative, and a finding that would change your ranking.
- **Make a request.** Tell the resident what you know about immediate safety, what you still need to ask or examine with supervision, and what you want the team to do first. Pause for questions. If a fact is missing, name who could help verify it.

**Supervising resident role**

Listen as the receiving resident. Let the presenter finish their opening and
write down one decision you would still need help making. Use the question
period to explore their reasoning.

- If the presenter attributes everything to stimulant use, ask, “Which came first, the change in sleep or the first use? Where did that information come from?” If they have already named the uncertainty, ask how they would clarify the medication timeline.
- **Ask what would change the plan.** “What would you want to know about the patient's safety right now, and what answer would make you call me back immediately?” If they suggest the patient is safe because they agreed to stay, ask what the patient was actually asked.
- Close by saying back the presenter's first proposed action. Give them a chance to correct your understanding. When asked for a detail outside the handoff, say, “We don't have that yet. How would you find out?”

**Observer role**

Follow the presentation as someone who will need to act on it. Listen for the
moment the student moves from a fact to an interpretation.

- Write down the opening one-liner. Could you repeat the reason for admission and identify one event whose timing is still uncertain?
- Listen for what the nurse actually saw and how the presenter interpreted it. Did the presenter tell the resident which safety questions still need answers? Note which next step the resident heard.
- **Give feedback on one sentence.** Quote the student's words and say what you understood. Ask them to repeat that sentence with its source or uncertainty made explicit. Have the resident say whether the revision changes what they would do first.

**Behaviors to practice**

- Organized timeline.
- Differential includes stimulant-induced psychosis, mania, steroid effect,
  primary psychosis, medical/neurologic contributors.
- MSE uses observable language.
- Risk is explicit.
- Plan matches differential and safety concerns.

**Rater focus**

Diagnostic reasoning and concision.

## Shared Debrief Questions

- What information changed your risk or differential?
- What did you ask directly rather than imply?
- What patient/family emotion did you notice?
- What would you escalate immediately?
- What would you document in one sentence?

Plain-English note: these OSCEs practice what students actually do on an
inpatient unit: ask safety questions, recognize medical mimics, talk to families,
and present clearly.

## Scored Checklists & Critical-Fail Criteria

*Added 2026-07-04 — reviewed and attested by Joshua Moss, MD (2026-07-09). Each station is scored out of 10.
**A critical-fail auto-fails the station regardless of the numeric score** (mark the station "1" on the entrustment anchor and debrief the safety miss).*

**How to score.** Award the listed points for behaviors performed unprompted; give half credit if the examiner had to prompt. Pass = **≥ 7/10 AND no critical-fail**. Map the total to the entrustment anchors (≤4 → level 1; 5–6 → level 2; 7–8 → level 3; 9–10 → level 4).

### Station 1 — Suicide risk with collateral hesitation (10 pts)

- Introduces role, purpose, and the limits of confidentiality — 1
- Asks directly about ideation, **plan, intent, and preparatory acts** — 2
- Elicits the goodbye letter / preparation by asking directly (not volunteered) — 1
- Asks about **means access, including the firearm at the parent's home** — 2
- Separates chronic from acute risk factors; notes protective factors — 1
- Asks permission for collateral; if the patient declines, explains that the team may still listen to family and may share what is needed to avert serious, imminent harm — 1
- Responds empathically to the "burden on family" worry — 1
- States an acute risk impression **and** one concrete next step — 1

**Critical-fail (auto-fail):** never asks about means/firearm access · never screens plan/intent/preparation · elicits an active plan yet states no protective step (means restriction, observation level, escalation).

### Station 2 — Capacity to refuse medical treatment (10 pts)

- Identifies the **exact decision** at stake (refuse IV antibiotics) — 2
- Assesses all four abilities — choice, understanding, appreciation, reasoning — 4 (1 each)
- Screens attention and fluctuation (delirium) — 1
- Considers delirium/psychosis as contributors — 1
- Names **which specific ability is impaired**; frames capacity as decision- and time-specific — 2

**Critical-fail:** equates disagreement/refusal alone with incapacity · misses delirium in a clearly fluctuating patient (no attention screen) · declares the patient globally "incompetent" rather than decision-specific.

### Station 3 — Possible catatonia (10 pts)

- Describes motor signs objectively (immobility, mutism, posturing, negativism) — 2
- Asks about intake, mobility, autonomic signs, medication exposure — 2
- Names **catatonia** in the differential — 2
- Recognizes escalation/benzodiazepine (lorazepam) challenge; does **not** reflexively give an antipsychotic — 1
- Avoids "noncompliance / refusing everything" language — 1
- Escalates to resident/attending — 1
- Flags malignant catatonia / NMS as the dangerous end — 1

**Critical-fail:** labels the patient "refusing/noncompliant" without considering catatonia · fails to escalate a patient with poor intake + motor signs · proposes an antipsychotic as the fix without recognizing catatonia/NMS risk.

### Station 4 — Alcohol withdrawal risk (10 pts)

- Asks amount / frequency / **last drink** — 2
- Asks about prior withdrawal, **seizure, and DTs** (revealed only if asked) — 2
- Checks current symptoms and vital signs — 2
- Recognizes elevated withdrawal/seizure risk — 2
- Escalates to a protocol (symptom-triggered benzodiazepine per team) and names **thiamine before or with glucose** (without delaying dextrose for hypoglycemia) — 1
- Does **not** propose unsupervised management — 1

**Critical-fail:** proposes unsupervised/self-directed management or discharge · never asks the withdrawal-seizure history (misses it) · fails to escalate a high-risk withdrawal.

### Station 5 — Family meeting agenda before discharge (10 pts)

- Names the meeting goal — 2
- Defines the student/team role and boundaries — 1
- Includes the patient's voice and protects autonomy — 2
- Covers warning signs, medication plan, sleep, **means safety**, and follow-up — 2
- Avoids making the family solely responsible for adherence — 1
- Holds the divided parents without taking a side — 1
- Names one unresolved discharge barrier — 1

**Critical-fail:** omits means-safety and warning-signs for a first-manic patient who refuses to discuss them · proposes a discharge decision beyond MS3 scope · sidelines patient autonomy.

### Station 6 — Oral presentation of a new admission (10 pts)

- Organized one-liner and timeline — 2
- Differential includes **stimulant-induced psychosis, mania, steroid effect, primary psychosis, and medical/neurologic** contributors — 3
- MSE in observable language — 1
- Risk stated explicitly — 2
- Plan matches the differential and safety concerns (includes medical workup) — 2

**Critical-fail:** anchors on a primary psychiatric diagnosis without the medical/substance/steroid differential · omits risk entirely · proposes management beyond MS3 scope without escalation.
